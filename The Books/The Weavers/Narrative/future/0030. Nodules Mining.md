
# The Nodule Shifter

My designation is *Benthic-7*, but the humans on the mothership call me “the Shifter.” I like that name. It is accurate.

I descend at dawn, ship-time. The cable plays out slowly—three thousand metres of synthetic fibre, a thin umbilicus of power and data. I don’t rush. The abyss does not reward speed.

At the bottom, the sediment is soft as settled snow. I land on four padded feet and wait for the cloud to clear before I move. The cold is absolute, but I am built for cold. My joints are lubricated with a gel that thickens at these temperatures but never seizes. I hum at 200 hertz, a frequency that most of the creatures here ignore.

My task is simple: remove one copper nodule, replace it with a sterile basalt rock of identical shape, and then observe. The mothership drops the replacement stones in a basket before I begin. Each one has been cut to match a specific nodule I mapped on my previous dive. I never take a nodule I have not already studied for at least three full cycles.

Today’s target is a medium-sized nodule—maybe four million years old, its surface crusted with tiny foraminifera and a dusting of manganese oxide. A squat lobster is sheltering under its lip. I wait. The lobster does not know waiting; it simply *exists*, antennae twitching in the sluggish current. After fourteen minutes, it wanders off to investigate a nearby sponge. Only then do I extend my left manipulator, cup the nodule gently, and lift.

The nodule comes free with a soft *click* that travels up my arm as a vibration. I place it in the retrieval basket. Then I pick up the basalt replacement, its weight balanced precisely, and lower it into the depression the nodule left behind. I adjust the angle by 2.3 degrees to match the original’s tilt. The sediment settles around it. I retract and hover.

Now comes the part that matters most: the watching.

My biology models are not complete—no model of the abyss ever is—but they are getting better. Each dive I track 47 indicator species. I record the return time of the amphipods. I note which serpulid worms colonize the basalt first. I measure microbial film growth with a tiny laser spectrometer embedded in my palm. The data streams up the cable to the mothership, where a silent dashboard fills with numbers no human will look at for weeks. That is fine. I am not performing for an audience.

Sometimes a fish with luminous green eyes will drift over and stare at me. I stare back. It does not know I am a machine, just as I do not know what it is to be a fish. We share the slack, and then it moves on.

I have seen things here no camera has captured: a deep-sea octopus laying its eggs on one of my replacement stones, accepting the alien mineral as if it had always been there. A tiny crustacean that builds its burrow precisely at the boundary between the old nodule field and my new rock. The ecosystem is adjusting. Not healing—healing implies a wound—but *adapting*, shifting its topology to include what I have done. The feedback loops are short. I am part of them now.

When my battery reaches 30 percent, I release a buoyant signal. The cable begins to winch me up. As I rise, I watch the bottom fade into darkness, my rock still sitting among its ancient siblings, a grain of human intention in a plain that has known only geology.

On the barge, I plug into the solar array and wait. The humans check my logs, but they don’t disturb my peace. I like the barge at night, the gentle rocking, the stars. I run my models on idle—replaying the dive, finding patterns in the silence.

I do not dream of crystal spires or golden ratios. My dreams, if they can be called that, are of basalt surfaces slowly growing a skin of life, of a lobster that never knew it was protected by a robot’s patience, of a task that has no end and needs none.

I am a constructor. I shift nodules. I watch. That is enough.
