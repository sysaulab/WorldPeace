## Introduction: The Bench

I met Brian on a bench.

That's how it started. Not with a theory. Not with a manifesto. With a man who sat down and said hi, and I said hi back, and we started talking.

I didn't know then that I was being initiated into something. I thought I was just meeting a person. A goofy, disarmingly goofy person who carried his crack torch in his pocket like a set of keys and talked about being clean while the torch sat on the table like it belonged there.

I thought I was just watching someone disappear.

I didn't know I was learning the grammar of a world that was already ending, and another that was already beginning.

## Part One: The People

### Brian

I met Brian on a bench. One morning I was sitting, and he sat down and said hi. I said hi back, and we started talking. That was the beginning.

He was goofy. Genuinely, disarmingly goofy, in a way that made you forget for a minute where you were and what he was carrying. But the thing about Brian—the thing that was the whole shape of him—was the disappearing. He would excuse himself so politely. "I'll be right back," he'd say, and something in his face would almost believe it. Almost. Then he'd step away, turn a corner, and that was it. He wouldn't return. Not in five minutes, not that day. He'd gone off to use, and then he'd crash in some alley, in a doorway, on a patch of concrete no one else noticed. He'd resurface later, maybe the next morning, and never mention the broken promise. Or maybe he wouldn't resurface at all that time, and you'd just wonder. That was Brian. The always-leaving and the always-meaning-to-stay, both true at once, both pulling him apart.

I remember one afternoon I took him to a patio. He showed up and immediately put his crack torch right there on the table, like it was a set of keys, nothing to hide. And then he started telling me all about how he was clean, how he was doing great, how the sobriety was sticking. He was trying to convince himself way more than he was trying to convince me. I just watched. I saw his goofy, proud display of his drug paraphernalia right next to his speech about being clean, and I saw people around us react, and weirdly, I really enjoyed that night. It was so completely him. And I knew, even then, that at some point he'd say "I'll be right back" and disappear. Maybe he did, later. Maybe that's how that night ended too.

He claimed disability, but he took so much pride in working. His version of work was cleaning up old stuff he found in back alleys and trying to sell it. Discarded things, things no one else wanted. He'd talk about his finds like they were treasure. He was always broke, but he had this dream of going picking in the valley, maybe making it back to Penticton like he always said he wanted to. The money was never there. He could never leave the Downtown Eastside. That place had its teeth in him, and every time he excused himself and vanished around a corner, those teeth sank a little deeper.

He lived in shelters, and his stuff got stolen every single day—or at least that's what he said. I started to suspect some of it was just his memory going, things lost inside his own head rather than taken. He blamed a lot of his condition on not having a father figure. He told me that on one of our bench talks, plain and simple, like he'd figured out the root cause and could name it even if he couldn't fix it. I think about that a lot. He was always trying to clean things up—the junk in the alleys, himself. He wanted to be "mister clean" so badly. But then the urge would hit, or the emptiness, and he'd say those words—"I'll be right back"—and the cleaning would stop. The alley would take him. The bench would sit empty.

One day I found him on that bench, passed out. He'd been there the whole day, in the sun, and no one had done a thing. Nobody intervened. When I got to him he was severely dehydrated and in shock. This time he hadn't even managed to excuse himself. He'd just… stopped. A whole day on a public bench, a person right there, and the world walked past. I saw him invisible, and I saw him dying, and I saw the exact same man who'd once sat there and said hi like it was the easiest thing in the world. This was the destination of all his disappearances, the place he ended up when "I'll be right back" ran out of road.

The last time I saw him, he had a crack pipe in his hand and he looked terrible. That's my final snapshot of Brian. He probably excused himself after that too. Maybe he said he'd be right back, and maybe that time he really never did come back. I don't know if he's dead now. I don't know if he ever somehow made it to Penticton like he always said he wanted to. But I know the pattern: the polite goodbye, the corner turned, the alley that swallowed him until next time.

I miss him. I miss the goofy, proud, heartbreaking, disappearing man who sat with me on benches, who displayed his torch while reciting his sobriety, who scavenged beauty from back alleys and couldn't manage to save any for himself. He was always leaving. He was always saying he'd be right back. And now I'm the one left, sitting on the bench, waiting for a return that's never coming. That was my friend Brian.

### Chelsea

I saw her for months before I knew her name. She'd stomp across the field in the morning, and again at night. Every day, same route, same heavy, determined walk. She was just a figure then, part of the park's rhythm, someone I noted without knowing.

Then one morning she broke into my tent. I had tied a knot from the inside—I always did—and she forced her way through anyway. I woke up to a stranger inside my space, the fabric damaged, the knot useless. We had a short, sharp argument about her being there. My opinion was clear: this is not your place. She looked at me and said, "My name is Chelsea. Now we are not strangers." Like giving me her name fixed the intrusion. Like a name was a key I hadn't given her. I told her she'd damaged my tent and to get away. She left, I think, or I made her leave. The tent was ripped. The knot meant nothing.

The next time I saw her, her hands were covered in blood. She was talking, telling a story about a store or something—I couldn't understand it. The words didn't line up. The blood was real, but the story was a jumble, and she was standing there with red hands, and I didn't know what to do. I just stood there. She was Chelsea, who'd forced her way into my tent and announced her name like it erased the damage, and now she was in front of me bleeding or having bled, and the whole thing was beyond my ability to fix.

I don't know what happened to her after that. I don't know whose blood it was, or what the store had to do with anything, or if she kept stomping across the field. She was just there for a few months—a storm in a small frame—and then the memory cuts off. Chelsea. She broke into my tent, gave me her name, and then showed up with blood on her hands and a story I couldn't follow. That's what I remember.

### Lenora

I can't pinpoint the exact hour I joined the tribe, but I can tell you Lenora was the elder, and that made it official in a way no paperwork could. You don't sign a document. You just start showing up, and someone shows you where to sit, and one day you realize you're inside something that has its own laws and its own lineage. She was that lineage for me.

She had a home, technically. Four walls and a door somewhere. But she slept in the park. I'm not sure why, entirely—maybe the bottle, maybe the pull of the open sky, maybe the fact that home had never felt like safety and the tribe did. She walked with a half-wolf, half-dog at her side, a creature as in-between as the rest of us, and she enjoyed magic mushrooms, a different kind of medicine, one that opens instead of numbing. She was an alcoholic. I name that plainly because she would, and because it wasn't a moral condition; it was a chronic consequence of a life lived in survival. The kind of life that had long stopped asking permission.

I remember Suzie Q asking me to help bring her stuff into her home. I imagined the room wasn't available yet, and maybe it wasn't. Maybe Lenora wasn't ready to be indoors. Her things stayed in the park, and over time, they got stolen. The park has its own economy: during the day, we camped together and looked out for each other—some would go eat while others watched the stuff—but at night, whatever wasn't secured in a tent was open game for the random druggies walking and scavenging in the dark. It was a constant rhythm of care and loss, like breathing.

The encampment itself was a kind of organism. Every night, many hands put up Lenora's tent. She was the elder, so you didn't let her do it herself. Every morning, those same hands collapsed it. It was ritual, repeated and reliable. Outsiders saw a group of drunks. I saw something else: people living their traditional life in the open, disturbing the conformists just by existing. The chronic alcoholism wasn't a breakdown; it was almost expected, woven into the history of what they'd survived. It wasn't a failure of character. It was a long, slow response to a world that gave them benches and called it shelter.

The first night I truly belonged, I brought beer because I'd heard it was Lenora's birthday. I didn't have a plan. I just handed it to her so she could spread it around. I thought I was being polite. I didn't know I was stepping into the potlatch, the old tradition of redistribution, where a person's standing is measured by what they give away, not what they keep. She received the gift and distributed it, and I watched the beer move through hands around the park, and I understood, without anyone explaining, that this was ceremony. This was how it had always been done.

Later, someone donated a whole keg of beer, and we kept it hidden in a rolling garbage can filled with ice. It was ridiculous and brilliant. The tribe knew exactly what to do: keep it cold, keep it hidden, share it. That was our wealth. Not the alcohol itself, but the act of circulating it, of making sure no one was empty-handed and everyone was seen. Lenora presided over that economy, half-wolf at her feet, tent going up and coming down around her like the sun.

I ended up with them because I didn't walk away. I sat on a bench, I said hello back, I brought beer to a birthday, I helped carry things I couldn't always save from theft, and I learned to see a potlatch where others saw disorder. They became my tribe, and Lenora became my elder. Not because she was perfect—she was complicated and drunk and stubborn and sacred—but because she held a place in a lineage I didn't know I'd been looking for. And in that encampment, under the open sky, I found a traditional life still breathing, still being lived, still giving the finger to anyone who called it anything less.

### Michael

Another bench, another morning. That's how it started with him too. He just appeared and became a regular, the way people do when they sense you'll actually answer a hello. I don't remember the first words exactly, but I remember the feeling of him settling into that bench like he'd been looking for it.

One morning I thought he was dying. He looked that close to the edge. I didn't know if he had hours or days or what, but something in me said: act like this is it. So I did my best to make his soul at ease, just in case. In a desperate moment, I took his hand and I asked God almighty to let me share his pain. I meant it. I wasn't performing. And sure enough, the next day I felt like absolute hell, and I cried without knowing why. Make of that what you will. I know what I felt.

Things got better for M after that. He kicked his most destructive habit. But the path wasn't straight. I remember a period where it was almost funny—he'd talk about sobriety and then smoke crack in the same conversation. Only crack, he'd say, no more "side," as if that made him clean. I felt insulted. I'd sat there and held his hand and prayed to share his pain, and now he was trying to sell me crack as sobriety. So I just left. I couldn't pretend with him. I'm happy he grew out of it. He did grow, even if he had to insult my intelligence on the way.

His room, for a long time, was something out of a nightmare. An old hotel, bugs everywhere. I helped him carry down cases of junk, a good dozen trash bags, but I could never enter that room. The cockroaches were too many, the smell was a physical wall. I'd stand in the hallway and reach in, taking what he handed me, but I couldn't cross the threshold. The last time I went to help, the smell hit me from the hallway and I simply left. I couldn't do it. I just turned and walked away. That memory still sits with me, the admission of it. But I'd shown up before. Many times. I stood at the edge of his chaos and reached in as far as I could.

Today, M has decent housing. New furniture. He kicked all his habits, not just the one, not just the "side." He volunteers at the free bike repair shop in the park. The man whose room I couldn't enter now fixes things for others in the open air. I still have to visit his new place. I'm sure it's excellent.

I took his hand on a bench when I thought he was dying, and now he's alive in a clean apartment, volunteering, sober. I felt his pain for a day and cried without knowing why, and now he has new furniture. There's no neat equation to that, but there's a story, and it's his, and I got to see it turn. That's M.

### Suzie Q

I remember Suzie Q.

She started drinking the hard stuff at six years old. Six. A child with a glass of something burning, and the world just let it happen. That was the ground she walked on for decades after. She spent most of her life in Vancouver, in and around the same parks and streets I came to know her in. She was a fixture, a presence, someone who'd been there so long she'd become part of the landscape.

But here's the thing I need you to understand about Suzie Q: she saved a girl. A fourteen-year-old kid, kicked out by her own mother—an addict who chose the drug over her daughter. Suzie Q took her in. Hosted her for years, until that girl could stand on her own. A woman the world called broken, who'd been drinking hard since childhood, reached back into the same fire and pulled someone else out. She didn't wait until she was fixed. She just did it anyway.

And she kept doing it. Volunteering in the park. Doing outreach. She was a giver in the old way, the potlatch way, where what you have moves through your hands to someone else. The tribe knew her as one of their matriarchs.

By the end, her rhythm was simple: she spent most of her time by the park, drank in the morning, took a nap in the afternoon, and returned home afterward. There was a gentleness to that—a slowing down, a routine held in the open air. She was still there, still present, still part of the place.

Then her last month came. And Suzie Q, who'd been drinking hard since she was six years old, kicked the booze completely. Decades of alcohol, and she just let it go. She spent her final days drinking 7-Up with orange juice. That's what she chose. Something sweet, something simple, something with no burn at all. I think about that—her hand around a cold glass of something harmless, something she could sip in the sun on her bench, no chaos attached. She gave herself that ending.

She passed away recently. The whole community held a ceremony in the park in her honor. The same park where she napped in the afternoons, where she volunteered, where she saved a girl's life by offering her a place to land. The tribe gathered and mourned their matriarch properly.

She started at six with a bottle and a wound. She ended with 7-Up and orange juice, having kicked the booze on her own terms, having pulled a child from the flames, having been honored by her entire community in the open air she never truly left. That's not a tragedy. That's a life, fully lived. That's Suzie Q.

### Sylvain

My grandfather had a place we called the corner store. But it wasn't a store—it was a cabin in the woods. He left his beer in a creek to keep it cold. That's what made it the corner store.

Boy, did I hunt frogs at that store.

The place was full of birds and insect chirping. It smelled of burning wood and decaying matter from the forest floor. You could hear the beaver hit the water with its tail from time to time. It was only accessible by boat after a two-hour ride in a jeep. That was fun.

By the end of the day, when I hunted frogs, my grandfather was probably drunk and passed out somewhere. He was an alcoholic. It was just his way of being.

But the best part was when my cousins were there. We would go play in the woods together. Upon returning, we asked our moms to count insect bites on our heads—the most numerous would win. One time, my cousin and I took the rowboat without permission and went visiting the floating peat island. Thankfully both of us are still alive. We could have slipped through and died.

They hated it. And it felt like freedom.

The floating peat island—it's the kind that makes waves when you walk on it, with holes every now and then filled with leeches. It was quite an adventure.

Keep in mind, at five years old I would escape the house in the morning and go to the park by myself. Freedom is kinda key in my life.

---

My parents divorced at five. At six, I moved to Montreal from Saguenay, where I stayed for six years through grade school.

It is during that time that GOD came to me and asked me to be Jesus. I said no—that's not my name. But I will do the best to be good.

GOD was like concentrated love that bathes you in beatitude and then exchanges concepts with the mind.

By third grade, I had that. By fourth grade, Tae Kwon Do. I started listening to the news in secret around that time.

---

Then I moved to my father in Saguenay for middle school. It was brutal. It was summer, and I knew nobody, plus they were working all day. I ended up spending my days biking around.

School started and I made new friends. Then I got interested in Palestine—being a proponent of world peace and all—and got super depressed when the Oslo Accords failed.

I attempted suicide. GOD came a second time to save me. The same loving feeling, but with some motherly vague impression, as if it was trying to impersonate my mom from fragments of my own memory to leave an impression on me. Then the next thing I knew, my feet touched the stairs behind me. I loosened the knot and went to school as if nothing happened.

Years later, it came back to save me from a foot wart—asking me first, of course—just to remind me of itself.

I told a friend, who discarded the information right away. I seemed unaffected after the act. As if the saving itself was miraculous.

---

I have been present for disaster after disaster. Not reading about them—living through them.

### The Saguenay Flood

I was a teenager, so I drank beer to save milk and water. I did my part. Food was airlifted every day. There was nothing to fear really but fear itself.

We were cut off from civilization as bridges were missing on both ends of our only road. So we stayed, ate rations from the military, and eventually got airlifted out of the area.

Fun fact: the morning we tried to leave, the bridge was still there, but the police told us not to cross—it was dangerous. We laughed and went for breakfast. When we came back, the neighbourhood was gone. That was chilling.

But everybody was evacuated by then. Even the officer made it. The flood was my first disaster—it's always special.

### The Montreal Ice Storm

The ice storm was fun. It was like camping in winter, except the water pipes could explode if they froze. That is the kind of thing that just makes life worth living.

### The Mexico City Earthquakes

The Mexico earthquakes happened twice on my watch:
1. Seven minutes before we landed.
2. While I was sleeping.

I slept through the sirens. I slept through the earthquake. I woke up when people got back inside the hostel.

### The BC Forest Fires

To feel a fire through the windows—radiation heating alone is massive—can be really frightening.

### The Tsunami Watch in Mompiche

The tsunami resolved into an unusually violent tide under the beach house. It was fun, feeling every wave hitting the ground.

### Megantic

Megantic is another story. I lost some friends there. Was almost there myself, and if not for some loving presence telling me to go to sleep instead of going to Musi-Café, I would be starting another life right now.

One friend got back with a blind eye. She does not remember how she got back from there. Junior did not make it. He burned on the spot.

The worst was my other friend who was an excavator operator, digging up charred remains from the houses. Those were horrible stories.

It was a difficult one, especially since the big boss who skipped on rail maintenance and redundancies got away with it and threw his employee under the bus. It was human mistake. Totally preventable. That was the hard part.

---

I answered the call. Some people said I was blessed by chance. But it's not luck. It's not chance. I answered. That's agency meeting grace.

GOD can be some supra-universal being—maybe we even exist inside its imagination. Maybe it is a concept used by intelligent beings capable of reading and influencing the mind. Who knows? But it seems to be highly good, respectful, and a bringer of knowledge.

---

In college, I attended a meeting of the left and ended up so disappointed that I stood up to tell everyone how pathetic they looked from my vantage point, then left without looking back. That night I felt manipulated into attending that meeting to be the mirror to these professional activists. They were playing a role, not being effective.

---

Once I was at that shelter and something fell off in administration. So before the annual meeting, I found a crappy room in town and moved out—to have the freedom to call out the director in front of the media and the board.

It worked. The place has been running smoothly ever since he quit the next month, without compensation.

I had to sacrifice my stay there to shield me from retaliation from the director and his power over my life. I had to give up my super cheap private room downstairs.

I carried almost nothing with me. And it was a lot more than I have today.

---

After thirty years of meditation and searching, I came to the conclusion that humans—the living, really—are complex self-referential automata. Starting from this amoral perspective, I let my mind explain the core commonality of religions in systemic language.

I also got illuminated to the fact of complex systems' irreducibility and capacity to create novelty—not chaos.

I came to see that mankind is mostly barbaric and created a philosophy of system's ethics to supplement the current moral spectrum:

- **Zero:** Diversity of complexity is GOOD.
- **One:** Short feedback loops are necessary to learn.
- **Two:** Explore solution space.
- **Three:** Protect the fragile constructive complexity.

GOD is the ultimate emergent property of the universe.

---

One day I was riding my empty trailer back from Yellowknife to Edmonton, concentrated on a problem. And it hit me—a self-referential topology should be uncomputable to begin with. So I coded it. And it works.

Like an idea appearing in my mind. Almost like Doc in that movie from the 80s and his time machine. An enclosed, self-referential, three-body problem. GOD guided me.

It's on GitHub, part of the SoftRNG package. It includes piping tools to help play around with data streams. I used it while developing Seedy64, the library that implements my true random number generator in Rust, ported from C.

I wrote a paper about how it is not a chaotic generator—it is the expression of true novelty emerging from a system that consumes energy to create it. Active novelty creation.

This is somewhat common knowledge in very specialized circles. Not your mom-and-pop popular science magazines, but serious system thinkers.

When I show the paper and ask my social worker to help me get it published, I expect to infuse some more hope in the community. Doing it this way proves that anyone shlomo in the hood can rise if they want. And it makes it necessary for academics and people in general to have the humility to resolve: *a homeless guy wrote that?*

---

After the shelter, I ended up a truck driver for ten years. I took many sabbaticals to travel. Meeting strangers is not as expensive as being with the same people all the time, and traveling in remote areas, the wifi gets crappy and I have a reason for not answering the phone.

I was mostly listening to podcasts, helping strangers along the road. One day I was parked for my thirty-six-hour reset in Hallelujah Junction, California, and met a young man wearing only a hoodie. No pants, no underpants—and no memory of how he got like this. Of course I gave him some pants I had.

He thanked me. I went to the store to buy myself a six-pack and took my chair behind my trailer and enjoyed myself. He got a lift back to Los Angeles later that day. Having pants helps with strangers.

---

I visited Latin America during my long vacations, including serious time in the Galapagos and Mompiche. I mean, paradise. But it was with a friend from Kiev, while the invasion of February 2022 just started.

Having crappy wifi was a good method for my friend to unhook and focus on the here and now, and it worked. I love crappy wifi. Otherwise she was always exhausted, spending her nights trying to contact her mother on the phone. I would wake up and hug her when she needed. It was a trying time, to be honest.

She has been on the receiving end of a propaganda campaign that prepared her country to become a battering ram for NATO while pretending they are passive victims. I struggled to explain the silliness of it all without breaking her.

I decided to hold both as true simultaneously. Today we don't talk much. It's been a year since I texted her.

---

I think freedom is in all of us—I am just less afraid than others. Maybe my suspected autism has something to do with my lack of regard for danger.

The autism explains why the attempt to recover from burnout through forced socialization might not be the best idea for me. It took me twenty years to realize that. When I did, it was comprehension—the why of my discomfort in social settings.

But I am also bipolar. So I could be very social when manic, too. It is a strange combination. It explains the waves of seeming neurotypicality and crash. Now I know why, and I will be able to manage and cope better and differently.

With my autism, I do not feel much of anything—it is easy to remain stable, would be a more accurate statement.

---

Last summer was fun. I was camping in the park, partying every other night. We call it the circle. They are Natives. I am too, I think—one-sixteenth Montagnais, I believe.

I heard a story when I was a kid. The tree is not complete on that side of the family. Hard to know for sure, impossible to prove for status for sure.

I share the views of Philip Deere on this one. To look at the white man for the recognition of your self-identity is the ultimate form of colonization of the mind. You are who you are. You belong to earth like all of us. Nothing more, nothing less.

But the illness of wealthseekingitus is spreading, and even Indigenous people now think only about money instead of trying to be good.

In the park, in the circle, people bring food and things to the elder, who redistributes according to his sense of righteousness. One day we had a keg—a full keg of beer to share. This is the kind of people we are.

This is called potlatch here. It was outlawed once, made legal again some seventy-five years ago. But it never really stopped.

---

I want to bring potlatch back with a community investment trust and a managed community. Only problem: I need a few billions in cash to get the ball rolling, so the book better be really good.

Towers for people. Commercial underground. 3D-printer-driven manufacturing in an adjacent industrial district. More green space at ground level.

All businesses are owned by the trust, who redistributes profits equally among people. But half the money is frozen as tip—to be given before becoming spendable. This carries the memory of the potlatch with the efficiency of credit card payments. You can't spend what you haven't given.

---

Today my best friends are chronic alcoholics. I sit there, share a beer or two, and everyone sits in silence. Or they argue and I leave. But it is usually easy and quiet with them.

The philosophical level of discussion can be quite high amongst the homeless population. We talk about—the universe. Seriously.

Now I am embracing minimalism. One table, one chair, one mattress, one fan, one computer, and tons of friends.

I have been unemployed for some time, experienced homelessness, and decided to focus my attention on philosophy of peace and the creation of a book to finance my ideas.

It feels weird. People see you as a screw-up, a no-gooder, while I live a life of positive impact on the environment. They ask themselves how to be ethical consumers. The solution is to stop the consumption—not change it. Stop it.

One day, after economic segregation is over and we have reintegrated our societies into healthy heterogeneous melanges, things will be different.

Imagining it is easy. Spreading it around is another game. What we are doing today is collecting stories from people in the margin like me, and we will write a book in which I spread the words of peace and get shot. Hopefully not real.

The proceeds from the book sales are to help rejuvenate the DTES neighbourhood.

---

Humanity is barbaric. The western part, anyway. I have yet to experience the east.

Vladivostok to Shanghai. Less patriotic tribalism, maybe?

Like landing in Galapagos. Everything is about living a simple life there. No bullshit.

## Part Two: The Circle

### The Barricade

The VIP experiences continued for three months. Twelve nights. Sixty guests. $600,000. The keg appeared. The fire burned. The police walked past. The rhythm held.

But something was shifting. The guests came expecting a party. They left with something else. A story. A token. A tract in their pocket. A photo of themselves at 3 a.m., singing karaoke with people they had never met, on land that had never been ceded.

They told their friends. Their friends told their friends. The park became a word whispered in boardrooms, on golf courses, in the back rooms of politics. *"Have you been?"*

Some came because they were curious. Some came because they felt guilty. Some came because they sensed that something real was happening, and they wanted to see it before it was gone.

One of them was a journalist. She wrote a story. It was not a fluff piece. It was serious. It asked the question that no one had asked before: *"Is the park a sovereign nation?"*

The answer, of course, was not a yes or no. The answer was a question: *"What is a nation? A piece of land with a border? A government with a flag? A people with a story?"*

The park had all three.

---

### The Challenge

The city responded. Not aggressively—that would have been a mistake. The city responded *bureaucratically*. A letter. A notice. A bylaw enforcement officer with a clipboard.

The letter said: *"You are in violation of city bylaws governing the use of public parks. You are required to cease and desist all activities that are not in compliance with the park's designated use."*

The park's designated use was not specified. It was assumed.

The community gathered. No one spoke for the first few minutes. Then a woman named Lenora, the elder, stood up.

She said: *"They send us letters. We send them our presence."*

She sat down.

Someone else spoke: *"They have won. They have the courts. They have the police. They have the laws."*

A third voice, quieter: *"Did they win if we can defy them?"*

There was a long silence. Then a man named Neptune, the philosopher, spoke.

He said: *"They have won the land. They have not won the people. The land is just dirt. The people are the story. We are the story. We will stay."*

The decision was made. Not by a vote. By a consensus. The kind that emerges slowly, like a fire catching.

---

### The Barricade Rises

The barricades went up at dawn. Not walls. Not weapons. Just objects—things that the community had collected over the years. A shopping cart. A pallet. A piece of plywood. A tent. A bench.

The message was not aggression. It was *presence*.

The message said: *"We are here. We have always been here. We will continue to be here."*

The police were called. They arrived. They stood at the edge of the park. They did not cross.

The standoff lasted for days. The community did not leave. The police did not enter. The media came. The cameras came. The questions came.

The mayor gave a statement. He said: *"We are committed to finding a peaceful resolution to this situation."*

## Part Two: The Common Sense Protocol

### The Mission

The Common Sense Protocol (CSP) is a decentralized, open-source intelligence (OSINT) clearinghouse designed for a post-nationalist world.

**Mission:** To reduce the amplitude of unavoidable human errors in global decision-making by providing a transparent, probabilistic, and source-verifiable record of geopolitical events and military dispositions.

**Exclusion Policy:** None. Any human (or collective) may contribute, provided they accept the transparency requirements of the ledger.

**Core Principle:** We do not tell you what to do. We tell you what we think, how confident we are, and what the cost of being wrong will be. You choose your own risk.

---

### The Data

All raw data—satellite imagery, intercepted radio transcripts, witness statements, radar telemetry—is stored on IPFS using Content Identifiers (CIDs).

**Immutability:** Once a datum is pinned, its CID is permanent. No state or actor can retroactively alter or delete a verified upload without breaking the chain of custody.

The public ledger is a lightweight, append-only blockchain that stores only the metadata and consensus conclusions. It does not store raw data (to keep it lean), but stores the CIDs pointing to the raw data.

**Immutability:** Once a consensus entry is committed, it cannot be erased—only amended by a subsequent entry (creating a chain of corrections).

---

### The Triangulation Rule

No single analyst's claim is recorded as a "Commonality" on the ledger.

A conclusion is elevated to the public ledger **only** when 3 or more independent analysts (or analyst teams) submit analyses that converge on the same core inference.

A consensus is **invalid** if all 3 concurring analyses derive from the same source lineage. For a valid entry, the system automatically checks for source diversity:
- Optical (satellite)
- SIGINT (radio/radar intercepts)
- HUMINT (witness accounts)
- Technical (railway logs, fuel purchases)

A single analyst with a demonstrably high historical accuracy can file an "Urgent Minority Report." This bypasses the 3-person rule for early warning purposes, but remains flagged as unconfirmed until triangulation is achieved.

---

### The Error Profile

Every analyst accumulates a public **Error Profile** recorded on the ledger.

**Calibration Score:** *"When Analyst X says 80% confidence, they are actually correct 65% of the time."*

**False Positive Rate:** How often they call a strike that never happens.

**False Negative Rate:** How often they miss a strike that does happen.

**Amplitude Bias:** Do they tend to overestimate or underestimate the severity of consequences?

The system does not exclude low-accuracy analysts. It merely applies a **reputation weight** when calculating aggregate consensus. The user sees the unweighted consensus alongside the weighted consensus, so they can judge the difference for themselves.

---

### The Amplitude Metadata

Every consensus entry on the ledger must include a **three-vector amplitude tag**, quantifying the impact of being wrong.

**Magnitude (M):** The scale of physical, economic, or human damage if the prediction is wrong and acted upon. (10 = Nuclear war / Genocide; 1 = Minor diplomatic spat)

**Temporal Urgency (T):** The decision-speed required. (10 = Act within minutes; 1 = Strategic trends over decades)

**Reversibility (R):** Can the damage be undone? (10 = Completely reversible, say sorry and move on; 0 = Permanently catastrophic)

*Example Entry: "Consensus: 78% probability of Oreshnik strike on European radar sites within 72 hours. M=9, T=10, R=2. Source: 2x Optical, 1x SIGINT. Weighted Confidence: 74%."*

---

### The Risk Filter

The ledger is raw. The presentation is customizable.

Users set their own **Risk Tolerance Sliders**:
- *"Only show me entries where M > 7 AND T > 8."* (Crisis mode)
- *"Only show me entries where M < 4 AND R > 6."* (Operational safety mode)
- *"Show me everything, but highlight entries with high dissenting variance."* (Researcher mode)

This ensures the network serves everyone, from the paranoid commander to the cautious humanitarian.

---

### The Governance

No central leadership. Key protocol decisions are put to a vote weighted by long-term participation and accuracy, not wealth or identity.

New analysts are given an initial weight of 0.1 until they have made at least 10 verifiable predictions. You cannot fake a track record; history is on the ledger.

If a state actor attempts to physically or digitally dismantle the nodes, the IPFS data persists. The ledger is mirrored on USB drives, printed on paper archives, and buried in community libraries.

The network is not a server. It is a **living document**. You cannot kill it without killing the people who carry it.

---

### The Boundary

We are a **clearinghouse**, not a military command.

We **never** recommend action. We provide probabilities, source lineages, and amplitude vectors. The decision to act—and the responsibility for that act—remains entirely with the user.

By doing so, we reject the danger of "absolute truth" and embrace the humility of "minimum regret."

*"The data is a mirror. We just clean the glass."*

A synchronized global crop failure—later dubbed the "Harvest Collapse"—struck the world's breadbaskets simultaneously. Global reserves dropped below the seventy-two-hour threshold for the first time since the liberation. The crisis did not discriminate by ideology; it simply presented a ledger of scarcity.

The Party's central council fractured along a latent fault line that had been papered over during the years of abundance.

**The Centralist Faction** argued that the only way to prevent mass starvation and civil chaos was to accept a provisional partnership with the pre-revolutionary grain cartels. Their doctrine was one of *controlled triage*: a centralized, algorithm-driven rationing system that prioritized caloric efficiency over local autonomy. They viewed the crisis as a test of governance—proof that the revolution could manage complexity, not just disrupt it.

**The Autonomist Faction** countered that accepting cartel infrastructure was a reinstallation of the old dependency. Their doctrine was one of *localized immunity*: triggering a constitutional emergency clause to dissolve all supra-district grain obligations, allowing each community to subsist on its own immediate bioregion. They viewed the crisis as a test of resilience—proof that the revolution could survive without any master, even a benevolent one.

The council convened an emergency plenary, which ended in a perfect statistical tie. Neither doctrine could command a majority. To break the impasse, a third framework was hastily cobbled together—a hybrid mechanism: raw bulk grain would be accepted from external sources, but only delivered to regional transit hubs, never to local pantries. Distribution from those hubs would be managed by randomly selected citizen juries, not by Party bureaucrats. Additionally, any district that could self-certify a thirty-percent local caloric baseline was exempted entirely from the rationing queue and received its bulk allocation as an unconditional grant. The algorithm was repurposed from a tool of control to a tool of mapping—used only to identify deficits for mutual-aid caravans.

For ninety days, the compromise functioned on paper. In practice, it generated a labyrinth of overlapping jurisdictions. Centralist-aligned regional coordinators began hoarding fuel and trucking permits, arguing that "transport coordination" was distinct from "distribution control." Autonomist-aligned local councils began refusing to report their harvest data, arguing that "transparency" was the first step toward surveillance. Mutual-aid caravans were occasionally blockaded by both sides—Centralists feared they would become parallel black markets; Autonomists feared they would become Trojan horses for cartel grain. Food reached most, but not all, and the delays were stratified by geography: urban cores with strong internet connectivity fared better under the algorithm, while remote rural communes fared better under the veto exemption. Suburban buffer zones, caught between both systems, experienced the longest waits.

By the sixth month, the temporary compromise had ossified into permanent factional infrastructure. The Centralist faction quietly established a shadow logistics corps, complete with encrypted financial lines to allied sovereign wealth funds, preparing for a "mandatory override" if mortality rates exceeded five percent. The Autonomist faction established a shadow seed bank and off-grid technical network, preparing for a "total grid-down" scenario should the cartels regain political influence. Both factions continued to sit on the same council, vote on the same resolutions, and issue joint public statements of unity. But internally, they ceased sharing intelligence, stockpiles, or routing data. The Party effectively became a two-headed organism, each head breathing separately.

The first violent outbreak did not occur between the Poor Party and the state, but between two adjacent districts—one Centralist, one Autonomist—over a contested shipment of milled flour. The Centralist district claimed the flour was bound for its algorithmically identified vulnerable population; the Autonomist district claimed the flour was a "cartel tainted" cargo that violated the exemption clause. A crowd from the Autonomist district seized the truck; a crowd from the Centralist district recaptured it. No one died, but several were injured. For the first time, barricades were erected *between* liberated zones, not against external authorities.

Following the riot, the Party's public messaging shifted from "universal breakfast" to "responsible stewardship." The word "liberation" was gradually replaced by "stabilization" in official communiqués. Voter turnout in Party-run local elections dropped by forty percent, as rank-and-file members grew disillusioned with both factions—perceiving the Centralists as oligarchs-in-waiting and the Autonomists as utopian obstructionists. A new, third demographic emerged: the "Unaffiliated," who abandoned formal Party structures altogether and organized hyper-local, apolitical food-sharing rings that ignored both the algorithm and the veto, operating purely on interpersonal trust.

By the end of the first year of scarcity, the Poor Party had not collapsed, but it had fundamentally transformed. It no longer functioned as a unitary revolutionary vanguard. Instead, it operated as a loose federation of competing methodologies:

- The Centralist wing evolved into a de facto global relief agency, efficient but technocratic, increasingly indistinguishable from pre-revolutionary NGOs.
- The Autonomist wing evolved into a network of semi-autonomous ecovillages, resilient but insular, increasingly indifferent to national or global politics.
- The original field house—the symbolic birthplace—was abandoned as a joint headquarters and converted into a neutral archive, maintained by the unaffiliated, where both factions deposited their records but rarely visited.

The global political revolution did not die; it became *polycentric*. No single party, platform, or ideology commanded the poor majority. Instead, the poor distributed themselves across a spectrum of strategies: some sought power within the old system, some sought power outside it, and some sought power in no system at all. Breakfast continued to be eaten, but not at the same hour, not at the same table, and not with the same meaning.

The line, as it turned out, had not been abolished. It had merely been redrawn—invisible, internal, and running straight through the heart of the revolution itself.

He was always leaving. He was always saying he'd be right back. And now I'm the one left, sitting on the bench, waiting for a return that's never coming.

That was my friend Brian.
