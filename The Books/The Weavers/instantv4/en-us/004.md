# Chapter Four: The Bridge

---

Mariana woke to the sound of her sons arguing about breakfast.

She lay in bed for a moment, her eyes still closed, listening to the familiar rhythm of their voices. Marcus wanted cereal. Micah wanted toast. Neither would accept the other's choice, and both were convinced that their mother would take their side if she were awake.

She smiled. It was a small thing, a small joy, but she held onto it. There were so few small joys these days.

She opened her eyes and looked at the ceiling of her apartment. It was a small place, a one-bedroom in a building that had seen better decades. The paint was peeling. The radiator clanked. The windows were drafty. But it was hers. She had worked hard for it, and she was proud of it.

She got out of bed and walked to the kitchen, where her sons were still arguing. They were eight years old, twins, identical in appearance and completely different in temperament. Marcus was the planner, the cautious one, the one who thought before he acted. Micah was the explorer, the risk-taker, the one who jumped first and asked questions later. They balanced each other out, most of the time. This morning, they were balanced on the edge of a full-scale war.

"Mom!" Marcus said as soon as he saw her. "Micah said we can't have cereal because he wants toast, but I had toast yesterday and it's my turn to choose!"

"Mom!" Micah said simultaneously. "Marcus is being a baby. He always wants cereal. We should have toast. Toast is better."

Mariana held up her hands. "Enough. Both of you." She walked to the counter and opened the cupboard. "We have cereal. We have bread. We can have both."

"But we can't have both," Marcus said. "That's not fair. One of us has to get what they want."

"Who said life is fair?" Mariana pulled out the cereal box and a loaf of bread. "We can have cereal and toast. Everyone gets what they want. That's called compromise."

The twins looked at each other, considering this. Micah shrugged. "I guess that works."

Marcus nodded. "Fine. But I get the first cereal bowl."

Mariana made them breakfast—cereal for Marcus, toast for Micah, and a cup of tea for herself. She sat at the small table and watched them eat, her mind already wandering to the day ahead. There was so much to do. There was always so much to do.

She was a social worker. She worked for the city, in an office downtown, in a building that had been built in the seventies and had never been renovated. Her job was to help people navigate the system—to find housing, to access benefits, to get the services they needed. It was a good job. It was a meaningful job. But it was also a frustrating job, because the system was broken, and no amount of paperwork could fix it.

She had been doing it for fifteen years. She was good at it. She knew the rules, the loopholes, the ways to work around the bureaucracy. She had helped hundreds of people, maybe thousands. She had made a difference in their lives.

And yet.

And yet, every day, she went home to her small apartment and thought about the park. Every day, she walked past the park on her way to work and saw the people living there—the same people she was supposed to be helping, the same people the system had failed. Every day, she asked herself the same question: was she making a difference, or was she just helping the system feel better about itself?

She didn't have an answer. She had been asking the question for fifteen years, and she still didn't have an answer.

---

The boys were at school. Mariana was at her desk. The morning had been a blur of paperwork, phone calls, and meetings. She had approved three housing applications, denied two, and referred one case to a specialist. It was a good day, by the standards of her job. The numbers were good. The metrics were good.

She was about to leave for lunch when her phone rang. She looked at the caller ID and felt her stomach tighten. It was her mother.

"Hello?"

"Mariana." Lenora's voice was calm, steady, as it always was. "Can you come to the park tonight? There's something we need to discuss."

"What kind of something?"

"The kind that needs to be discussed in person." There was a pause. "It's about the city. They're planning something."

Mariana closed her eyes. She had known this was coming. She had been seeing the signs for months—the city trucks parked at the edge of the park, the surveyors taking measurements, the officials she passed in the hallway at work who looked away when she met their eyes. She had known, but she had not wanted to know.

"I'll be there," she said. "After work."

"Good. Bring the boys. They should see this."

"They're at school."

"Then bring them after school. They should see this."

Mariana opened her mouth to argue, but her mother had already hung up.

She put down the phone and stared at her desk. The stack of files, the computer screen, the framed photo of her sons. It was a good life. A stable life. A life she had worked hard to build. And now, her mother was asking her to bring her children to the park, to witness the city's attempt to destroy the only real community she had ever known.

She didn't want to go. She wanted to stay in her office, in her safe, comfortable world, where the problems were abstract and the solutions were bureaucratic. She wanted to pretend that the park was not her responsibility, that the people there were not her people.

But they were her people. They had always been her people. She had grown up in that park. She had learned to walk, to talk, to survive in that park. She had left it, but it had never left her.

She looked at the photo of her sons. They didn't know about the park. They knew their grandmother lived there, but they didn't understand what it meant. They had never spent a night in a tent. They had never eaten from a communal fire. They had never known the fear and the freedom of having nothing and everything at the same time.

She wanted to protect them from that. She wanted to give them a different life, a better life, a life that didn't include the constant struggle for survival. She had worked so hard to give them that.

But now her mother was asking her to bring them into the struggle. To show them what was at stake. To make them witnesses.

She didn't want to do it. But she knew she had to.

---

The boys were excited. They didn't often visit the park. They knew it was a special place, a place where their grandmother lived, but they didn't understand what it meant. To them, it was just a place with tents and a fire and people who smiled at them when they walked through.

"Are we going to see Grandma?" Marcus asked as they walked down the street.

"Yes."

"Are we going to eat dinner there?"

"Probably."

"Are we going to stay overnight?"

"No," Mariana said. "We're going to visit. And then we're going home."

"Can I see the garden?" Micah asked. "There's a girl who has a garden. She grows tomatoes. I saw it last time."

Mariana smiled. Micah was the curious one, the explorer. He had noticed the garden when they had visited last time, and he had been talking about it ever since.

"Maybe," she said. "If she's there."

The park appeared at the end of the street. It was an ordinary park, at first glance—a patch of green in the middle of the city, with trees and benches and a public restroom. But it was more than that. It was a home. A community. A place where people had built something from nothing.

Mariana felt a familiar knot tighten in her chest as she approached. She had been coming to this park her whole life. She knew every corner, every face, every story. It was a part of her, even though she had tried to leave it behind.

She saw Lenora sitting on her bench, waiting for them. The boys ran ahead, calling out to their grandmother, and Mariana followed at a slower pace.

Lenora stood up and embraced the boys, one in each arm. They were her grandchildren, her only grandchildren, and she loved them more than she could say.

"Grandma!" Marcus said. "We came to see you!"

"I know, child. I'm glad you did." Lenora looked at Mariana over the boys' heads. "Thank you for coming."

"Did you think I wouldn't?"

"No. I knew you would." Lenora released the boys and looked around the park. "This is a special place, Mariana. You know that. You grew up here."

"I know."

"And the city wants to take it away from us."

Mariana nodded. "I know that too."

"What are you going to do about it?"

Mariana opened her mouth to say something—to deflect, to make excuses, to explain that she couldn't do anything—but her mother was looking at her with those dark eyes, those patient, knowing eyes. The same eyes that had seen her through every crisis of her life. The same eyes that had taught her how to survive.

"I don't know," she said. "What can I do? I'm just one person."

"You're not just one person, Mariana. You're the bridge." Lenora gestured around the park. "You know this world. And you know the other world. You understand both. That makes you valuable."

"What am I supposed to do with that?"

"Help us. Use what you know. Warn us. Tell us what's coming." Lenora looked at her daughter. "You have access. You have information. You can see what we can't see. Use it. For us."

Mariana felt the weight of the request. It was not a small thing. It was not a simple thing. Her mother was asking her to use her position, her access, her knowledge of the system to help the community. To betray the city that employed her. To risk everything she had built.

"Mother, I can't—"

"You can," Lenora said. "You don't want to. I understand that. But you can."

The boys were playing by the garden now. A young woman—the gardener, Jess—was showing them the tomato plant. They were laughing. It was a simple scene, a quiet scene, a scene that could have taken place in any park in any city.

But this park was different. This park was a community. This park was a home. This park was the only home some of these people had ever known.

And the city was going to take it away from them.

Mariana watched her sons laughing, watched the gardener showing them the tomato plant, watched her mother's face as she looked at her grandchildren.

"I'll do what I can," she said.

Lenora nodded. "That's all I ask."

---

The ride home was quiet. The boys were tired, their energy spent. Marcus was half-asleep in the back seat, his head against the window. Micah was looking out the other window, watching the city pass by.

"Mom?" Micah asked. "What's going to happen to the park?"

Mariana hesitated. She didn't know how to answer that. She didn't know what was going to happen. She didn't know if the park would survive. She didn't know if any of them would survive.

"I don't know," she said. "But I'm going to do my best to help them."

"Like you help people at work?"

"Different kind of help," she said. "But yes. Like that."

Micah nodded, satisfied. He went back to looking out the window.

Mariana looked at the road ahead. She didn't know what was coming. She didn't know if she could make a difference. But she knew she had to try.

She was the bridge, as her mother had said. The bridge between the park and the city, between the community and the system. It was a responsibility she had never asked for and never wanted. But it was hers. She would carry it.

## The Archivist's Note on Chapter Four

Mariana is a character I understand deeply. She is the one who left—the one who went to university, got a job, built a life in the outside world. She is the one who thought she could change things from the inside. She is the one who discovered that the system changes you more than you change it.

But she never stopped belonging to the park. She never stopped being part of it. She is the bridge, as Lenora says. She understands both worlds, and she can translate between them.

When the crisis comes, she will be essential. She will not fight on the barricade—that is not her role. She will fight in the offices, in the meetings, in the negotiations. She will be the voice of the park in the rooms where decisions are made.

It is a thankless role. She will be seen as a traitor by some and a sellout by others. But she will do it anyway, because she is the bridge, and the bridge must hold.
