# Chapter Thirteen: The Informant

---

Rhea had been carrying the secret for fifteen years.

It sat in her chest like a stone, heavy and cold, pressing against her ribs. She had tried to forget it. She had tried to bury it. She had tried to pretend it didn't exist. But it was always there, always present, always waiting.

She had been a journalist once. A good one, by the standards of the trade. She had written stories that exposed corruption, revealed injustice, told the truth. She had believed that words could change the world. She had believed that telling the truth was always the right thing to do.

She had been wrong.

The story had been about a community, a lot like the park. A group of people who had been pushed to the margins, forced to survive on the edges of society. She had spent months with them, gaining their trust, learning their stories. She had written about their struggles, their triumphs, their resilience. She had thought she was helping them.

Instead, she had destroyed them.

The story had been published in a major newspaper. It had been read by thousands of people. It had been shared, discussed, debated. And it had brought attention to the community. The wrong kind of attention. The kind that led to investigations, evictions, displacement.

Three people had died because of her story. Three people had lost their homes, their communities, their lives. She had not meant for that to happen. She had thought she was helping. But the cost had been real. The guilt had been unbearable.

She had fled to the park to escape. She had come here to hide, to disappear, to become someone else. She had stopped writing. She had stopped telling stories. She had become the gossip, the watcher, the archive. She had held the secrets of the community, but she had never written them down. She had never betrayed anyone again.

But the guilt had never faded. It had only grown heavier, more burdensome, more impossible to carry.

Now, as the crisis deepened and the barricade went up, she knew she had to tell someone. She had to confess. She had to share the weight.

She had to become part of the community in the way she had never been able to before.

---

She found Lenora on her bench, as she always did at this hour. The elder was sitting in the morning light, her eyes closed, her face calm. She looked like a statue, ancient and immovable, the anchor of the community.

Rhea approached slowly, her footsteps heavy on the pavement. She sat down beside Lenora, her cigarette unlit in her hand.

"I need to tell you something," she said.

Lenora opened her eyes. "I'm listening."

Rhea took a deep breath. She had rehearsed this moment a hundred times, but the words still wouldn't come. She had to force them out, one by one.

"Before I came here, I was a journalist," she said. "I wrote a story about a community. A community like this one. I thought I was helping. I thought I was telling the truth."

She paused. "But I was wrong. The story led to investigations, evictions, displacement. Three people died because of what I wrote."

Lenora was silent. Her face was unreadable, calm and steady as always.

"I came here to escape," Rhea continued. "I came here to hide. I've been carrying this guilt for fifteen years. I've never told anyone. I've never been able to."

Lenora nodded slowly. "That's a heavy burden to carry alone."

"I know. I thought I could handle it. I thought I could just disappear, start over, become someone else. But it never went away. It only got heavier."

Lenora reached out and took Rhea's hand. It was a simple gesture, a gentle gesture, but it meant everything. It meant she was not alone. It meant she was forgiven.

"Thank you for telling me," Lenora said. "That took courage."

"I don't feel courageous. I feel like a coward. I've been hiding for fifteen years."

"Showing up is not hiding. Telling the truth is not hiding. You've been part of this community for fifteen years, Rhea. You've protected us, watched over us, held our secrets. You've been one of us. You just didn't know it."

Rhea felt tears welling in her eyes. She had not cried in years. She had not let herself. But now, the tears came, hot and painful, running down her cheeks.

"I'm sorry," she whispered. "I'm so sorry."

"Don't apologize," Lenora said. "Just stay. That's all I ask."

Rhea nodded. She couldn't speak.

They sat in silence, holding hands, watching the sun rise over the park.

---

Later that day, Rhea went to the barricade. She had been avoiding it, afraid of what it represented, afraid of being part of something so visible, so public. But now, she felt a need to be there. A need to contribute. A need to show that she was part of the community.

She found Owen working on the barricade, his hands busy, his focus intense. He looked up when she approached.

"Rhea. I didn't expect to see you here."

"I need to do something," she said. "I need to help."

Owen nodded. "Okay. What can you do?"

"I know people. I have contacts in the city. I can find out what they're planning."

Owen looked at her, his eyes questioning. "Why would you do that?"

"Because I owe this community. Because I've been hiding for too long. Because it's time to stop hiding and start helping."

Owen nodded slowly. "Okay. If you can find out what they're planning, we need to know. We need to be ready."

Rhea nodded. "I'll find out."

She walked away, her footsteps steady on the pavement. She had a purpose now. A mission. She was going to use her old skills, her old contacts, her old knowledge to protect the community. She was going to be the informant. The one who saw what was coming and warned them in time.

It was not a redemption. She knew that. She had done too much harm to ever be redeemed. But it was a start. It was a way of giving back. It was a way of being part of the community she had been hiding from for so long.

She walked to the edge of the park, pulled out her phone, and made a call.

"I need to talk to someone," she said. "Someone who knows what the city is planning."

The voice on the other end was cautious. "Rhea? Is that you? It's been years."

"I know. I need information. It's urgent."

There was a pause. "Okay. What do you need?"

Rhea told them. She told them about the eviction notice, the barricade, the community. She told them everything she knew.

And when she was done, she had what she needed. The city was planning something. Not just an eviction, but a raid. A coordinated effort to remove the community by force. They were coming in two days.

Rhea hung up the phone and walked back to the park. She found Lenora, Sylvain, and Owen gathered at the barricade. They looked at her, waiting.

"They're coming," she said. "In two days. They're going to raid the park."

The group was silent. Then Sylvain spoke.

"Then we have two days to prepare."

---

The community mobilized. It was not chaos. It was not panic. It was the quiet, steady work of people who had been through worse and survived.

They reinforced the barricade. They added new objects, strengthened the weak points, made it more formidable. They gathered supplies—food, water, blankets—to sustain them through the crisis. They organized watches, shifts, rotations. They were prepared.

Rhea watched it all, a strange sense of peace settling over her. She had been part of the community for fifteen years, but she had never really been part of it. She had always been on the outside, holding secrets, keeping distance. Now, she was inside. Now, she was part of the work. Now, she was part of the fight.

She found Jess near the garden, watering her plants. The girl was young, fierce, determined. She had found a home here, the way Rhea had found a home here. The way everyone had found a home here.

"You're doing good work," Rhea said.

Jess looked up, startled. "Thank you."

"You're part of the community now. You know that, right?"

Jess nodded slowly. "I think I'm starting to understand that."

"It's not easy," Rhea said. "Being part of a community. It means you have to show up. You have to be present. You have to be willing to be seen."

"I'm trying."

"I know. That's all anyone can ask." Rhea paused. "I've been hiding for fifteen years. I thought I could escape my past by disappearing. But you can't disappear. You can only show up. And hope that people accept you."

Jess looked at her. "Did they accept you?"

"Yes. Eventually. But it took me a long time to accept myself."

Jess nodded slowly. "I think I understand."

They stood in silence, watching the garden. The plants were growing, green and vibrant, reaching toward the sun. It was a small miracle, the way life persisted in the most unlikely places.

"Thank you," Jess said finally. "For telling me that."

"Thank you for listening." Rhea smiled. "That's the most important thing, you know. Listening. Being present. Showing up."

Jess nodded. "I'm going to keep showing up."

"Good. That's all any of us can do."

Rhea walked away, her footsteps steady on the pavement. She had done what she needed to do. She had shared her secret. She had become part of the community. She had shown up.

It was not a redemption. It was a beginning. A way of being present. A way of being part of something bigger than herself.

She had been hiding for fifteen years. She was done hiding.

Now, she was going to fight.

## The Archivist's Note on Chapter Thirteen

Rhea's confession is the turning point of her character arc. She has been carrying the weight of her past for fifteen years, hiding from the consequences of her actions, afraid to be seen. But the crisis has forced her to confront her past and choose a different path.

She chooses to be present. She chooses to be part of the community. She chooses to fight.

This is the stool in action. Short feedback loops: she sees the problem and acts. Curiosity: she explores the possibility of redemption. Protection of the fragile: she uses her knowledge and skills to protect the community.

Rhea is not a hero. She is a woman who has done terrible things and is trying to do something good. She is a woman who has been hiding and is finally choosing to show up. She is a woman who has been carrying a heavy burden and is finally learning to share it.

That is what the park does. That is what the community does. It takes broken people and helps them heal. It takes people who have been hiding and helps them show up. It takes people who have been carrying heavy burdens and helps them share the weight.

The stool is not a utopia. It is a practice. A way of being present. A way of showing up. A way of protecting the fragile.

Rhea has learned that. She is ready to fight.
