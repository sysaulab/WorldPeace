# Chapter Seven: The Dealer's Prayer

---

Dime woke to the sound of his sister crying.

It was a familiar sound, one he had heard a thousand times before. His sister, Maya, was nine years old, small for her age, with big eyes and a quiet voice that rarely spoke above a whisper. She was in foster care now, placed with a family on the other side of the city, a family Dime had never met. He visited her once a month, on the first Saturday, and every time he saw her, she was crying.

He sat up on the park bench where he had slept. It was not a comfortable place to sleep—the wood was hard, the metal cold, the night air biting—but it was better than the street. It was better than the shelters, where the violence was constant and the fear never ended. The park was dangerous too, but it was a different kind of danger. The people here were broken, but they were also trying to survive. That mattered.

He stood up and stretched. His body ached from the night on the bench, but he had learned to ignore the pain. He had learned to ignore a lot of things. Hunger. Cold. Fear. They were just sensations. They didn't mean anything.

He walked to the edge of the park, to the spot where he kept his stash. It was not much—a few dime bags, a small amount of product, enough to get through the day. He was not a big dealer, not like the ones who controlled the streets. He was just a kid trying to survive, trying to keep his sister safe, trying to find a way out.

He had been doing this for three years now. He had started when he was seventeen, after he had been kicked out of his last group home. He had needed money, needed to eat, needed to survive. The trade had been the only option. It was not a good option. It was not an option he was proud of. But it was the only one he had.

He thought about Maya, about her small face and her quiet voice. He thought about the way she cried when he visited, the way she clung to him as if he were the only anchor in a stormy sea. He was her only family. He was all she had.

He had to get out of the trade. He knew that. He wanted to get out. But he didn't know how. Every time he tried to stop, the money ran out, the hunger came back, the fear returned. The trade was a trap, and he was caught in it.

---

The park was waking up around him. People were emerging from their tents, lighting fires, starting their day. Dime watched them with a mixture of envy and longing. They had something he didn't have. They had each other. They had a community. They had a place where they belonged.

He had never belonged anywhere. He had been in foster homes, group homes, shelters, and streets. He had always been on the outside, looking in, never quite part of the picture.

He watched a young woman walk past—the gardener, Jess—and felt a pang of something he couldn't name. She was new to the park, but she was already part of it. She had found a place here. She had found people who cared about her.

He wanted that. He wanted it more than anything. But he didn't know how to get it. He didn't know how to be the kind of person people cared about.

He was still standing at the edge of the park, lost in thought, when a group of men approached. They were not from the park. They were from the outside, from the streets, from the world he was trying to escape.

"Hey, Dime," one of them said. "We need product."

Dime tensed. He knew these men. They were not customers. They were predators. They took what they wanted and left nothing behind.

"I don't have anything," he said. "I'm out."

"Bullshit. We know you have product. Give it to us."

Dime shook his head. "I can't. It's not mine. It's for someone else."

The men moved closer. Dime backed up, his heart pounding. He was not a fighter. He had never been a fighter. He had survived by being small, invisible, unnoticeable. But there was nowhere to hide now. There was nowhere to run.

"I'm going to ask you one more time," the man said. "Give us the product."

Dime was about to comply—he had no choice, he knew, no choice at all—when something unexpected happened. A figure appeared at his side. It was the old man with the prosthetic leg, the one who walked the perimeter every morning.

"Is there a problem here?" Harvey asked. His voice was quiet, steady, calm. It was the voice of someone who had faced worse and survived.

The men looked at Harvey, assessed him, and made a decision. They backed away.

"No problem," the leader said. "We were just leaving."

They walked away, disappearing into the morning. Dime stood frozen, his heart still pounding, his breath coming in short gasps.

"You okay?" Harvey asked.

Dime nodded. He couldn't speak. His throat was too tight.

"You should stay away from those men," Harvey said. "They're not good people."

"I know." Dime's voice was barely a whisper. "I didn't have a choice."

Harvey looked at him for a long moment. His eyes were not judgmental. They were just... present. Watching. Seeing.

"There's always a choice," he said. "Sometimes it's just not the one you want."

He walked away, his footsteps steady on the pavement. Dime watched him go.

He had been saved. Saved by an old man with a prosthetic leg and a quiet voice. Saved by someone who didn't even know his name.

He looked at the park. The tents. The fires. The people moving through their morning routines. This was a community. This was a place where people helped each other, protected each other, survived together.

He wanted to be part of it. He wanted to belong. He wanted to be the kind of person who helped instead of hurt.

But he didn't know how.

---

That evening, Dime found himself near the center of the park. The community fire was burning, and people were gathering around it, sharing food and stories. He stood at the edge, watching, not sure if he was welcome.

A woman approached him. She was older, grey-haired, with a cigarette in her hand and a knowing look in her eyes. Rhea. The one who knew everything.

"You're the dealer," she said.

Dime tensed. "I'm not—"

"I know what you do. Everyone knows." She took a drag on her cigarette. "But I also know you're not a bad person. You're just a kid trying to survive."

Dime didn't know how to respond. He had never been seen like this—as a kid trying to survive. He had always been seen as a threat, a problem, a target.

"Why are you telling me this?" he asked.

"Because I think you're looking for a way out," Rhea said. "And I think you might find it here."

She gestured to the fire. "This is not a perfect place. It's broken, just like everyone here. But it's a place where people help each other. Where people protect each other. Where people give each other second chances."

She looked at him, her eyes sharp and knowing. "You could have a place here. If you wanted it."

Dime was silent for a long moment. He looked at the fire, the people gathered around it, the warmth and light and community. He thought about his sister, about the life he wanted for her. He thought about the life he wanted for himself.

"Thank you," he said. "I'll think about it."

Rhea nodded. "That's all I ask."

She walked away, her footsteps heavy on the pavement. Dime watched her go, then looked back at the fire.

He didn't know if he could change. He didn't know if he could be the person he wanted to be. But for the first time in a long time, he thought maybe it was possible.

He thought maybe there was a way out.

## The Archivist's Note on Chapter Seven

Dime is one of the most vulnerable characters in the park. He is young, alone, and trapped in a system that has failed him repeatedly. He has made choices he is not proud of, choices that have hurt people, choices that have kept him alive. He is not a bad person. He is a person who has done bad things to survive.

The park offers him something he has never had: a community. A place where he can be seen, accepted, and given a second chance. It is not easy. It is not simple. But it is possible.

When the barricade goes up, Dime will be there. Not as a dealer, but as a protector. He will stand with the community, defending the fragile, protecting the vulnerable. He will find his way out of the trade and into something better.

It will not be easy. It will not be simple. But it will be possible.

That is the gift of the stool. That is the gift of the park.
