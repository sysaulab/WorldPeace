
# Part II, The Stool

## Chapter 6  
### The Three Axioms  

After the barricade, after the keg and the police and the quiet, stubborn refusal of the tribe to disappear, I began to ask a different question. Not “how do we survive this?” but “why *did* we survive this?” What was the invisible grammar that held the park together when every official structure had written it off?

I spent thirty years meditating on complex systems. I’d coded a true random number generator from a self‑referential topology — a machine that didn’t just shuffle bits but actively created novelty, not chaos. I’d seen God twice: once at six, who asked me to be Jesus and I said no, and once at sixteen, who saved me from a suicide attempt and left me standing on the stairs with a loosened knot and a silent, loving presence. So when I sat on that bench and watched the community operate, I wasn’t just a homeless man; I was a system‑thinker, a philosopher, someone who’d been trained by silence and pattern to see the deep structure beneath the surface noise.

What I saw in the park was not anarchy. It was a set of principles so basic, so biological, that they made the encampment more resilient than the city that surrounded it. I came to call them the three axioms of the stool.

**The first axiom: short feedback loops.** In the park, decisions were made by those affected. If someone was hungry, they were fed — not by a distant agency with a form and a waiting period, but by the person two tents over who had extra. If someone broke the fragile trust of the camp, the consequence came not from a judge but from the immediate withdrawal of the community’s protection. Harm was addressed quickly, visibly, and with the full weight of the only currency that mattered: belonging. The feedback loop was minutes, not months. In the city outside, harm could echo for years before a court date, by which time the wound had festered into a permanent scar. Here, the loop was short enough that the system could learn. That learning was everything.

**The second axiom: curiosity about unexplored solution spaces.** The community did not freeze into a single answer. It experimented. Someone tried a different way to barter. Someone else invented a new signal for danger. When a conflict arose, no one said, “this is how we’ve always done it.” They asked, “what would bring life here?” The park was a laboratory of adaptation, not a museum of tradition. This curiosity kept the system from calcifying into dogma. It was the opposite of ideology.

**The third axiom: protection of the fragile.** The quiet, the vulnerable, the easily ignored — they were structurally protected. The elder who couldn’t put up her own tent had a dozen hands doing it for her before she could ask. The child who wandered in was watched by every eye in the vicinity. The mentally ill, the broken, the suicidal — they were not shunned; they were the early warning system. If a fragile member started to deteriorate, it meant the whole camp was under strain. The fragile were the canaries in the coal mine, and the community knew that crushing them would blind itself.

These three axioms were not a moral code. They were *adaptations*. They were the way any complex system — a forest, a body, a society — stays alive in a universe that is fundamentally random. I’ll get to randomness later. For now, understand that the stool was not my invention. It was my observation. The park had discovered it organically, through the pressure of survival and the memory of older ways. The potlatch, the circle, the bench — they were expressions of the stool, not its source.

I wrote the axioms down on a scrap of paper and taped it to the inside of my tent. *Zero: Diversity of complexity is good. One: Short feedback loops are necessary to learn. Two: Explore solution space. Three: Protect the fragile constructive complexity.* That was the whole philosophy. Everything else — the protocols, the technate, the galaxy — would grow from those four lines.

But a stool needs more than legs. It needs something to sit on. The park provided that in the form of a universal surplus floor: every person’s basic needs were met, not as charity but as a right of membership. The floor wasn’t money. It was the collective guarantee that no one would starve, freeze, or be left alone in their worst hour. That guarantee was the ground upon which people could build the rest of their lives. Without it, the short feedback loops become a scramble for scraps. With it, curiosity has the oxygen to breathe.

The park, then, was a micro‑civilization operating on the stool’s grammar. The question became: could this grammar scale? Could the same principles that kept forty people alive on a patch of grass in Vancouver be applied to a city, a nation, a planet? I believed they could. But I also knew that scaling wouldn’t happen by force. The stool is not an ideology to be imposed. It’s a pattern to be *inhabited*. That’s why I started writing — not to convince, but to blueprint.

## Chapter 7  
### The Cube  

The three axioms are powerful, but they’re also vague. You can nod at “protect the fragile” and still crush a minority underfoot while thinking you’re doing it for their own good. You can claim “short feedback loops” and build a surveillance state that punishes dissent instantly. You need a tool that makes the axioms visible, measurable, discussable — something that cuts through the self‑justifying fog of human cultures and shows where a system is actually headed.

I found that tool in the form of a cube. Not a physical cube, but a conceptual one. Three axes, each mapping to one of the axioms. The first axis is Feedback & Learning: do actions have visible consequences quickly, or do errors accumulate in the dark until the whole system collapses? The second is Curiosity & Exploration: does the society encourage genuine questions and novel experiments, or does it punish heresy and enforce orthodoxy? The third is Respect & Protection of the Fragile: are delicate things deliberately shielded, or are they sacrificed for efficiency, power, or the convenience of the majority?

Each axis runs from negative to positive. A system that scores low on all three — long feedback, dead curiosity, no protection — is drifting toward what I call the destructive attractor. A system that scores high on all three is oriented toward the constructive attractor. Between those extremes lie six fuzzy middles, where every real society actually lives. The cube doesn’t demand perfection. It demands *direction*. It asks: when you wobble, which way do you correct?

The cube is not a moral commandment. It’s a language of systemic health. Any culture can translate it into its own traditions. A Buddhist might see it as an expression of interdependence, compassion, and beginner’s mind. An engineer might see it as robust control theory. A potlatch elder already knows it in her bones. The cube just gives it a vocabulary that can travel.

I used the cube to map the park. Short feedback? Check. The community knew within hours if someone had violated trust. Curiosity? Present — the experiments with barter, with the keg economy, with new rituals. Protection of the fragile? Visible every night when the elder’s tent went up by many hands. The park was a (+++) corner, wobbling but always correcting.

Then I used the cube on the world outside. The feedback loops were decades long — climate damage done today would not be felt by the decision‑makers at all. Curiosity was being strangled by media bubbles, algorithmic conformity, and the professionalization of activism into a performance. The fragile — refugees, children, ecosystems — were being traded for quarterly profits and electoral cycles. The world was sliding toward (---), and that slide had a thermodynamic signature, which I’ll come to next.

But first, I want to be clear about what the cube is not. It is not a substitute for love, courage, or the hard labour of listening. It cannot tell you how to resolve a conflict between two fragile things — the factory worker’s job and the river’s health both matter. It cannot give you the strength to look in the mirror and act on what you see. Those are the domains of the heart. The cube is a compass, not a captain. It tells you which way north is; you still have to walk.

## Chapter 8  
### Thermocracy — Governance as Energy Flow  

What made the park’s stool possible? Not good intentions, not ideology, but something more fundamental: energy. The universal surplus floor — the guarantee that everyone would eat — was only possible because the community had access to a small, steady flow of resources: donations, recycling, the informal economy. That flow was distributed widely, not concentrated in a few hands. And that distribution was what kept the feedback loops short, the curiosity alive, and the fragile protected.

I call this insight Thermocracy: the thermodynamic study of governance. Every society is a dissipative structure. It lives by consuming and distributing free energy — food, fuel, information, money. Its political form crystallizes around the pattern of that flow. When energy is widely shared, you get democratic, adaptive, open societies. When energy concentrates in a few hands, the system cools into authoritarianism, rigid hierarchies, and long feedback loops.

The key metric is the a/m ratio — average income divided by median income. When the ratio is low, near 1, the society is in a democratic phase. Energy is distributed; feedback is fast; curiosity and protection of the fragile are easier to maintain. When the ratio rises above 1.3 or so, the median person feels the pinch, and the system begins to lock. The rich insulate themselves from consequences. Feedback loops lengthen. Fragile populations are the first to be squeezed, because the concentration of energy requires suppressing their voices. The system drifts toward the destructive attractor.

This is not a moral argument. It’s a thermodynamic inevitability. Austerity that starves the median citizen isn’t just cruel; it’s a phase transition toward autocracy. Democracy is not a moral achievement but a thermodynamic phase that requires widely shared exergy.

The park’s a/m ratio was practically 1. Everyone was poor, but no one was hungry, and the surplus — the keg, the food, the time — circulated. The potlatch was the distribution mechanism. That’s why the stool held.

On a planetary scale, we have the opposite. Global wealth is astronomically concentrated. The feedback loops are broken — those who make decisions do not feel their consequences. Curiosity is purchased by corporations and bent toward profit, not exploration. The fragile are sacrificed daily. The thermometer is reading a fever, and the patient is convulsing toward the destructive attractor.

But Thermocracy also gives us a lever. If we can redesign the distribution of energy — not just money, but the underlying metabolic flows of civilization — we can shift the phase. A universal surplus floor is not charity; it’s a thermodynamic intervention. Shortening feedback loops through radical transparency is not surveillance; it’s restoring the body’s ability to sense its own wounds. Protecting the fragile is not sentimentality; it’s preserving the early warning system without which the whole organism goes blind.

This is where the blueprint begins. The Common Sense Protocol, the Commons Eye, LivingOS — they are not just technical toys. They are the institutional machinery for scaling the stool to a planet. They are the nervous system of a thermocratic civilization.

## Chapter 9  
### The Schism of the Poor Party  

Before we build, a warning. The stool is fragile, not because its principles are weak, but because we are human. I want to tell you a story about a movement that almost made it — and how it tore itself apart.

Imagine a global revolution that actually succeeded. The poor party, a vast coalition of the dispossessed, has overthrown the old order and installed a regime of universal breakfast. Distributed networks, community pantries, mutual aid at scale. For a while, it works. The stool is in place.

Then comes the Harvest Collapse. A synchronized global crop failure. Within seventy‑two hours, the party’s just‑in‑time logistics become mathematically nonviable. Scarcity returns.

And the movement fractures.

Two factions emerge. The Centralists argue that the only way to prevent mass starvation is to partner with the surviving pre‑revolutionary grain cartels. They propose an algorithm‑driven rationing system, centralized, efficient, and blind to local autonomy. The Autonomists reject any deal with the cartels and invoke a constitutional emergency clause to dissolve all supra‑district grain obligations. Each community, they say, must subsist on its own bioregion.

Neither side is evil. Both are responding to genuine pressure. But the stool begins to wobble. The Centralists lengthen feedback loops in the name of efficiency. The Autonomists sacrifice collective protection in the name of local purity. Neither is curious about a third way; both dig into ideological trenches.

A compromise is forged: bulk grain from external sources is accepted but only delivered to regional hubs, not local pantries; distribution is handled by citizen juries rather than party bureaucrats; any district that can self‑certify a thirty‑percent local caloric baseline is exempted. For ninety days, it works on paper. In practice, it creates a labyrinth of overlapping jurisdictions. Centralist coordinators hoard fuel; Autonomist councils refuse to report harvest data; mutual‑aid caravans are blockaded by both sides. Food reaches most, but not all, and the delays are stratified by geography: urban cores with good internet do well under the algorithm; remote communes do well under the exemption. Suburban buffer zones, caught between both systems, wait the longest.

Within six months, the temporary compromise has calcified into permanent factional infrastructure. Both factions continue to sit on the same council, vote on the same resolutions, and issue joint statements of unity. But internally they cease sharing intelligence, stockpiles, or routing data. The party becomes a two‑headed organism, each head breathing separately.

The first violent outbreak is not between the party and the state, but between two adjacent districts — one Centralist, one Autonomist — over a contested shipment of milled flour. Barricades are erected *between* liberated zones.

The party does not collapse. It transforms. The Centralist wing evolves into a global relief agency, efficient but technocratic, increasingly indistinguishable from the old NGOs. The Autonomist wing evolves into a network of semi‑autonomous ecovillages, resilient but insular. The original field house — the symbolic birthplace — is abandoned as a joint headquarters and converted into a neutral archive, maintained by the unaffiliated, where both factions deposit records but rarely visit.

The line, as it turned out, had not been abolished. It had merely been redrawn — invisible, internal, and running straight through the heart of the revolution itself.

I tell this story not to discourage, but to immunize. The stool will always wobble. The drift toward the destructive attractor is constant, subtle, and often comes dressed in the language of necessity. The only defence is to make the drift visible. That’s what the cube is for. That’s what the protocols are for. That’s why the open mutual intelligence network must be encoded into the fabric of any stool‑based civilization — so that when the line starts to redraw itself, someone can point and say, *look, there, it’s happening again.*

The schism of the poor party is a ghost we must carry with us into the blueprint. It is the memory of every revolution that ate its own children, every movement that became the thing it overthrew. It is the reason we build not a platform but a stool — something that rocks, corrects, and never pretends to be perfectly flat.
