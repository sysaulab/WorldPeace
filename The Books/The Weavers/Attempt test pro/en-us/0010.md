
# Part I, The Park  

---

## Chapter 1  
### The Bench  

The bench was green, chipped paint, bolted into concrete. It wasn’t mine. I just sat on it one morning, and then another, and then it became the place where I met my people.

Brian sat down first. “Hi,” he said, like we were neighbours, like we’d been doing this for years. He was goofy and immediate and had a crack torch he’d set on the table like a set of keys. Chelsea stomped across the field for weeks before she ever spoke; when she finally did, she broke into my tent and announced her name as if that mended the torn fabric. M was another bench regular, a man who once looked so close to death that I held his hand and prayed to share his pain — and did, for a day, crying without knowing why. Lenora was the elder, a woman who had a home but slept in the park by choice, a half‑wolf at her side, a keg hidden in a rolling garbage bin. Suzie Q was the matriarch, drinking since childhood, who saved a girl from the street and died clean on 7‑Up and orange juice.

I didn’t join them so much as I stopped leaving. The bench became a parliament, a throne, a place where no one performed gratitude or civic propriety. The air was heavy with exhaust and sweat and urgency, but it was honest. The mask lay trampled on the sidewalk. Down here, in the treason quarter of the DTES, the national script — *Canada the good, the tolerant, the perfect* — had been torn up and used as rolling paper. Every body was a living refusal of the happiness myth.

You walk down East Hastings and the first thing that goes is the performance. No one smiles to reassure you they’re a good person. No one keeps their emotional range inside the polite rails. The people here have been abandoned by the official story, so they built their own. A nod on the sidewalk here carries more genuine acknowledgement than an hour of polite small talk uptown.

This was where my education began. Not in a library, not in a movement, but on that bench, watching the rhythm of a community that had been designated disposable and had therefore made themselves indispensable to one another. They shared sandwiches, warnings, blankets, eye contact. The constant state of quasi‑emergency stripped away the luxury of indifference. I saw more mutual aid in that park than in any church, NGO, or government program I’d ever witnessed. And the aid didn’t need a manifesto — just a body in need and a body that could offer something.

The City called it a blight. I called it the only place in the country where the social contract was being rewritten from the ground up, not as a utopia but as a raw, ongoing experiment in what happens when the pretence collapses. Violence and clarity lived side by side here, like siblings sharing a room too small. The violence wasn’t hidden behind polished doors or mortgage payments; it was out in the open, and that meant you could see it, negotiate it, understand its shape. There was a strange safety in that visibility.

I was a homeless philosopher, an unemployed truck driver, autistic and bipolar, carrying a Calling I’d had since childhood. I’d spent thirty years meditating on complex systems, on the nature of novelty, on the God who’d come to me at six and asked me to be Jesus and I’d said no, that’s not my name, but I’ll do my best to be good. I carried all of that onto that bench. And the tribe didn’t blink. They just made room.

That was the beginning. The bench was the seed. Everything that follows — the philosophy, the protocol, the galaxy — grew out of the dirt under my feet and the people who shared their stories with me.

## Chapter 2  
### The Barricade  

It started as a game. The VIP experiences. Twelve nights. Sixty guests. Six hundred thousand dollars. The keg would appear, the fire would burn, the police would walk past. The guests — business people, politicians, journalists — came expecting a party and left with a story, a token, a photo of themselves singing karaoke at 3 a.m. on unceded land.

Then a journalist wrote a serious piece: *“Is the park a sovereign nation?”* The city responded with a letter. Bylaw enforcement. A clipboard. The letter said: *“You are in violation of city bylaws governing the use of public parks.”* The designated use was not specified. It was assumed.

The community gathered. No one spoke for a long time. Then Lenora, the elder, stood and said: *“They send us letters. We send them our presence.”* She sat down. Someone else said: “They have won. They have the courts. They have the police.” A third voice, quieter: *“Did they win if we can defy them?”*

Neptune, a philosopher who lived on another bench, spoke: *“They have won the land. They have not won the people. The land is just dirt. The people are the story. We will stay.”*

Not a vote. A consensus. The kind that emerges slowly, like a fire catching.

The barricades went up at dawn. Not walls. Not weapons. Objects — a shopping cart, a pallet, a piece of plywood, a tent, a bench. The message was presence: *We are here. We have always been here. We will continue to be here.*

Police arrived. They stood at the edge of the park. They did not cross. The standoff lasted days. The community lived. The officers watched: children, elders, the woman with the half‑wolf, the philosopher on the bench, tokens passing from hand to hand, the fire that never went out, the keg that never ran dry. They saw something they could not name.

On the fifth day, the city declared a state of emergency. A legal formality that permitted intervention without warning. The police did not intervene. They’d seen too much. On the sixth day, they withdrew. Not in defeat — in *recognition*. The community had won something that wasn’t legal but was real: they could not be removed.

The barricade was not a wall. It was a reminder. The bench was a throne. The keg was a flag. The 3 a.m. hour was a parliament. The park was a nation — not in the legal sense, but in the deeper one: a people who belonged to each other and to the land.

The letter was forgotten. The bylaws went unenforced. The rhythm continued. The answer to the question — *Did they win if we can defy them?* — remained unanswered. That was the point.

## Chapter 3  
### The Law of the Potlatch  

Lenora had four walls and a door somewhere, but she slept in the park. I’m not sure why entirely — maybe the bottle, maybe the pull of the open sky, maybe the fact that home had never felt like safety and the tribe did. She walked with a half‑wolf, half‑dog at her side, a creature as in‑between as the rest of us. She enjoyed magic mushrooms, a different kind of medicine, one that opens instead of numbs. She was an alcoholic. I name that plainly because she would, and because it wasn’t a moral condition; it was a chronic consequence of a life lived in survival. The kind of life that had long stopped asking permission.

She was the elder, and that made things official in a way no paperwork could. You didn’t sign a document to join the tribe. You just started showing up, and someone showed you where to sit, and one day you realized you were inside something that had its own laws and its own lineage. Lenora was that lineage.

Every night, many hands put up her tent. She was the elder, so you didn’t let her do it herself. Every morning, those same hands collapsed it. It was ritual, repeated and reliable. Outsiders saw a group of drunks. I saw people living their traditional life in the open, disturbing the conformists just by existing.

The encampment had its own economy. During the day, we camped together and looked out for each other — some would go eat while others watched the stuff — but at night, whatever wasn’t secured in a tent was open game for the random scavengers walking in the dark. It was a constant rhythm of care and loss, like breathing.

The first night I truly belonged, I brought beer because I’d heard it was Lenora’s birthday. I didn’t have a plan. I just handed it to her so she could spread it around. I thought I was being polite. I didn’t know I was stepping into the potlatch, the old tradition of redistribution, where a person’s standing is measured by what they give away, not what they keep. She received the gift and distributed it, and I watched the beer move through hands around the park, and I understood, without anyone explaining, that this was ceremony. This was how it had always been done.

Later, someone donated a whole keg of beer, and we kept it hidden in a rolling garbage can filled with ice. It was ridiculous and brilliant. The tribe knew exactly what to do: keep it cold, keep it hidden, share it. That was our wealth. Not the alcohol itself, but the act of circulating it, of making sure no one was empty‑handed and everyone was seen. Lenora presided over that economy, half‑wolf at her feet, tent going up and coming down around her like the sun.

The potlatch had been outlawed once, made legal again some seventy‑five years ago, but it never really stopped. In that park, it was just the air we breathed. I saw it with Suzie Q, who gave a home to a fourteen‑year‑old kicked out by her mother. I saw it with M, who, after kicking his habits, volunteered at the free bike repair shop, fixing things for others in the open air. I saw it in the way people shared food at the circle, brought offerings to the elder, and let the surplus flow.

This was the law that preceded the state. The law of the short feedback loop. The law of the fire. The law that said: you are as rich as what you give. The park was not a charity case. It was a living demonstration of an alternative sovereignty — a potlatch civilization in miniature.

## Chapter 4  
### The Disappearing  

I met Brian on that bench. One morning I was sitting, and he sat down and said hi. I said hi back, and we started talking. That was the beginning.

He was goofy. Genuinely, disarmingly goofy, in a way that made you forget for a minute where you were and what he was carrying. But the thing about Brian — the thing that was the whole shape of him — was the disappearing. He would excuse himself so politely. “I’ll be right back,” he’d say, and something in his face would almost believe it. Almost. Then he’d step away, turn a corner, and that was it. He wouldn’t return. Not in five minutes, not that day. He’d gone off to use, and then he’d crash in some alley, in a doorway, on a patch of concrete no one else noticed. He’d resurface later, maybe the next morning, and never mention the broken promise. Or maybe he wouldn’t resurface at all that time, and you’d just wonder.

That was Brian. The always‑leaving and the always‑meaning‑to‑stay, both true at once, both pulling him apart.

I remember one afternoon I took him to a patio. He showed up and immediately put his crack torch right there on the table, like it was a set of keys, nothing to hide. And then he started telling me all about how he was clean, how he was doing great, how the sobriety was sticking. He was trying to convince himself way more than he was trying to convince me. I just watched. I saw his goofy, proud display of his drug paraphernalia right next to his speech about being clean, and I saw people around us react, and weirdly, I really enjoyed that night. It was so completely him. And I knew, even then, that at some point he’d say “I’ll be right back” and disappear. Maybe he did, later. Maybe that’s how that night ended too.

He claimed disability, but he took so much pride in working. His version of work was cleaning up old stuff he found in back alleys and trying to sell it. Discarded things, things no one else wanted. He’d talk about his finds like they were treasure. He was always broke, but he had this dream of going picking in the valley, maybe making it back to Penticton like he always said he wanted to. The money was never there. He could never leave the Downtown Eastside. That place had its teeth in him, and every time he excused himself and vanished around a corner, those teeth sank a little deeper.

He lived in shelters, and his stuff got stolen every single day — or at least that’s what he said. I started to suspect some of it was just his memory going, things lost inside his own head rather than taken. He blamed a lot of his condition on not having a father figure. He told me that on one of our bench talks, plain and simple, like he’d figured out the root cause and could name it even if he couldn’t fix it. I think about that a lot. He was always trying to clean things up — the junk in the alleys, himself. He wanted to be “mister clean” so badly. But then the urge would hit, or the emptiness, and he’d say those words — “I’ll be right back” — and the cleaning would stop. The alley would take him. The bench would sit empty.

One day I found him on that bench, passed out. He’d been there the whole day, in the sun, and no one had done a thing. Nobody intervened. When I got to him he was severely dehydrated and in shock. This time he hadn’t even managed to excuse himself. He’d just… stopped. A whole day on a public bench, a person right there, and the world walked past. I saw him invisible, and I saw him dying, and I saw the exact same man who’d once sat there and said hi like it was the easiest thing in the world. This was the destination of all his disappearances, the place he ended up when “I’ll be right back” ran out of road.

The last time I saw him, he had a crack pipe in his hand and he looked terrible. That’s my final snapshot of Brian. He probably excused himself after that too. Maybe he said he’d be right back, and maybe that time he really never did come back. I don’t know if he’s dead now. I don’t know if he ever somehow made it to Penticton. But I know the pattern: the polite goodbye, the corner turned, the alley that swallowed him until next time.

I miss him. I miss the goofy, proud, heartbreaking, disappearing man who sat with me on benches, who displayed his torch while reciting his sobriety, who scavenged beauty from back alleys and couldn’t manage to save any for himself. He was always leaving. He was always saying he’d be right back. And now I’m the one left, sitting on the bench, waiting for a return that’s never coming.

That was my friend Brian.

## Chapter 5  
### The Matriarch  

I remember Suzie Q.

She started drinking the hard stuff at six years old. Six. A child with a glass of something burning, and the world just let it happen. That was the ground she walked on for decades after. She spent most of her life in Vancouver, in and around the same parks and streets I came to know her in. She was a fixture, a presence, someone who’d been there so long she’d become part of the landscape.

But here’s the thing I need you to understand about Suzie Q: she saved a girl. A fourteen‑year‑old kid, kicked out by her own mother — an addict who chose the drug over her daughter. Suzie Q took her in. Hosted her for years, until that girl could stand on her own. A woman the world called broken, who’d been drinking hard since childhood, reached back into the same fire and pulled someone else out. She didn’t wait until she was fixed. She just did it anyway.

And she kept doing it. Volunteering in the park. Doing outreach. She was a giver in the old way, the potlatch way, where what you have moves through your hands to someone else. The tribe knew her as one of their matriarchs.

By the end, her rhythm was simple: she spent most of her time by the park, drank in the morning, took a nap in the afternoon, and returned home afterward. There was a gentleness to that — a slowing down, a routine held in the open air. She was still there, still present, still part of the place.

Then her last month came. And Suzie Q, who’d been drinking hard since she was six years old, kicked the booze completely. Decades of alcohol, and she just let it go. She spent her final days drinking 7‑Up with orange juice. That’s what she chose. Something sweet, something simple, something with no burn at all. I think about that — her hand around a cold glass of something harmless, something she could sip in the sun on her bench, no chaos attached. She gave herself that ending.

She passed away soon after our story began. She was sitting next to me when I was shot — but that comes later. She died at the hospital from a bullet fragment. The whole community held a ceremony in the park in her honour. The same park where she napped in the afternoons, where she volunteered, where she saved a girl’s life by offering her a place to land. The tribe gathered and mourned their matriarch properly.

She started at six with a bottle and a wound. She ended with 7‑Up and orange juice, having kicked the booze on her own terms, having pulled a child from the flames, having been honoured by her entire community in the open air she never truly left. That’s not a tragedy. That’s a life, fully lived. That’s Suzie Q.
