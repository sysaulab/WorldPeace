# The Shot

---

## I. The Biker

The man who gave the order had a tattoo of a skull on his right hand. He ran a small operation—drugs, mostly, but also stolen goods, a little money lending, the usual. He had been in the Downtown Eastside for twenty years. He knew the rhythm. The beat cops, the shelters, the dealers, the addicts. He was part of the furniture.

He did not like the pamphlets.

At first, he ignored them. Some homeless guy handing out paper—who cares? The Downtown Eastside was full of people with ideas. But the pamphlets kept appearing. In the shelters. On the benches. In the pockets of people who had never read anything longer than a bus schedule. People were talking about it. Not just the addicts. The workers. The volunteers. Even some of the cops.

He picked one up from a table in a drop-in center. "The Stool of Peace." He read it. He did not understand all of it—the grammar, the axioms, the cube of ethics—but he understood enough. It was about not needing money. About sharing. About building something that did not depend on people like him.

He did not like the pamphlets.

He had a friend who knew a guy. The guy knew another guy. The chain was long enough to be deniable. He made a call. He said, "There's a man. Sylvain. He distributes these booklets. He's getting too much attention. I want him to stop."

The voice on the other end said, "Stop meaning what?"

"Stop meaning he won't do it again."

There was a pause. Then: "This is going to cost you."

"I know."

---

## II. The Morning

Sylvain woke early. He had slept in a storage locker that night—too many people knew where he usually stayed. The concrete floor was cold. The air smelled of dust and old cardboard. He did not mind. He had slept worse.

He checked his bag. Two hundred copies of the tract. He had printed them the day before, using the last of his money. He had a plan: Hastings Street, near the Carnegie Centre. High traffic. People would take them. Some would read them. A few would pass them on.

He wrote a note in his journal:

*"The solution must rise from the ground, not fall from a single flame."*

He underlined it. Then he closed the journal and put it in his bag.

He walked out of the storage locker into the grey Vancouver morning. The sky was low and heavy. It was going to rain later. He pulled his hat down and started walking.

---

## III. The Street

Hastings Street at 10 a.m. is a river of movement. People heading to shelters, to clinics, to the corner where they sell what they have. A few tourists, looking lost. Outreach workers with clipboards. Cops in pairs.

Sylvain found a spot near the Carnegie Centre. He leaned against the wall and started offering the tracts to people passing by. Not pushing. Just offering. His hand extended, the booklet held up like a leaf.

Most people walked past. Some took one. A woman in a worn coat stopped and read the first page. She looked at him. "Is this real?" she asked.

"It's a possibility," he said. "That's all it is."

She nodded and walked away, the tract in her hand.

He distributed forty copies in an hour. Then fifty. Then sixty. A small crowd had gathered—not listening to him, but watching. He was not preaching. He was just standing there, offering. People were taking. That was enough.

---

## IV. The Gunman

The man who approached was ordinary. Medium height. Medium build. A jacket, a cap, sunglasses. He could have been anyone. He could have been no one.

He had been watching for twenty minutes. He had seen the crowd grow. He had seen people take the pamphlets and read them. He had seen the attention.

He walked up to Sylvain. Not fast. Not slow. Just walking.

Sylvain saw him. He saw the ordinary face, the ordinary clothes, the ordinary walk. He saw nothing that made him afraid. He smiled. "Would you like one?" he asked, offering a tract.

The man raised his hand. Not to take the tract. To hold a pistol.

Time does not stop in moments like this. It stretches. It becomes elastic. Sylvain had time to see the gun, to register the intention, to think: *"Oh. That's how this ends."*

He had time to see the tracts scattered on the ground around him—some still in his hands, some already in the hands of strangers, some folded into pockets and tucked into shoes.

He had time to think of Brian. Of the disappearing. Of the bench.

He had time to think: *"I'll be right back."*

Then the crack. The sound of a branch breaking in a silent forest. The impact was not pain—it was absence. The world tilted. The concrete rose. The tracts scattered like leaves in a sudden wind.

He fell.

---

## V. The Witnesses

The gunman turned and walked away. No one stopped him. No one could describe him later. "Average height," they said. "Maybe this, maybe that." The system's perfect agent: unremarkable, forgettable, effective.

People walked past Sylvain's body. Some paused. Most didn't. A man in a blue jacket knelt down and checked for a pulse. He found none. He stood up and walked away.

A woman picked up one of the tracts. She read the title. "The Stool of Peace." She tucked it into her pocket.

An outreach worker called 911. The response was slow. By the time the ambulance arrived, there was nothing to do but cover the body.

---

## VI. The Scattering

The tracts scattered. Some blew into the gutter. Some were picked up by passersby. One was found by a man named Neptune, who had heard about the pamphlets but never seen one. He read it that night, by the light of a streetlamp, on a bench not far from where Sylvain had fallen.

He did not know that the man who wrote it was dead.

He read it anyway. He read the axioms. The short feedback loops. The curiosity. The protection of the fragile. He read the dream—the bay town, the common table, the tossing of tokens.

He thought: *"This is what we needed."*

He folded the tract and tucked it into his shoe. He did not know that it had been printed by a dead man. He did not know that it was the seed of something that would outlive its author.

He just knew that he was not alone anymore. The stool was on the ground. All he had to do was sit down.

---

## VII. The Storage Locker

Three days later, the journal was found in a storage locker rented under a false name. It contained the manuscript of a book. It contained the tracts. It contained a note:

*"If I don't come back, you know what to do."*

There was no signature. But there was a name on the journal's inside cover: Sylvain.

The journal was passed to a woman named Mariam. She lived in a tent in a ravine. She read the manuscript. She cried. Then she started making plans.

---

## VIII. The Aftermath

The news covered it as a "random act of violence." They did not use his name. They did not mention the tracts. They said "a man was fatally shot" and buried him in a paragraph.

But the tracts kept circulating. People copied them. People passed them on. A man named Sven, a peer worker on a tricycle, started using them in his art therapy sessions. A woman in a shelter read one aloud to a group. Someone asked: "Where do we build the stool?"

The question spread.

The gunman was never found. The biker who ordered it went back to his business. But something had changed. The idea had already moved beyond the body. The shot had not killed it. It had only made it real.
