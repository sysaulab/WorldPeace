I remember that morning on the black ash. The tide was still retreating, hissing against the volcanic sand, and I had left my sandals behind—not out of piety, but out of practicality. I wanted to feel the hard, cool grain under my arches, and I wanted dry soles for the muddy village roads ahead. A simple trade.

Halfway to town, I cut across the cement staircase embedded in the rock barrier. The waves were battering the stones, loud enough to make me think every living thing had tucked itself deep into the cracks. I wasn't cautious. I was just moving, my mind already in the village, already dry and distracted.

Then—a white-hot needle shot through the ball of my left foot.

I looked down. A crab, no bigger than my palm, had its claw clamped into my flesh. It wasn't attacking. It was *braced*. Its legs were splayed against the rock, anchoring itself, using the only weapon it had to say the only thing it could: *I am here. You are about to crush me.*

The pain was sharp, unmistakable. I could have shaken it off, stamped it dead, felt righteous in my rage. But I didn't. I froze. I let the signal travel up my leg, into my chest, into my skull. And I realized: if I had been wearing shoes, I would never have known. I would have taken one more step, felt nothing but a mild crunch under the rubber, and continued to town, oblivious to the life I had ended and the warning I had missed.

That's when I started thinking about the rich. Not as villains, but as the shod. Money is the thickest sole I know—layered, cushioned, waterproof. It lets you walk over homes, over lives, over futures, without a twinge in your gait. And the tragedy is, it's not even malice. It's *habituation*. Once you've ridden in a car for a decade, the thought of walking a town over doesn't just seem hard—it seems impossible. Your legs forget. Your nerves dull. The car becomes your baseline, and the barefoot road becomes a foreign country.

But here is the truth that pinned me that morning, even more than the claw: I had *chosen* to be barefoot. The shoes were in my bag. I could have put them on at any moment. And the rich? They choose too. Every morning, they lace up. Not because they are forced, but because the bubble of comfort is warm, and the world outside is noisy, and the silence of the cushioned step is so very, very easy to prefer.

The crab wasn't bad. The crab was honest. It gave me a pain I had earned by stepping into its world without my armor.

And now, when I walk, I ask myself: *Am I wearing shoes today because I need them, or because I'm afraid of the pinch?* Because free will isn't the grand, heroic choice to abandon all comfort. It's the small, daily decision to leave the car in the driveway, to leave the sandals in the bag, and to let the ground—sharp, wet, unpredictable—tell you where you are and who you might be stepping on.

I felt that crab's clamp for days afterward. A purple bruise, a tender reminder. I still carry it. I hope I never stop feeling it.
