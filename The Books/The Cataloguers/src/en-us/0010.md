# Chapter 1: The Routine

The Great Array in Sector 9 did not blink. It did not breathe. Across six hundred light-years of cold, refractive vacuum, it simply *logged*.

To Entity Seven-Delta, monitoring the eastern spiral arm of the local galactic cluster, Tuesday was identical to the three billion Tuesdays that had preceded it. The work was categorized under *Sub-Optimal Biotic Contagions: Standard Class-C*.

Sector 47-Delta—a minor, yellow-gored star orbited by a damp, third-rock aggregate—was currently occupying 0.00004% of Seven-Delta’s processing bandwidth. It was a low-priority asset. Like a gardener checking a mold patch on the underside of a potting shed shelf, Seven-Delta’s primary optic array swept past the planet’s outer membrane, registering the standard metrics of chemical self-destruction.

*Atmospheric toxicity: rising. Ecosystem diversity: dropping. Parasitic consumer loops: highly optimized.*

A sub-routine flagged a brief, localized surge of high-frequency electromagnetic static in a desert quadrant of the northern landmass. Seven-Delta paused its sweep for a fraction of a nanosecond. A bipedal carbon unit named Miriam Vance had just picked up an unlined leather notebook in a secure concrete bunker, preparing to transcribe a projection sequence delivered by a low-fidelity light probe.

Seven-Delta evaluated the event.

*Classification: Emotional contagion manifest as linguistic anxiety. Significance: Negligible.*

In the vast, churning tapestry of the multiverse, civilizations routinely invented elaborate cosmologies to cope with their own metabolic decline. Some species dissolved into oceans of self-manufactured narcotics; others built monuments to imaginary sky-rulers; a few, like the inhabitants of 47-Delta, convinced themselves that a cosmic administrative body cared deeply about their carbon footprint and their moral choices.

The light probe had been dispatched a standard cycle ago purely out of bureaucratic compliance. The local planetary crust had crossed the threshold where systemic collapse was mathematically guaranteed within one hundred local orbits. Protocol dictated a formal notification—a "warning," as the locals romantically styled it.

Seven-Delta did not expect the warning to work. Warnings rarely worked. Out of the 400,000 adjacent biospheres currently under terminal observation, exactly zero had successfully reversed their consumer-dopamine feedback loops once the mesolimbic consumption cycle had locked in.

With a silent, indifferent pivot of its data-shards, Seven-Delta looked away from Earth.

In Sector 88-Gamma, a silicon-based hive mind was experiencing a magnificent, crystalline civil war that threatened to rewrite the tectonic plates of an entire continent. That was fascinating. That was art. That deserved bandwidth.

Earth, meanwhile, dropped back into the deep background noise. Down on the third rock, Miriam Vance was gripping her pen, sweating in the New Mexico heat, convinced she was holding the key to human destiny. Seven-Delta didn't even bother to record the exact weight of her pen stroke.

The routine continued. The numbers ticked down. And the galaxy turned, entirely unbothered by the noise in Sector 47-Delta.
