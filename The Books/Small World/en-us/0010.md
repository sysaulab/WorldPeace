# Lake Meridian

## A Novel

---

# Part One: Boom & Bust

## Chapter One: The Miracle Lure

---

The lake had always been there.

Nobody knew how long. The oldest residents—the snapping turtles, the ancient catfish with whiskers like fishing line—claimed the lake had simply appeared one Tuesday afternoon, fully formed, slightly damp, and smelling faintly of possibility. The turtles were unreliable narrators, but nobody had a better story.

What mattered was this: Lake Meridian was the world. Not *a* world. *The* world. Beyond its shores, there was nothing. Not emptiness, exactly. Just... less. The horizon simply stopped being interesting past a certain point, like a conversation with a man who only wanted to discuss his lawn.

The shoreline was divided.

On the northern shore, where the sun seemed to linger a little longer and the reeds grew suspiciously straight, lay the **Grand Duchy of Upper Shoria**. Its citizens had large cottages, larger opinions, and the largest motorboats the lake had ever seen. They believed, with the unshakeable conviction of people who had never been contradicted, that they were the only ones who truly understood Lake Meridian.

"We saved this lake once," they would say, apropos of nothing. "And we can save it again."

Nobody knew what they had saved the lake from. The lake seemed fine. But Upper Shorians had a way of looking at you when they said it—a squint that suggested you were either part of the problem or part of the solution, and you probably weren't part of the solution.

On the eastern shore, where the wind came cold and the trees grew gnarled and suspicious, lay the **Collective of Lower Shoria**. They had a lot of land. They had a lot of timber. They had a perpetually grumpy attitude and a deep, abiding belief that Upper Shoria was loud, vulgar, and probably cheating at something.

They were correct on all three counts.

The southern shore belonged to the **Federation of Brindlemark**—a collection of small, elegant plots owned by people who had once been important and never quite gotten over it. They had the best wine, the oldest grudges, and a system of committees so complex that deciding whose turn it was to bring the potato salad could take three weeks and two diplomatic incidents.

On the western shore, connected to the mainland by a narrow, historically significant isthmus, sat the **Kingdom of Albion's Reach**. They had once ruled the entire lake. Their rowboats had been legendary. Now they mostly sat on their porches, sipping tea, and reminiscing about the good old days when people listened to them.

"We had an empire once," they would tell anyone who would listen.

"Yes," the other shores would reply. "We remember."

"Not fondly," Lower Shoria would add.

The **Coastal Provinces**, on the far misty western shore, had been ignored for centuries. They had been there the whole time—building things, inventing things, occasionally being invaded by people who immediately regretted it—but nobody had paid them much attention. They seemed content to wait.

The **Southern Archipelago** was a chain of small, friendly islands where the fish were plentiful and the people were pleasant. They were largely ignored, which they didn't mind, until they were suddenly dragged into someone else's argument, which they minded very much.

And in the middle of it all, surrounded by all these shores and their opinions, was the lake itself.

Lake Meridian.

The reason for everything.

---

The year was 1925.

It was, everyone agreed, a very good year to be alive. Or at least, a very good year to be wealthy, which was almost the same thing, and certainly the same thing if you were wealthy enough.

In Upper Shoria, the parties had reached a level of extravagance that historians would later describe as "tacky" and participants would later describe as "Tuesday." The cottages were expanding at a rate that suggested the citizens believed the shoreline would simply grow to accommodate them. It would not, but that was a problem for a future generation.

The economy was booming. The fishing was excellent. And the miracle lures were selling faster than they could be manufactured.

The miracle lures were the invention of a man named **Herbert "Herbie" Fontaine**, a citizen of Upper Shoria who had discovered, through a combination of luck, laziness, and an accidentally spilled bottle of cough syrup, that certain fish would bite at almost anything if it was shiny enough and moved in a vaguely suggestive manner.

Herbie had no formal training in fishing. He had no formal training in anything. He had been, until the age of thirty-seven, a man who described himself as "between opportunities," which was a polite way of saying he had never had one.

But Herbie was a genius at one thing: convincing people that they needed what he was selling.

"Friends," he would announce to gathered crowds on the docks of Upper Shoria, holding aloft a small, glittering object that looked like a fish had mated with a chandelier, "do you want to catch more fish?"

The crowd would murmur assent.

"Do you want to catch *better* fish?"

The crowd would murmur more enthusiastically.

"Do you want to catch fish so magnificent that your neighbors will weep with envy and your wife will look at you with something approaching respect?"

The crowd would reach for their wallets.

The miracle lure was, in fact, a piece of painted tin with a hook attached. It caught fish about as well as a rock caught fish, which is to say: occasionally, by accident, and usually when the fish was already having a bad day.

But that didn't matter. What mattered was the *idea* of the miracle lure. What mattered was the promise. What mattered was that everyone in Upper Shoria bought one, and then everyone in Brindlemark bought one, and then even the Lower Shorians bought one (though they would deny it later), and Herbie Fontaine became the richest man on the lake.

He built a cottage so large it required its own weather system. He bought a motorboat that was technically a yacht. He purchased a hat that was technically a small building.

And he smiled, and he sold, and he believed—truly believed—that he was helping people.

"The lake provides," he would say, "for those who know how to ask."

Nobody asked what he meant by that. Nobody wanted to seem stupid.

The lake, for its part, said nothing. It simply continued to be a lake. It was good at that.

---

In Brindlemark, the mood was different.

The Federation had once been the center of the lake's cultural life. They had invented the dinner party. They had perfected the art of the passive-aggressive compliment. They had, for a glorious century, been the arbiters of taste, the gatekeepers of sophistication, the ones who decided which fork was appropriate for which fish.

Then Upper Shoria had happened.

It wasn't that Upper Shorians were uncultured. It was that they were *aggressively* uncultured. They didn't want to learn the proper fork. They wanted to invent a new fork, patent it, and sell it back to you at a markup. They didn't want to appreciate wine. They wanted to drink it from a bucket while shouting about freedom.

"They have no subtlety," complained **Marcel de la Fontaine** (no relation to Herbie, a fact he mentioned frequently and with great distress), the current President of the Brindlemark Cultural Committee. "They have no grace. They have no... no..."

"Sense?" suggested his assistant.

"I was going to say 'appropriate footwear,' but yes, that too."

Marcel was a man who believed deeply in the importance of civilization. He believed that the lake was not merely a body of water but a *community*, a *society*, a *shared project* that required careful nurturing and regular committee meetings.

He had been trying to schedule a committee meeting to discuss the possibility of forming a subcommittee to investigate the feasibility of creating a working group on the topic of possibly considering the idea of a lake-wide cultural exchange program.

The meeting had been postponed four times due to scheduling conflicts.

"Our greatest challenge," Marcel would say, "is not Upper Shoria. It is not Lower Shoria. It is that nobody can agree on what day to meet."

He was right, and he knew he was right, and that made it worse.

---

On the eastern shore, the Lower Shorians watched the Upper Shorian boom with a mixture of contempt and envy.

They had the timber. They had the land. They had the cold, unforgiving wind that built character and destroyed picnic baskets. What they didn't have was the miracle lures, the motorboats, or the ability to smile convincingly while selling someone something they didn't need.

**Joseph Vissarionovich** was not a man who smiled. He was a man who glowered. He glowered at the sunrise. He glowered at the sunset. He glowered at the fish, who had done nothing to deserve it.

"We should have miracle lures," he would mutter to his advisors. "We should have the best miracle lures. Ours would be *real* miracle lures, not the fake ones the Upper Shorians sell."

"Comrade Joseph," his advisors would say, "the miracle lures are all fake."

"Exactly! Ours would be real fake ones! Superior fake ones! Fake ones that actually work!"

The advisors would nod and back away slowly. This was the safest response to most of Joseph's ideas.

Joseph had a vision. It was a vision of a lake where everyone was equal, where everyone shared the fish, where nobody had a motorboat unless everyone had a motorboat. It was a beautiful vision, and it would have been more convincing if Joseph hadn't personally owned three motorboats and a private fishing pond.

But the vision was the point. The vision was always the point.

"The lake provides," Joseph would say, "for those who take."

Nobody asked what he meant by that either. Nobody wanted to be sent to the eastern reeds.

---

In Albion's Reach, the mood was one of dignified decline.

They had once ruled the lake. Their rowboats had been the envy of every shore. Their naval commanders had been legends. Their fish had been... well, fish, but *important* fish, caught by *important* people, in *important* ways.

Now they had tea.

**Winston Churchill**—not the famous one, but a distant cousin who had inherited the name, the bulldog, and the cigars—spent most of his days on his porch, looking out at the lake, and muttering.

"We used to matter," he would say to his bulldog, **Neville**.

Neville would snort.

"We used to be respected."

Neville would snort again.

"We used to have the best rowboats."

Neville, who had heard this speech approximately four thousand times, would sigh and put his head on his paws.

Winston believed—deeply, sincerely, with every fiber of his being—that Albion's Reach would rise again. The lake needed them. The lake *missed* them. The lake was simply waiting for the right moment to acknowledge that Albion's Reach was, and always had been, the natural leader of the lake.

"It's just a matter of time, Neville," he would say. "The lake provides."

Neville would snort. The lake had never provided Winston with anything except a damp porch, a dwindling bank account, and a bulldog with digestive issues.

But Winston had faith. Faith was what Albion's Reach had instead of resources.

It was, everyone agreed, not a fair trade.

---

In the Coastal Provinces, the ancient docks stretched into the mist.

The people there had been watching the other shores for centuries. They had seen empires rise and fall. They had seen fashions change and opinions shift. They had seen the Upper Shorians arrive, convinced they had discovered the lake, when the Coastal Provinces had been fishing its waters since before memory began.

"We should say something," a young official suggested to his elder.

"Patience," the elder replied.

"They're selling miracle lures. They're getting rich. They're claiming they invented fishing."

"Patience."

"They're *loud*."

The elder smiled. It was not a warm smile. It was the smile of someone who had seen this all before, many times, and knew how it ended.

"The lake provides," the elder said, "for those who wait."

The young official nodded, though he didn't understand.

He would understand later. Everyone eventually understood.

Or they didn't, and they became Upper Shorians.

---

In the Southern Archipelago, the fish were biting.

**Tane**—a man of indeterminate age, with a smile that suggested he knew something you didn't, and a fishing rod that had been passed down through seven generations—sat on his favorite rock and watched the sun set over Lake Meridian.

"You know," he said to no one in particular, "the other shores are arguing again."

The fish, who had heard this before, didn't respond.

"Something about lures. Something about who invented what. Something about how everyone else is wrong."

The fish continued not responding.

"I think they're going to have a war."

The fish, if they had opinions on this, kept them to themselves.

Tane sighed and cast his line. The Southern Archipelago had been involved in three wars in the past century. Each time, they had been dragged in by someone else. Each time, they had lost fishermen, lost boats, lost sons. Each time, they had been forgotten when the peace treaties were signed.

"Ah, well," Tane said. "The lake provides."

The sun sank below the horizon. The sky turned orange, then pink, then a deep purple that was almost black. The stars came out, one by one, like witnesses taking their positions.

Tomorrow, the other shores would continue their arguments.

Tomorrow, the world would continue its slow, steady march toward disaster.

But tonight, the lake was quiet.

Tonight, the fish were biting.

Tonight, Tane was content.

It would not last. It never did.

But it was nice while it lasted.

---

# Part Two: The Great Lakes War

## Chapter Two: The Reed Dispute

---

It began, as most wars do, with a misunderstanding about property lines.

The reeds in question were a small patch on the southern shore, technically within the borders of Brindlemark, but historically claimed by Germania—a tiny, disgruntled nation squeezed between Brindlemark and the Lower Shorian border.

The reeds were not valuable. They were reeds. They provided no resources, no strategic advantage, no fish, no timber. They were, in every measurable way, worthless.

But they were *there*, and they were *disputed*, and that was enough.

"We have always owned these reeds," declared **Adolf Schicklgruber**, the new leader of Germania. He had risen to power on a platform of resentment, anger, and an impressive ability to shout for extended periods without losing his voice. "They are rightfully ours. They were stolen from us by the Treaty of the Flowerbeds."

The Treaty of the Flowerbeds had been signed after the Great War of the Flowerbeds (1914-1918), a conflict that had started over a disputed rosebush and ended with millions of wilted petals. The treaty had been harsh, as treaties tend to be when written by people who have just won a war and want to make sure everyone knows it.

Among its provisions: Germania had been forced to give up the reed patch.

"The reeds are ours!" Schicklgruber would shout at rallies. "They belong to Germania! We will reclaim them, no matter the cost!"

The crowds would cheer. They didn't know why they were cheering. They didn't particularly care about reeds. But Schicklgruber was *convincing*. He had a way of making you feel that the reeds mattered, that everything mattered, that the world was a grand struggle and you were either part of the struggle or part of the problem.

"The lake provides," Schicklgruber would say, "for those who take."

Nobody asked what he meant by that.

They should have.

---

The invasion of the reeds took three hours.

The Brindlemark guards stationed there—two elderly men and a dog—were not prepared for an invasion. They had been stationed at the reed patch for twenty-seven years, and in that time, the most exciting thing that had happened was the day a duck got tangled in some fishing line.

"What's that?" asked the first guard, whose name was **Henri**.

"Looks like soldiers," said the second guard, whose name was **Pierre**.

"Germans?"

"I think so."

"Are they... are they invading us?"

"I believe so."

Henri considered this. "Should we do something?"

"I don't know. What do you do when you're invaded?"

"I think you're supposed to fight."

"I don't want to fight. I'm seventy-three."

"I'm sixty-eight."

They looked at each other.

"We could surrender," Pierre suggested.

"That seems reasonable."

And so, without firing a single water balloon, the reed patch fell to Germania.

Schicklgruber declared it a great victory. The crowds cheered. The newspapers printed headlines about the triumphant return of the reeds to the Germanian homeland. Nobody mentioned that the reeds were still just reeds, or that the guards had been offered a cup of tea and a biscuit during the surrender ceremony.

"Now," Schicklgruber announced, "we will reclaim the rest of our territory. We will make Germania great again!"

The crowds cheered louder.

The other shores watched, and worried.

---

"We should do something," Winston said to Neville.

Neville snorted.

"No, really. This Schicklgruber fellow—he's dangerous. He's not just talking about reeds. He's talking about *living space*. He's talking about expansion. He's talking about..."

Winston paused. He had been about to say "war," but the word felt too heavy, too real, too much like something that happened to other people in other places.

"He's talking about us," Winston finished. "Eventually."

Neville, who had been sleeping, opened one eye.

"We need to stand up to him. We need to show him that Albion's Reach won't be intimidated. We need to..."

Winston paused again. What did they need to do? They had no army to speak of. Their rowboats had been scrapped decades ago. Their once-mighty navy was now a single canoe and a paddle with a crack in it.

"We need to find allies," Winston said finally. "We need to work with Upper Shoria."

Neville made a noise that suggested this was a bad idea.

"I know, I know. They're loud. They're vulgar. They have no sense of history. But they have the biggest motorboats, and right now, we need motorboats more than we need dignity."

Neville put his head back down.

Winston sighed. "The lake provides," he muttered. "I just wish it would provide something useful."

---

In Upper Shoria, **Franklin Delano**—known to his friends as Franky, to his enemies as "that man in the wheelchair," and to history as the man who would lead the lake through its darkest hours—sat on his porch and considered the situation.

The situation was not good.

Germania was expanding. Lower Shoria was making threatening noises. Brindlemark was panicking. Albion's Reach was... being Albion's Reach.

And somewhere in the middle of all this, the ordinary people of Lake Meridian just wanted to fish.

"We can't stay out of this," Franky said to his advisors. "If we let Schicklgruber take the reeds, he'll take the shoreline next. Then he'll take the docks. Then he'll take the lake."

"Can one man take a lake?" asked an advisor.

"One man can try. And if enough people let him, he'll succeed."

Franky lit a cigarette. He had been smoking since he was twelve, a habit he attributed to "stress" and his doctors attributed to "a complete disregard for personal survival."

"The lake provides," Franky said, "but only if we're willing to work for it. Only if we're willing to fight for it."

"Are we willing to fight?"

Franky took a long drag. "We're going to have to be."

---

The war, when it came, was strange.

It was not fought with guns or bombs or any of the traditional instruments of destruction. It was fought with water balloons, sabotaged picnic baskets, and propaganda leaflets that were mostly just insults written in large letters.

The Upper Shorians had the advantage in resources. They had more motorboats, more lures, more ability to make a lot of noise.

The Lower Shorians had the advantage in numbers. They had more people, more timber, more willingness to endure suffering for no apparent reason.

Brindlemark had the advantage in sophistication. They had better wine, better cheese, and a more nuanced understanding of the situation, which was unfortunately useless in a war.

Albion's Reach had Winston, who was determined to lead even though nobody had asked him to, and Neville, who was determined to sleep through the entire conflict.

And Germania had Schicklgruber, who was determined to conquer the lake, one reed patch at a time.

The battles were fought over strategic locations: the best fishing spots, the most scenic overlooks, the patches of shoreline where the sunsets were most photogenic. Soldiers on both sides would arrive at dawn, occupy the position, and then spend the rest of the day arguing about who had gotten there first.

"Outrageous!" the Germanian commander would shout. "This is our fishing spot! It has been our fishing spot for generations!"

"You only say that because you want it!" the Upper Shorian commander would reply. "You don't have any historical claim to this spot! Your people have never even *fished* here!"

"We fished here in our hearts!"

"That's not how fishing works!"

The arguments would continue until sunset, at which point both sides would agree to withdraw and try again tomorrow. It was, everyone agreed, the most civilized war in the history of warfare.

Except for the screaming. And the water balloons. And the leaflets.

And the screaming.

---

Schicklgruber was not satisfied with the slow pace of the war.

"We should be winning!" he shouted at his advisors. "We should have conquered the entire lake by now! Why are we still fighting over fishing spots?"

"Because, Mein Führer, the other shores are resisting."

"Then we need to hit them harder! We need to make them *suffer*! We need to..."

He paused. A thought had occurred to him. It was a terrible thought, a monstrous thought, but it was also a useful thought.

"We need to target their morale," he said slowly. "We need to destroy the things they love. The things that make them happy. The things that give them hope."

"What things, Mein Führer?"

Schicklgruber smiled. It was not a pleasant smile. It was the smile of a man who had just found a new way to make the world worse.

"The pond," he said. "The little pond near the Brindlemark border. The one with all the colorful fish."

"The one they call the 'Cultural Pond'?"

"Yes. That one."

"Mein Führer, that pond is... it's just a pond. There's nothing strategic about it. It's just a place where people go to look at fish and feel good about themselves."

"Exactly," Schicklgruber said. "And that's why we're going to destroy it."

---

The draining of the Cultural Pond took two weeks.

The Brindlemarkers, who had not expected this, could only watch in horror as the water level dropped, the fish flopped helplessly in the mud, and everything that had made the pond beautiful slowly disappeared.

"We have to stop them!" someone shouted.

"How?"

"We could... we could..."

Nobody knew what to do. The pond was not strategically important. It was not militarily significant. It was just a pond. But it was *their* pond, and its destruction felt like a wound, a violation, a crime that could never be forgiven.

"This is not war," Marcel de la Fontaine said, tears streaming down his face. "This is... this is..."

"Barbarism?"

"Yes. That."

The other shores watched in silence. They had all heard rumors about what was happening at the pond. They had all heard stories. But they had not believed them, not really. Surely nobody would be so cruel. Surely nobody would target a pond, a simple pond, a place of no strategic value.

But Schicklgruber had done it. And now the pond was gone, and the fish were gone, and the world was a little darker.

"The lake provides," someone whispered. "But it also takes away."

---

The war continued.

The Upper Shorians, now fully engaged, began to turn the tide. They had the resources. They had the motorboats. They had Franky, who had a gift for strategy, for making the impossible seem possible, for convincing people that the lake could be saved.

The Lower Shorians, after a period of cooperation with the Germanians, switched sides when Schicklgruber made the mistake of underestimating Joseph's ego.

"We are not allies," Joseph declared. "We are... temporary co-belligerents."

"That's not a real thing," his advisors said.

"It is now."

The Brindlemarkers, who had been the initial victims, discovered a reserve of strength they hadn't known they had. They fought with the fury of people who had lost something precious, something irreplaceable.

The Albion's Reachers fought with dignity, which is to say they fought bravely and then retreated to their porches for tea.

And the Southern Archipelago, drawn into a conflict they had no stake in, sent their best fishermen to serve as scouts. They were not soldiers. They were not warriors. But they knew the lake better than anyone, and they used that knowledge to help the allies.

"The lake provides," Tane said, as he guided an Upper Shorian boat through a hidden channel. "But it also helps those who help themselves."

"Is that a saying?"

"It is now."

---

The war ended with a splash.

A very large splash.

The Upper Shorians had been working on something in secret—a project that Franky called "the Atomic Splash." It was a device, a weapon, a force so powerful that it could change the course of the war in an instant.

Nobody knew exactly what it was. The scientists who had built it were not entirely sure themselves. But they knew it would work, because they had tested it on a small scale, and the results had been... impressive.

"We drop this in the lake," Franky explained, "and it creates a splash so large that it will flood every shore. It will destroy everything in its path. It will end the war."

"But it will also destroy us," an advisor said.

"I know."

"Then why are we doing this?"

Franky took a long drag on his cigarette. "Because if we don't, Schicklgruber will. And I'd rather be the one who ends the world than the one who lets it end."

The advisors nodded. They didn't understand, not really. But they trusted Franky. They trusted him with their lives, their futures, their lake.

They dropped the Atomic Splash on a quiet Tuesday morning.

---

The splash was magnificent.

It rose from the lake like a tower of water, like a fist of the gods, like everything that had ever been good and pure and holy rising up to cleanse the world. It spread outward in concentric rings, each wave larger than the last, each ripple more powerful.

The shores were flooded. The cottages were swept away. The reeds were uprooted, the docks were destroyed, the fishing spots were erased.

But the war was over.

Schicklgruber was caught in the splash. He had been standing on a dock, shouting at his advisors, when the wave hit him. He was swept away, his shouts drowned out by the roar of the water, his dreams of conquest washed away like so much mud.

Germania surrendered. The remaining Germanians—those who had not been swept away, those who had not fled, those who had somehow survived—laid down their water balloons and begged for mercy.

The lake, for its part, said nothing. It simply absorbed the splash, as it had absorbed everything else, and continued to be a lake.

"The lake provides," Tane said, watching from his island. "And sometimes, it takes away."

"Is that a saying?"

"It is now."

---

The war was over, but the world was changed.

The shores were scarred. The cottages were destroyed. The reeds were gone. The Cultural Pond was a memory, a ghost, a wound that would never fully heal.

But the lake remained.

And the people remained.

And as they looked out at the waters, still rippling from the splash, they knew that they had survived something terrible.

Something that should never have happened.

Something that must never happen again.

Never again.

---

*But, of course, it did happen again.*

*It always did.*

*The lake provides, after all.*

*For those who take.*

# Part Three: The Cold War

## Chapter Three: The Staring Contest

---

After the war, the lake was divided.

Not by water—the water was the same, it was all the same lake—but by ideology. By belief. By the fundamental question of how to fish, and who should get the fish, and what to do with the fish once you had them.

On one side: Upper Shoria. They believed in freedom. They believed in motorboats. They believed that everyone should have the right to fish however they wanted, as long as they could afford the equipment.

On the other side: Lower Shoria. They believed in equality. They believed in shared resources. They believed that everyone should have the same fishing equipment, even if the equipment was terrible and nobody wanted it.

In between: everyone else, trying to figure out which side to join, or whether to join a side at all.

"This is ridiculous," Marcel de la Fontaine said, watching the two superpowers glare at each other across the water. "We just had a war. We just lost the Cultural Pond. Can't we have a moment of peace?"

"No," said his advisor.

"Why not?"

"Because the Upper Shorians and the Lower Shorians hate each other."

"They didn't hate each other during the war."

"They were allies of convenience. Now that the convenience is over, they remember that they hate each other."

Marcel sighed. "The lake provides," he muttered. "But it also provides reasons to argue."

---

The Iron Curtain was an ugly floating fence.

It had been built by the Lower Shorians, who claimed it was necessary to protect their fishing waters from Upper Shorian "aggression." The Upper Shorians claimed it was a violation of the lake's natural unity. Nobody asked the fish what they thought.

The fence stretched across the middle of the lake, from the northern shore to the southern shore, dividing the water into two spheres of influence. On one side, the Upper Shorians and their allies fished freely. On the other side, the Lower Shorians and their allies fished according to a five-year plan that was revised every three years and never actually implemented.

"It's not a fence," Joseph said. "It's a... a protective barrier."

"It's a fence," his advisors said.

"It's a barrier that happens to look like a fence."

"It's a fence."

Joseph glowered. "You're all fired."

"You say that every week."

"This time I mean it."

He didn't mean it. He never meant it. But it made him feel better to say it.

---

The staring contest lasted for decades.

It was not a literal staring contest—though there were moments, during diplomatic summits, when it came close. It was a contest of wills, of beliefs, of systems. It was a competition to see who could build the most impressive gazebo, the most efficient fishing rod, the most convincing propaganda.

The Upper Shorians built gazebos so elaborate that they required their own maintenance crews. The Lower Shorians built gazebos so utilitarian that they were basically just roofs on posts.

The Upper Shorians developed fishing rods with so many features that it took a manual to operate them. The Lower Shorians developed fishing rods that were designed by committee and produced by factories that were managed by committees that reported to other committees.

The Upper Shorians made movies about how great it was to be an Upper Shorian. The Lower Shorians made movies about how terrible it was to be an Upper Shorian.

Both sides watched the other's movies and felt superior.

"The lake provides," Franky said, watching the latest Lower Shorian propaganda film, "for those who believe."

"Do you believe?"

"I believe that we're right. And I believe that they believe they're right. And I believe that somewhere in the middle, there's a truth that neither of us can see."

"That's very philosophical."

"I have a lot of time to think. I'm in a wheelchair."

---

The Space Race was the most absurd part of it all.

It wasn't actually about space—there was no space, there was only the lake—but about who could build the best floating gazebo. The Upper Shorians wanted to put a gazebo on the moon. The Lower Shorians wanted to put a gazebo in the deepest part of the lake.

"There's no moon," their advisors pointed out. "There's just the lake. And the sky. And the sky is mostly empty."

"The sky is the moon," the Upper Shorians declared. "And we will put a gazebo on it."

"The deepest part of the lake is just... deeper," the Lower Shorian advisors said. "There's nothing there. Just darkness. And probably some very confused fish."

"Then we will put a gazebo there, and the fish will be less confused."

The gazebo race continued for years. The Upper Shorians built gazebos that could float on the surface. The Lower Shorians built gazebos that could sink to the bottom. Neither gazebo was particularly useful, but both were very expensive, and that was the point.

"We are winning," Franky declared.

"We are winning," Joseph declared.

Both were wrong. Both were right. It depended on how you defined winning.

The lake, as always, said nothing.

---

The Cuban Missile Crisis—known in this world as the "Bay of Pigsy's Pier Incident"—was the closest the lake came to another war.

It started with a wind chime.

The Lower Shorians had installed a wind chime on a small island near the Upper Shorian shore. It was a large wind chime, a loud wind chime, a wind chime that played "The Internationale" every time the wind blew.

The Upper Shorians were not amused.

"This is a provocation!" Franky declared. "They're trying to annoy us into submission!"

"Or maybe they just like the music," an advisor suggested.

"Nobody likes that music. It's terrible. The notes are all wrong."

The Upper Shorians demanded that the wind chime be removed. The Lower Shorians refused. The Upper Shorians blockaded the island. The Lower Shorians threatened to install more wind chimes.

For thirteen days, the world held its breath.

For thirteen days, the lake was silent.

For thirteen days, everyone waited to see who would blink first.

In the end, it was Joseph.

"We will remove the wind chime," he announced, "in exchange for the Upper Shorians removing their blockade."

"Agreed," Franky said.

The wind chime was removed. The blockade was lifted. The world exhaled.

But the tension remained. It always remained.

"The lake provides," Tane said, watching from his island. "But it also reminds us of what we almost lost."

"Is that a saying?"

"It is now."

---

The proxy wars were fought over potato salad.

Nobody knew how it started. One day, the Upper Shorians and the Lower Shorians were arguing about fishing rights. The next day, they were arguing about potato salad recipes.

"The Upper Shorian recipe is clearly superior," declared the Upper Shorian ambassador. "It has more mayonnaise."

"Mayonnaise is a tool of oppression," replied the Lower Shorian ambassador. "Our recipe uses vinegar. It is the people's dressing."

"Vinegar is sour. Like your attitude."

"Mayonnaise is bland. Like your culture."

The argument spread. Other shores took sides. Brindlemark, ever the diplomat, suggested a compromise that involved both mayonnaise and vinegar. Both sides rejected it.

"This is not about potato salad," Marcel said. "This is about ideology. This is about power. This is about who gets to decide what the lake eats at its picnics."

"Can't we just have both?"

"NO!"

The proxy wars continued for decades. They were fought in kitchens, at picnics, at potlucks. They were fought with recipes and ingredients, with words and gestures. They were fought with a intensity that seemed absurd, that was absurd, but that was also real.

Because in the end, it wasn't about potato salad.

It was about everything.

And everything, as always, was worth fighting for.

---

# Part Four: New World Order

## Chapter Four: The End of the Fence

---

The fence fell in 1991.

It didn't fall dramatically—it wasn't pushed, it wasn't torn down, it wasn't swept away by a wave. It simply... gave up. The ropes rotted. The posts tilted. The sections drifted apart, one by one, until the Iron Curtain was nothing more than a collection of floating debris.

The Lower Shorians, who had built the fence, watched it go with a mixture of relief and despair.

"What will we do now?" they asked.

"We will sell the scrap," Joseph said. "We will transition to a market economy. We will..."

"Comrade Joseph, the market economy is an Upper Shorian invention."

"I know. That's why it will work."

The transition was not smooth. The Lower Shorian economy, which had been designed around the fence, struggled to adapt. Factories that had produced fence posts now produced... nothing. Workers who had maintained the fence now had no work. The five-year plans, which had never worked, now worked even less.

"We are free," Joseph announced. "We are finally free."

"Free to do what?"

"Free to... to..."

He paused. He didn't know. None of them knew. They had spent so long fighting the fence that they had forgotten what it was like to live without it.

The lake, as always, said nothing. It simply continued to be a lake.

---

Upper Shoria, now the only superpower, became insufferable.

They had always been loud. They had always been confident. But now, with no one to oppose them, they became something worse: smug.

"We won," they declared. "The Cold War is over. We are the victors. The lake is ours."

"It's not yours," Brindlemark pointed out. "It's everyone's."

"Everyone who matters."

"Everyone matters."

"Everyone who agrees with us matters."

"That's not how it works."

"That's how it works now."

The Upper Shorians began to reorganize the lake according to their preferences. They created new committees, new organizations, new rules. They called it "the New World Order," which sounded ominous but was mostly just boring.

"We will bring democracy to the lake," Franky's successor announced. (Franky had died years earlier, a loss that the entire lake mourned.) "We will bring freedom. We will bring..."

"More committees?" Marcel suggested.

"Yes! Committees are the foundation of democracy!"

"We have enough committees. We have too many committees. We have committees to manage our committees."

"Then we will create a committee to streamline the committees!"

Marcel sighed. "The lake provides," he muttered. "But it also provides bureaucracy."

---

The War on Terror—known in this world as the "War on Litter"—began with a goose.

A goose had nipped an Upper Shorian diplomat. The diplomat, who had been walking along the shore, had not expected to be nipped. The goose, who had been defending its nest, had not expected to be disturbed.

But the nip happened. And the Upper Shorians, who had been looking for a reason to assert their power, found one.

"This is an act of war!" they declared. "We will not tolerate aggression against our diplomats! We will find the geese responsible and bring them to justice!"

"The geese are... geese," Brindlemark pointed out. "They don't have a government. They don't have a military. They don't have..."

"They have nests! They have territory! They have weapons of mass destruction!"

"Geese don't have weapons of mass destruction."

"That's what they want you to think."

The Upper Shorians invaded the reed beds of the Coastal Provinces, looking for the geese responsible. They found no geese, but they did find a few surprisingly large snapping turtles, which they declared "suspicious" and "potentially dangerous."

"We will liberate the turtles," they announced. "We will bring them democracy."

The turtles, who had been minding their own business for approximately two hundred years, were not consulted.

---

The Coastal Provinces watched the invasion with patience.

They had been watching the other shores for centuries. They had seen empires rise and fall. They had seen wars start and end. They had seen the Upper Shorians make the same mistakes over and over, each time believing that this time would be different.

"They will learn," the elder said. "Eventually."

"Will they?"

"Probably not."

"Then why do we wait?"

The elder smiled. "Because we have no choice. Because the lake is patient, and we must be patient too. Because..."

He paused. He looked out at the water, at the docks stretching into the mist, at the ships that had been built and would be built, at the future that was coming whether anyone was ready for it or not.

"Because the lake provides," he said, "for those who wait."

"Is that a saying?"

"It is now."

---

The 2008 recession hit everyone hard.

The lakefront property market, which had been booming for years, suddenly collapsed. Cottages that had been worth fortunes were now worth nothing. Motorboats that had been status symbols were now liabilities. The miracle lures, which had been the foundation of the Upper Shorian economy, were revealed to be exactly what they were: pieces of painted tin.

"We are ruined," the Upper Shorians wailed. "We are destroyed. We are..."

"Reaping what you sowed," the Lower Shorians suggested.

"Shut up."

"Make us."

They couldn't. Nobody could. The recession had affected everyone, from the richest Upper Shorian to the poorest Lower Shorian to the most forgotten Southern Archipelago fisher.

"The lake provides," Tane said, watching the boats being repossessed. "But it also takes away."

"Is that a saying?"

"It's always been a saying."

---

# Part Five: The Modern Era

## Chapter Five: The Great Evaporation

---

The water level began to drop in 2015.

It was gradual at first—so gradual that nobody noticed. A few inches here, a few inches there. The docks were a little higher above the water. The reeds were a little further from the shore. The fish were a little harder to find.

Then, in 2020, the drop accelerated.

It wasn't just the water. The sky itself seemed to be changing. The sunsets were different—more orange, less pink. The stars were dimmer. The air was warmer.

"We need to do something," Brindlemark declared. "We need to form a committee."

"We already have a committee."

"Then we need to form a committee to manage the committee."

"That's what we did last time."

"Then we need to form a committee to manage the committee that manages the committee."

Marcel sighed. "The lake provides," he muttered. "But it also provides reasons to form committees."

---

The 2020 pandemic—known as the "Pond Scum Outbreak"—was the most peaceful period in decades.

Everyone stayed in their own cottages. Everyone communicated by megaphone. Everyone yelled at each other from a safe distance.

"We are flattening the curve!" the Upper Shorians announced.

"We are protecting our people!" the Lower Shorians announced.

"We are... we are..." the Brindlemarkers stammered. "We are trying our best!"

The Pond Scum was a mysterious illness. It affected the fish first, then the birds, then the people. It made you tired, then sick, then—in the worst cases—gone.

Nobody knew where it came from. Nobody knew how to stop it. Nobody knew how long it would last.

But the lake, as always, continued to be a lake.

---

The Great Evaporation was undeniable by 2025.

The water level had dropped by ten feet. The shorelines had expanded, creating new mudflats where the water had once been. The fish were fewer. The sunsets were stranger. The air was warmer.

"We need to do something," the Upper Shorians declared. "We need to save the lake!"

"How?"

"We will build a dam!"

"A dam where?"

"A dam... a dam..."

They didn't know. There was nowhere to build a dam. The lake was the lake. It had no source, no tributary, no river feeding it. It was self-contained. Self-sufficient. Self...

Self-evaporating.

"The lake provides," Tane said, watching the water recede from his favorite rock. "But it also takes away."

"Is that a saying?"

"It's the only saying."

---

The "Hair-Trigger" moment came in the summer of 2025.

It was a shoving match, nothing more. An Upper Shorian fisherman and a Lower Shorian fisherman, both claiming the same prime sunbathing spot on a newly exposed mudflat. They pushed. They shouted. They called each other names.

"You're a capitalist pig!"

"You're a communist dinosaur!"

"The lake is ours!"

"The lake is everyone's!"

"NO!"

Other fishermen joined in. Other shores sent support. The shoving match became a brawl. The brawl became a battle.

And somewhere in the chaos, someone threw a punch that landed wrong.

The world held its breath.

Again.

---

But the war didn't come.

Not this time.

The two fishermen, panting and bruised, looked at each other. They looked at the mudflat, which was just mud. They looked at the lake, which was smaller than it had been. They looked at the horizon, which was still there, still waiting.

"This is stupid," the Upper Shorian said.

"What?"

"This. All of this. We're fighting over mud."

The Lower Shorian looked down. The mud was brown. It was squishy. It was not worth fighting over.

"Yeah," he said. "It is stupid."

They didn't shake hands. They didn't apologize. They simply walked away, in opposite directions, back to their own shores.

The other fishermen watched, confused.

"Aren't you going to fight?"

"No."

"Why not?"

The Upper Shorian paused. He looked out at the lake, at the water that remained, at the sun setting over the horizon.

"Because the lake is disappearing," he said. "And if we keep fighting, there won't be anything left to fight over."

The Lower Shorian, on the other side of the mudflat, said the same thing at the same time. Neither heard the other. But they both understood.

---

The lake was still disappearing.

The future was still uncertain.

The arguments were still happening, every day, about everything, from potato salad to fishing rights to whose turn it was to bring the potato salad.

But something had changed.

Not much. Not enough. But something.

A small recognition. A tiny understanding. A moment of clarity in a world that had been cloudy for too long.

"We are all in this together," Tane said, watching the sun set. "We are all part of the same lake."

"Is that a saying?"

"It's always been a saying. We just forgot."

---

# Epilogue: The Lake Provides

---

The lake continued to be a lake.

It did not disappear. It did not evaporate completely. It did not become a mudflat, a memory, a cautionary tale. It simply... shrank. A little more each year. A little less each decade.

The shores adapted. They built new docks. They found new fishing spots. They learned to live with less water, less fish, less certainty.

They continued to argue. They continued to form committees. They continued to make the same mistakes, over and over, each time believing that this time would be different.

But they also continued to fish. To picnic. To watch the sunsets. To tell stories about the old days, when the water was higher and the miracle lures sold like hotcakes and the future seemed endless.

"The lake provides," they said.

"For those who take," they added.

"For those who wait," they continued.

"For those who remember," they finished.

And the lake, as always, said nothing.

It simply continued to be a lake.

It was good at that.
