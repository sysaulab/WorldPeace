# Generative Topologies: Self-Referential Networks and the Origin of Novelty

**Author:** Sylvain Saucier  
**Repository:** github.com/sysaulab/softrng

---

**Abstract**

For centuries, Laplace’s demon has embodied the belief that a perfect snapshot of the present, combined with deterministic laws, yields the entire future. This paper presents a logical, not probabilistic, refutation of that belief. I introduce the concept of a *generative topology*: a system whose internal dependencies form closed loops, such that its evolution is underdetermined by any static description. When such a topology is placed in a physical runtime—a computer, a cell, a brain—the system must actively resolve its own mutual constraints in time, generating states that did not exist, even implicitly, in its initial specification. I provide an empirical minimum viable proof: the `Seedy64` device, a deterministic ring of three threads that, from an all-zero start, produces statistically ideal randomness and exhaustively covers the 32-bit integer space. The device demonstrates that self-referential code, coupled to a real execution environment, forms a generative topology whose output cannot be predicted by any simulation shorter than the run itself. This principle is then extended: the fertilised egg is a generative topology of genome and cytoplasm; the conscious brain is a generative topology of neural loops and sensory input; the Big Bang itself was the ultimate generative topology. The universe, life, and mind do not unfold pre-written scripts—they actively create novelty. Generative topologies are the grammar of becoming.

---

## 1. Introduction – The Surprise That Built the Universe

Imagine the smartest demon that could ever exist. Laplace’s demon knows every particle in the universe, every force, every law. Give it a single perfect snapshot of Now, and it could tell you the entire future and past. For centuries this thought experiment felt unshakeable. If the world is made of deterministic pieces, then everything that ever happens is already folded inside the present, like a movie that has already been shot.

But what if some things simply *aren’t there yet*—not because the demon is ignorant, but because the snapshot itself is incomplete? Not missing a detail, but missing something that only *happening* can provide. This is not a gap in our physics. It is a gap in logic, and a tiny computer program has made it impossible to ignore.

Picture three friends playing a strange game. Each must call out a number, but the rule is perverse: Ana’s next number depends on what Ben is about to say, Ben’s depends on Clara’s, and Clara’s depends on Ana’s. They all must answer *at the same time*. No one can go first. There is no correct answer in advance; the rules don’t pick a unique number. Yet if they actually start shouting, a number will emerge, shaped by tiny real‑time accidents—a hesitation, a breath, a glance. That number didn’t exist before they played. It was *created* by the playing.

This is the heart of a device called `Seedy64`, a ring of three simple threads on a computer chip, designed to probe the nature of creative novelty. The threads depend on one another in exactly such a loop. The machine starts with every value set to zero. No outside randomness is fed in. Yet when it runs, the first output is completely unpredictable; run it again from the same zeros, and you’ll eventually get every possible number. It passes the toughest randomness tests. The code is totally deterministic, yet the system behaves as if it is throwing dice. It isn’t chaos in the usual sense—chaos amplifies tiny hidden differences, but here the starting point is perfectly identical each time. The system genuinely *generates* new information, paying for it in processor heat. The novelty is not hidden in the initial state; it is born in the act of resolving a mutual stand‑off.

Philosophers have long flirted with the idea that time itself creates possibilities. Henri Bergson insisted that the future is not just a rearranged past, but an ongoing invention. The ring gives this a concrete skeleton: some patterns of interdependence have no unique solution on paper. They must be *lived* to be settled. That act of settling is a creative event, a tiny big bang of newness.

Now scale this up. At the beginning of our cosmos, the newborn universe was not a neat line of dominoes waiting to fall. It was a dense, over‑excited tangle where everything was coupled to everything else—a ferociously self‑referential knot. The Big Bang itself may have been the grandest resolution of a mutual‑dependency network. The laws were there, but the outcome was not yet fixed. The first structure—the clumping of matter, the breaking of symmetries—was the universe’s first “number,” shouted into existence by the sheer necessity of becoming. Dice were not added later by quantum mechanics; they were woven into the beginning because a self‑implicating origin cannot be statically determined.

That same creativity echoes in every living thing and every conscious moment. An embryo, a thought, a decision—each, as we will see, is a generative topology that must run to resolve. The future is not yet written. It never was. And this paper is about why that must be so.

## 2. The Demon’s Blind Spot: Why Static Descriptions Fail

Laplace’s confidence rested on an unexamined assumption: that a complete state description, plus complete laws, forms a well-posed initial value problem. In physical modeling, this works for systems whose dependency graph is a directed acyclic network—each variable’s future value depends only on variables at earlier times, never on peers or on itself. But not all systems are so linear. When dependencies form closed loops, the assumption collapses.

Consider three variables, A, B, and C, whose next states are determined simultaneously by each other: A ← f(B), B ← g(C), C ← h(A). This is an algebraic loop. Mathematically, one may search for a fixed point that satisfies all three equations at once; but such a fixed point may not exist, may not be unique, and even if it exists, the process of *finding* it is not captured by the equations themselves. In a physical implementation, there is no instantaneous global solver. The system must evolve, components updating asynchronously, and the order of those updates becomes a determining factor—yet that order is not deducible from the static equations. It must be *provided by time*.

This is the logical gap. The demon, given only the equations and the current values, would have to simulate the system’s entire serialization history to know the outcome. But that simulation is itself a physical process that consumes time and energy. The demon cannot leap ahead of the world it is trying to predict without *being* that world. The snapshot is insufficient not because it lacks precision, but because it lacks a dimension: the order of resolution.

## 3. Generative Topologies: A Definition

I propose the term **generative topology** to name the class of systems that exploit this gap to produce novelty. A generative topology is defined by two components:

1. **A self-referential rule set:** a set of deterministic update rules whose dependencies contain at least one closed loop, meaning that the next state of some component depends, directly or indirectly, on its own current state via a cycle of mutual reference.
2. **A physical runtime environment:** a non-ideal execution medium that forces the rule set to be resolved asynchronously in time, introducing an irreproducible serialization of events that is not determined by the rules alone.

When these two ingredients combine, the system exhibits **active creative novelty**: it generates states that are underdetermined by any static description of the initial conditions and rules. Such states are not merely unpredictable due to hidden variables or measurement error; they are *ontologically new*—they come into being only through the act of resolution.

Generative topologies are not chaotic. A chaotic system can be simulated perfectly if given perfectly precise initial conditions. A generative topology cannot, because the initial condition (the code plus the starting values) does not, even in principle, pick out a unique trajectory. The trajectory is *chosen* by the runtime. Generative topologies are thus a third category, distinct from both deterministic chaos and indeterministic randomness.

The following section provides a minimal, empirically testable instance of such a system.

---

## 4. The Seedy64 Proof: A Minimal Generative Topology

### 4.1 Construction

`Seedy64` consists of three 64-bit unsigned integer memory cells, `nodes[0]`, `nodes[1]`, `nodes[2]`, initialised to zero. Three operating-system threads are launched, each dedicated to one directed edge of a ring: thread 0 reads `nodes[0]`, transforms the value via a mixing function, and writes the result to `nodes[1]`; thread 1 reads `nodes[1]` and writes to `nodes[2]`; thread 2 reads `nodes[2]` and writes to `nodes[0]`. The mixing function involves bit rotations, XOR operations, and addition with a prime-based accumulator, designed to propagate any bit-flip to all bits within a few iterations. All reads and writes are performed with volatile semantics, forcing actual memory access and preventing compiler reordering. No synchronization primitives (locks, atomics) are used.

The main thread allows the ring to churn for an initialisation period, then samples the XOR of all three nodes at regular intervals. Whenever the sampled value differs from the previous sample, the difference is used to fill a user-provided byte buffer.

### 4.2 The Generative Mechanism

The ring of dependencies forms a self-referential topology: the next value of `nodes[1]` depends on `nodes[0]`, which depends on `nodes[2]`, which depends on `nodes[1]`. Because the threads run concurrently on a multi-core processor, the order in which reads and writes interleave is determined by the hardware’s memory subsystem—cache coherence traffic, bus arbitration, and core scheduling. This order is not encoded in the program; it is a physical event, paid for in processor energy. Each such event is a *resolution* of the mutual constraint, and that resolution becomes part of the evolving state, amplified and redistributed by the mixing function.

The critical point: the initial state (all zeros) and the deterministic code do not force any particular sequence of memory-operation interleavings. Numerous sequences are compatible with the initial condition, and each yields a different subsequent state. The static description of the system is therefore underdetermined. The system must run to resolve which sequence actually occurs, and that resolution is a novel, irreproducible act.

### 4.3 Empirical Results

Two empirical properties confirm that `Seedy64` is a generative topology, not a chaotic one.

**Statistical randomness.** Output streams from `Seedy64` pass the PractRand test suite up to at least 2^20 bytes and beyond. No algorithmic pattern is detectable by standard statistical batteries.

**Exhaustive coverage.** When the device is started, allowed to produce only its first 64-bit output, then immediately destroyed and rebuilt, the collected first outputs over thousands of runs uniformly cover the entire 32-bit integer range. The same all-zero start yields, over many fresh runs, every possible small integer. This is not a hidden attractor; it is exhaustive novelty.

These results refute the hypothesis that `Seedy64` is merely amplifying microscopic differences in initial conditions (because the initial condition is digitally identical each time). They demonstrate that the system *creates* its output, paying for it in energy, and that its output cannot be derived by any simulation shorter than the actual run.

## 5. The Code and the Machine: Why Generativity Requires a Runtime

The empirical success of `Seedy64` clarifies an essential feature of generative topologies: the self-referential rule set alone is not sufficient. The code, if executed in a perfectly deterministic, sequentially ordered virtual machine that imposes a fixed update order, would produce a repeatable trajectory. Generativity arises from the *coupling* between the self-referential rules and a physical runtime whose behaviour is not fully captured by those rules. In the case of `Seedy64`, the runtime is the CPU, operating system, and memory hierarchy—complex enough to supply irreproducible interleavings, yet not random in the sense of a hardware random number generator. The interleavings are the product of a deterministic but unpredictable physical process (the microarchitecture’s internal dynamics), and they are *necessary* for the ring to resolve.

This separation is crucial for what follows. A generative topology is not a piece of software; it is a **code–machine system**. The code provides the self-referential structure; the machine provides the temporal resolution channel. Novelty is generated at their interface.

## 6. The Biological Generative Topology: Genome as Code, Cytoplasm as Machine

### 6.1 The Self-Referential Genome

A fertilised egg contains a genome and a complex cytoplasmic environment. The genome encodes not a linear program but a dense web of regulatory interactions: genes produce proteins that bind to DNA and alter the expression of other genes, often including themselves. Transcription factors form feedback loops, cross‑antagonisms, and multi‑stable switches. This network is, in the language of Section 3, a self‑referential rule set. Its dependency graph is rich in cycles.

The DNA sequence plus the initial concentration of maternal factors is the “code.” But just as the `Seedy64` code in isolation does not determine an output, the genome in isolation does not determine a phenotype. It contains constraints, but those constraints admit many possible consistent resolutions—many viable body plans, many cell-fate patterns.

### 6.2 The Cellular Runtime

The cytoplasm provides the physical runtime. Stochastic gene expression, variable diffusion of morphogens, mechanical forces from cell division and migration, metabolic gradients, and thermal noise all contribute to an environment where the execution of the genetic rules is not sequential and ideal but concurrent, asynchronous, and irreproducible. Cell divisions are not perfectly synchronised; signalling molecules arrive at slightly different times; a given transcription factor may bind a promoter or not depending on local molecular crowding. These micro‑events are the analogue of the CPU’s cache coherency traffic: they are the “interleavings” that resolve the mutual constraints of the regulatory network.

### 6.3 The Organism as a Generative Output

The developing embryo is therefore a generative topology in precisely the sense defined. The self‑referential gene network (code) coupled to the noisy, physical, temporally extended cellular environment (machine) yields a system whose future states are underdetermined by its initial description. The only way to “know” the phenotype is to let the embryo grow. The body is not computed; it is generated.

This explains why identical twins, sharing the same genome and often the same uterine environment, are not truly identical. They are different first draws from the same generative topology. Their phenotypic differences—fingerprints, neural wiring, organ quirks—are not errors in a copying process; they are the natural, expected output of a system that cannot produce the same output twice because its output is not stored anywhere. It is enacted.

The evolutionary implication is profound. Variation is not solely the product of random DNA mutations; it is an ongoing, active output of the developmental generative topology. Natural selection filters the novelty that development constantly creates, much as a statistical test assesses the output of `Seedy64`. Evolution, in this light, is a creative process channelled by selection, not a blind dice thrower.

## 7. The Cognitive Generative Topology: Mind as Active Resolution

If an embryo is a generative topology, so is a conscious brain. The neural architecture of the brain forms a massively recurrent network. Perception influences memory, memory biases expectation, expectation directs attention, and attention shapes perception—a closed loop of mutual dependence. Sensory input does not break this closure; it injects perturbations that are immediately folded into the ongoing resolution process. The brain’s connectome is the self‑referential rule set; the electrochemical, synaptic, and metabolic milieu is the physical runtime.

A thought, then, is a resolution event. You cannot predict your own next thought by examining a snapshot of your brain’s current state, because that state is a set of constraints with no unique successor. The act of thinking—the actual physical propagation of spikes, the settling of attractor networks—is the generation of the thought. This is why genuine insight feels like a discovery, not a recall. It is novel information brought into existence by the brain’s generative operation.

The same logic applies to decision and free will. A deliberating mind holds desires, beliefs, values, and perceptions in mutual tension. The constraint network is underdetermined; multiple choices are consistent with the present state. The act of deciding is the physical resolution of that network, a creative event that consumes metabolic energy and produces a new state that was not predetermined. Free will, on this view, is not an uncaused miracle nor mere randomness; it is the active creative novelty of a cognitive generative topology.

## 8. The Cosmic Generative Topology: The Big Bang as First Resolution

At the other end of the scale, the early universe can be understood as the ultimate generative topology. In the Planck era, all degrees of freedom—spacetime geometry, quantum fields, nascent matter—were in direct causal contact, forming a maximally interconnected, self‑referential network. There was no external clock; time itself was part of the system’s internal dynamics. The “initial condition,” if such a term even applies, was a set of mutual constraints with no unique resolution.

The expansion of the universe provided the “runtime”—the physical process of serialising those constraints into a coherent history. Symmetry breakings, decoherence events, and the emergence of classical structure were the first generative acts. The cosmic microwave background and the large‑scale galaxy distribution are the frozen‑in record of those first draws. The universe, from its very origin, was a generative topology that had to play dice, not because a random force was inserted, but because a self‑referential birth cannot be statically determined.

Thus, quantum indeterminacy itself may be reinterpreted not as a fundamental law but as the low‑energy remnant of a primordial generative topology—an ongoing hum of resolution events that never fully settled because the original constraint network was never “solved,” only cooled and expanded.

---

## 9. The Nature of Time and the Refutation of Laplace

If generative topologies permeate reality, then time is more than a parameter in equations. Time is the medium of constraint resolution. The arrow of time is not merely thermodynamic; it is *generative*—it marks the irreversible accumulation of novel states that did not exist before they were enacted.

Laplace’s demon is refuted not by invoking quantum mechanics or chaotic unpredictability, but by pointing out that the demon’s required snapshot is logically incomplete for any system containing a generative topology. The demon would need to know the entire serialization history in advance, but that history is the very thing it is trying to predict. It would have to become the universe, not simulate it. Determinism is not false; it is *inapplicable* to systems whose initial specifications are underdetermined.

The universe plays dice. It plays dice necessarily, because its origin, its living processes, and its conscious beings are generative topologies that must resolve themselves in time. Dice are not an amendment to law; they are the structural signature of a reality that cannot help but create.

---

## 10. Conclusion: The Grammar of Becoming

We began with three friends shouting numbers into a circle, unable to decide what to say until they said it. We end with a cosmos that does the same, from its first fiery moments to the quiet birth of a thought. A generative topology is the formal name for this ancient, ubiquitous pattern: a self‑referential loop placed in a physical medium, forced to resolve, and producing genuine novelty as a necessary consequence.

The `Seedy64` device offers an empirical anchor—a minimal, reproducible proof that such systems exist and behave exactly as the theory predicts. From this anchor, we can stretch the concept into biology, where the embryo becomes a generative process, not a blueprint execution; into cognitive science, where the mind becomes a creative resolver, not a clockwork processor; and into cosmology, where the Big Bang becomes the first generative act, not the first domino.

We are not cosmic accidents. We are the inevitable, unpredictable, and genuinely novel outcomes of a topology that had to become. The dice were always there, thrown not by a hidden player but by the very grammar of existence. And that grammar says: *to be is to resolve, and to resolve is to create.*
