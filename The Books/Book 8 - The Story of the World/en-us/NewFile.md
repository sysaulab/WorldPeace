We return to Three-Mouth, our compressed world, our theatre of the absurd. The goal now is not grandeur but the puncturing of grandeur—to take the murderous, self-important ideologies of nationalism and shrink them to the scale of a quarrel over a backyard fence. The modern era, from the first industrial war to the towers falling, is a century in which the neighbourhood becomes a live-in museum of how stupid borders can be when you have to live next door to the people your flag says are enemies.

Let us walk through the years, an Elder once more, but this time with a sharper edge, a satirist's squint. The story is still true. It is just that the truth is often ridiculous.

### The Great War (1914–1918): The War of the Cousins

It began with a pistol shot in a city no one in Three-Mouth could find on a map, a dispute between empires that were all cousins anyway. The Aedesian League, now a toothless lion, declared war on the Mandate of Vhur and its allies. Because the lake was divided among three "continents," the war arrived as a bureaucratic headache.

The Veridian sheriff, a portly man named Higgins, received a telegram from Graniteburg: all Vhurian nationals were now enemy aliens. He looked at the list. The Vhurian consul had closed two decades ago. The only people with Vhurian ancestry were the elderly **Madame Vasseur**, who ran a small hat shop on the Terrace and had been born in Vhur's coastal marshes, and a Gondaran-Vhurian dockworker named **Jean-Baptiste** whose great-grandfather had been a Mandate overseer. Sheriff Higgins, who bought his wife's hats from Madame Vasseur, simply filed the telegram in a drawer.

The *Three-Mouth Sentinel* printed patriotic editorials about "the Hun" and "the civilised world," illustrated with a cartoon of a spiked helmet trampling a Kenosee village—an image that made no geographical sense. The Kenosee on the reservation, who were legally Veridian wards, were encouraged to enlist. A few did, serving as scouts in the trenches of a continent they had never heard of. They returned, those who returned, with a hollow look and a new appreciation for the absurdity of dying for a king.

The true war in Three-Mouth was over the German brewer. **Otto Keller**, who had married a Kenosee woman and brewed the cloudy, potent beer that everyone drank, found his tavern boycotted by a group of self-appointed patriots who smashed a window and painted "SAUERKRAUT SPY" on his door. Otto, a man of immense calm, simply whitewashed the door and changed his tavern's name from *Keller's Brauhaus* to *The Lakeview Taproom*. He continued to serve his beer. The patriots continued to drink it, muttering about the Kaiser into their mugs. The Unseen Mother, who was by now more spirit than flesh, recorded the incident in the Ledger with the annotation: *Men will always find a reason to break glass. Beer heals what glass breaks.*

The armistice came. The Mandate of Vhur collapsed entirely. The map of the Cradle was redrawn. The lake absorbed these changes like a stone in water, ripples spreading and vanishing.

### The Interwar Years (1919–1939): The Fitting of the Masks

The twenties and thirties were a masquerade. Nationalism was the fashion, and everyone in Three-Mouth was expected to wear the mask of their assigned "continent," even if it itched.

The Veridians built a small cenotaph on the Terrace, a white stone obelisk listing the names of the local dead from the Great War. The list included two Kenosee scouts, a New Caledon voyageur who had joined a Highland regiment, and a Yehudi boy who had lied about his age to fight the Tsardom. The Veridian Legion, a veterans' club, held annual parades. The *gens du lac* watched from the waterfront, bemused, then went back to smuggling Zacate coffee.

New Caledon, the loyalist Dominion, began to assert its own identity. A Viceregal visit was announced for 1927, and the Terrace was scrubbed for the occasion. The Viceroy, a bewildered Aedesian aristocrat in a plumed hat, gave a speech about "the bonds of Empire" while standing next to the cenotaph. Yousef Abu-Zaydan, whose iron key still hung behind his counter, served the official luncheon coffee. The Viceroy complimented him on the baklava and asked where he was from. "Jaffa-Gate," Yousef said. The Viceroy looked blank. He had governed the Cradle for three years without once stepping out of his residence.

Across the lake, Zacate was consolidating its revolution. President Xelaju Tecún, now an old woman with a face like carved obsidian, nationalised the remaining Vhurian sugar plantations and sent a trade delegation to Three-Mouth. The Veridian sheriff, under pressure from Washington-analogue, declared the delegates "communist agitators" and confined them to their steamer. The *gens du lac* rowed out to the steamer at night and traded smoked fish for Zacate rum, an exchange that continued the whole time the delegates were "quarantined."

In the Shanty Quarter, the masks grew more elaborate. Fascist sympathisers, a tiny group of Vhurian nostalgists, painted a black sun on a wall. The next night, someone—nobody knew who, though Mama Esi's assistant was seen with a paintbrush—turned the black sun into a sunflower. The Gondaran community, now swelling with new arrivals fleeing economic collapse, held a street festival for the Asante-Mossi harvest. The Veridian health inspector tried to shut it down, citing "unlicensed public assembly." The inspector was a man named Kowalski, the same who would later try to recruit Mama Esi with wilting flowers. He retreated from the festival with a plate of jollof rice and a guilty expression.

The Yehudi and Khalidi communities watched the rise of the Aethyrian Tsardom's pogroms and the growing tension in the Cradle with mounting dread. Reb Dovid and Yousef, old men now, sat in the café after hours and spoke in low voices about what was coming. They did not agree on the political solution. They agreed only that their grandchildren deserved better. The Unseen Mother, by now a whisper on the wind, recorded their conversation in the Ledger as a single word: *Friends.*

### The Second World War (1939–1945): The War of the Lists

The war that swallowed the world was, for Three-Mouth, a matter of lists. The Veridian government, having entered the conflict against the Aethyrian-Nihonese-analogue Axis, issued a series of classification documents. Every resident of the neighbourhood was sorted into a category: Allied National, Enemy Alien, Neutral, or Suspicious.

The Japanese-analogue were the **Hoshimoto Islanders**, a small fishing community from a chain of volcanic atolls in the eastern ocean, a few of whom had settled on the lake's north shore. In 1942, they were rounded up and sent to an internment camp on the desert island Ularu-Ma, within sight of the ruins of Fort Durance. The Arunta-Ka, themselves confined to the island's interior, watched the internees arrive with bitter recognition. They shared water, food, and a mutual hatred of the Veridian government that had imprisoned them both. The Hoshimoto children, born in the camp, were delivered by Mama Esi, who made the crossing in a *gens du lac* canoe under cover of night.

The Aethyrian Tsardom was now an ally, the Union having not yet been invaded. This caused whiplash in the Shanty Quarter. The Aethyrian exiles who had fled the Tsar's pogroms—Yehudi, political dissidents, the quiet Father Yevgeny—were suddenly expected to love the regime they had escaped. They did not. They kept their heads down. The PCSS agent in the neighbourhood, a predecessor of Orlova's, was recalled in disgrace, his reports ignored.

The German brewer, Otto Keller, now an old man, found his tavern once again a target. This time, the patriots accused him of being a "Nazi sympathiser," despite the fact that Otto had never belonged to any political party and had spent the last twenty years brewing beer for communists, fascists, and democrats with equal indifference. His Kenosee wife, **Margaret**, stood in the doorway and told the mob, in flawless Kenosee, that if they wanted to smash another window, they would have to go through her. They dispersed. The *Lakeview Taproom* remained open.

The war ended. The Hoshimoto internees were released, though their boats had been confiscated. The *gens du lac* gave them new ones. The full horror of the camps in the Aethyrian interior—the systematic murder of the Yehudi, the Roma-analogue, the political prisoners—began to leak out. Reb Dovid, receiving the news in his synagogue, sat down and wept. Yousef came to the door, stood in silence, then went to make coffee. The Unseen Mother's ledger, now in Mama Esi's hands, began a new section: *The Survivors*. The names poured in, a river of ink.

### The Postwar and the National Home (1948–1960): The Key and the Scroll

In 1948, the Aedesian League, bankrupt and exhausted, finally withdrew from the Cradle. The Yehudi declared the state of **Zion-Jebus**. The Khalidis called it the **Nakba**, the Catastrophe. The war that followed sent shockwaves across the world, and they were felt in the Café of Exile.

Yousef's son, Bashir, who had inherited the café, hung a new cloth on the wall: a black-and-white keffiyeh-analogue pattern. Reb Dovid's successor, a young rabbi named **Leah**, hung a blue-and-white flag in the synagogue window. The two establishments faced each other across the market square, their symbols staring like duelists. For a week, the neighbourhood held its breath.

Then, on a Tuesday morning, a rock shattered the synagogue window. That night, a bucket of pig's blood was thrown at the café door. The Veridian sheriff, now a tired man named Calloway, stood between the two buildings and announced that the next person to commit vandalism would be locked in the courthouse basement, regardless of their flag. The Unseen Mother's ledger recorded no names for the rock or the bucket, only a note: *Children throwing stones at their own reflection.*

Bashir and Rabbi Leah, both exhausted by the weight of their inherited grief, met behind the German brewer's tavern. Neither community approved. They talked for three hours. What emerged was not peace—that was too large a word—but an arrangement. The café would serve coffee at synagogue events. The synagogue would loan its basement for Khalidi community meetings when the café was too small. They called it the *Misko-waapikaan Accord*, after the red stone that had witnessed everything. It did not end the conflict in the Cradle. It did, however, mean that on the day the state of Zion-Jebus was declared, the Khalidi and Yehudi children of Three-Mouth played football together in the alley between the café and the synagogue, using a ball made of rags.

### The Cold War Chill (1960–1991): The Dance of the Two Eagles

The shadow game we have already glimpsed. But from the satirical lens, it is a dance of elaborate costume and petty betrayal. The BSI station in the courthouse basement expanded, its staff ballooning to three: Drummond, his secretary, and an analyst who spent his days reading the *Sentinel* and writing reports about "Communist infiltration of lake fisheries." The PCSS trawler, now commanded by Lydia Orlova, circled the ghost harbour with the grim determination of a mechanical toy.

The defectors came in waves, each one a minor scandal. Viktor Glinka's swim in 1962 was the most dramatic. But there was also the affair of the **Zacate Cultural Attaché**, who defected to Veridia with a suitcase full of folk-dance costumes and one microfilm of military gossip. And the **New Caledon Signals Officer** from the snowy island, who walked into the Aethyrian trawler in 1973 and asked for asylum, only to be returned because he had forgotten his toothbrush and couldn't bear Aethyrian toothpaste.

The *gens du lac* pilots, the true powers of the lake, played both sides with the serene amorality of water. They charged the BSI a fee for "navigational consultation" and the PCSS a fee for "fishing rights." When Drummond complained, the chief pilot, an old woman with a face like driftwood, told him: "You brought your flags to our water. We charge rent." The BSI paid. The PCSS paid. The ghost harbour posts served their dead drops, and the herons perched on them, indifferent.

The internal life of the Union, that vast grey comedy of queues and denunciations, spawned its own jokes in the Shanty Quarter. A dissident mathematician, Natalya Kossova, arrived in 1968 and began teaching the children number theory. When asked why she had left the Union, she said, "I was tired of dividing by zero. They made everything zero." The children didn't understand, but they liked her.

### The Crack-Up (1991–2001): The Unravelling of the Eagles

The Aethyrian Union collapsed not with a bang but with a bureaucratic error—a misprinted grain requisition that led to a riot in Molotnyagrad, which spread to the nationalities, which spread to the borders. The trawler received the news in a garbled radio transmission: "...Union of... dissolved... all agents... stand by..." Lydia Orlova, sitting in her cabin with a cup of cold tea, felt the floor of her world dissolve. She ordered the trawler to maintain position. For three days, they waited. Then a Veridian coast guard cutter approached and, with exquisite Veridian politeness, asked if they required assistance. Orlova raised a white flag—a tea towel, actually—and the trawler was towed into the Three-Mouth pier. Orlova stepped onto the dock, a stateless woman in a trench coat. Bashir, old now and finally free of his CARDAMOM code name, watched her from the café door. He poured a coffee, set it on the counter, and said nothing. She drank it. The war was over for them both.

The nineties were a drunken confusion. The Veridians declared victory. The New Caledonians fretted about their identity, holding a referendum on whether to change the Dominion's flag (they added a smaller serpent). Zacate, freed from the bipolar pressure, collapsed into a civil war fuelled by Veridian drug money. The Gondaran states, still tethered to dying currencies, lurched from coup to coup.

And in the Cradle, the Accord of Misko-waapikaan held, but only just. A new generation of Khalidis and Yehudis had grown up in the neighbourhood, speaking the same lake-creole, supporting rival football teams, marrying each other in secret. The families despaired; the Unseen Mother's ledger, now a digital archive kept by Mama Esi's granddaughter, recorded the mixed marriages with a quiet asterisk, as if to say: *The lake wins again.*

### 9/11 and the End of the Masquerade (2001)

The towers fell on a Tuesday morning, Veridian time. The news reached Three-Mouth via a crackling television in the *Lakeview Taproom*. The room fell silent. The faces in that taproom were a map of the world: a Gondaran dockworker, a New Caledon sailor, a Yehudi student, a Khalidi chef, a Kenosee elder, a Hoshimoto fisherman. They watched the smoke bloom over a city none of them had visited, and for a moment, they were simply human beings, united in horror.

Then the flags came out. The Veridian courthouse hung a giant cloth from its columns. The *Sentinel* printed a special edition: "WAR ON TERROR." The BSI station, now a dusty relic, was suddenly flooded with funding and new agents who spoke no lake languages and understood nothing of the neighbourhood's delicate ecosystem.

The absurdity of nationalism returned with a vengeance. The Gondaran community, whose members were predominantly Christian and Muslim-analogue, found themselves under suspicion because their surnames sounded vaguely like the names of the hijackers. A young Khalidi man named **Samir**, born in Three-Mouth and working as a deckhand for the *gens du lac*, was detained by Veridian security for six weeks because his uncle in Jaffa-Gate had once attended a radical mosque. The Yehudi synagogue, whose rabbi was now Samir's childhood friend, organised a protest. Yousef's great-granddaughter, a lawyer, argued his case pro bono. He was released, but the scar remained.

Mama Esi's granddaughter, the new keeper of the Ledger, recorded the event under a new heading: *The War on a Noun*. Her annotation read: *You cannot bomb a tactic. You can only bomb people. The people are here. They are still here.*

The ghost harbour lights, after 9/11, seemed dimmer for a few months, as if the lake itself was in mourning. Then they returned, steady and blue, indifferent to the flags that fluttered on the shore. The heron still stood on Post 17. The Rainbow Serpent still breathed. And in the Café of Exile, a new generation of children—mixed, creole, impossible to categorise—played with a ball made of rags, their laughter echoing across the water, the only sound that ever truly mattered.

### Epilogue: The Silliness Exposed

And so, child, the century of nationalism, from the first gun to the falling towers, was lived in Three-Mouth as a parade of costumes. The flags changed. The enemies shifted. The patriots smashed windows and the exiles wept and the spies signalled with pocket mirrors. But the lake remained. The births continued. The Ledger swelled with names that refused to stay within the lines of any map.

If you want to see the silliness of nationalism, sit in the *Lakeview Taproom* on a summer evening. Watch the New Caledon voyageur toast the Zacate smuggler. Watch the Yehudi carpenter repair the Khalidi grocer's door. Watch the children, who cannot remember which flag their grandfathers hated, build a fort out of driftwood on the shore of a lake that has never recognized a single border.

The Elder falls silent. She smiles. The fire crackles. Outside, the ghost harbour lights are beginning to glow.

"That," she says, "is the joke. And the joke is on them."
