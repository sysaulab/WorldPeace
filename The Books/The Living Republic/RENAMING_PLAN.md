# Renaming Strategy for *The Living Republic*

## 1. Diagnosis

The manuscript has three kinds of name collisions:

| Type | Example | Problem |
|------|---------|---------|
| **Intentional lineage** | Miriam Vance → Katherine Vance → Ellie Vance | Works, but needs clear generation markers. |
| **Accidental same surname** | Maya Chen, Howard Chen, Harold Chen, Margaret Chen, Chen Wei, David Chen | Confuses readers; breaks immersion. |
| **Accidental same first name** | Maya (2030), Maya (2100), Maya (2485), Maya (3472); Amara (Last Mile), Amara Diallo (2468), Amara (2100), Amara Okafor (2472), General Amara Okafor | Readers lose track of who is who across timelines. |

The core problem is **timeline bleed**: because the novel spans 1,500 years, identical names appear in different eras without sufficient differentiation.

---

## 2. Strategy

**Principle 1 — Anchor names.**  
Keep one iconic name per lineage. Do not rename the central figure of a timeline unless absolutely necessary.

**Principle 2 — Generation markers for intentional descendants.**  
When a name is reused deliberately (e.g., Maya Chen named after her ancestor), add a generation suffix: *Maya Chen I*, *Maya Chen II*, *Maya Chen III*. In prose, use “the Founder,” “the Inheritor,” “the Child of the Millennium.”

**Principle 3 — Rename accidental collisions.**  
If two unrelated characters share a first or last name, rename one. Choose names that fit the character’s cultural/ethnic context and the era.

**Principle 4 — Master registry.**  
Create a single table of all named characters with: full name, era, first appearance, unique identifier. Use it for all future edits.

**Principle 5 — Search-and-replace with era filters.**  
Because the manuscript has duplicate section titles and interleaved timelines, never do a global replace. Always check the date/era at the top of the section before replacing.

## 5. Implementation Notes

1. **Do not global replace.** The manuscript has duplicate section titles and interleaved timelines. Always check the date/era at the top of the section before renaming.
2. **Create a master character registry** (spreadsheet or document) with columns: Full Name, Era, First Appearance, Unique Identifier, Notes. Update it as you edit.
3. **Use generation markers in prose.** When two characters share a name across eras, introduce them with their generation marker on first appearance in each section: “Maya Chen II, the Inheritor, stood in the garden…”
4. **Check for unintended new collisions.** After renaming, search for the new names to ensure they don’t collide with existing ones (e.g., “Lin” might appear elsewhere).
5. **Consider a style sheet.** Decide whether to use “Maya Chen (2030)” in section headings or “Maya Chen I” in prose. Consistency is key.
6. **Resolve structural duplicates first.** The manuscript currently has duplicate section titles (e.g., “The Numbers” appears in both Part Two and Part Three). Fix the structure before applying renames, or you will rename the same text twice.
