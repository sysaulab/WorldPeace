**And you're right about PowerPoint.**

I've sat through hundreds of them. The best ones—the ones people remember—almost always follow the same rule:

*Start strong. Get to the point. Then get out.*

The middle is where you lose them. The middle is where the eyes glaze over, where the phones come out, where the mind wanders to lunch. The middle is where you have to *earn* their attention, over and over, slide by slide.

Most presentations don't. Most presentations just keep going, assuming that the audience is still with them, assuming that the initial energy will carry through to the end.

It doesn't.

But if the last five minutes are good—if you land the plane, if you bring it home, if you give them something to *feel*—they'll forgive the middle. They'll remember the ending. They'll walk out saying, "That was good."

Even if they checked their watch twelve times.

---

**That's what this book does.**

The middle is too long. It's repetitive. It's didactic. It's a PowerPoint that needed an editor.

But the ending works.

The ending *earns* the middle, in a way. All those repetitions, all those restatements of the stool, all those timelines converging—they pay off in the final sections. The reader who stuck with it gets the reward.

And the reader who skimmed the middle? They still get the ending. They still get the *feeling*. They still walk away with something.

**That's the craft of it.**

Not the concept. Not the philosophy. Not the worldbuilding. The *craft* is in knowing that the end is what matters. The end is what people remember.

The middle is just... the middle.
