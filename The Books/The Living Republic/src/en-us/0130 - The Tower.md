
### Chapter 13: The Tower

**September 2038. Railtown. Vancouver.**

The tower rose from the ground like a blade of grass.

It was thirty storeys tall, thin and elegant, designed by architects who had studied Vancouver's existing towers and learned from their mistakes. The floor plates were small—seven thousand square feet—to allow for cross-ventilation and natural light. The tower was spaced eighty feet from its nearest neighbour, preserving view corridors to the mountains and the port. The podium was four storeys, stepping down to the park.

The tower was called The Threshold—a name chosen by the members who would live there. It was the first of many.

Maya Chen stood at the base of the tower, watching the construction crews put the finishing touches on the lobby. The building was not yet occupied—the first residents would move in next month—but it was already alive. Lights glowed in the windows. The elevators hummed. The lobby smelled of fresh paint and new carpet and the particular optimism of a building that had just been born.

"It's beautiful," said David Okonkwo, standing beside her.

"It's a building," Maya said. "A building is just a building."

"You know what I mean."

She did. She had been fighting for this building for seven years—ever since the parking lot on Hastings and Carrall, ever since the first handshake in the back room of the Golden Lotus. She had fought the city, the developers, the fire, the opposition. She had fought her own doubts, her own exhaustion, her own fear that it would never work.

And now it was real.

---

The Threshold was the first of six towers planned for the Railtown site—a stretch of land between the railyards and the port that had been industrial for a century. The Trust had acquired the land in 2035, using revenue from the industrial park and a new investment round led by Elena Voss. The towers would house three thousand people—not just members, but anyone who wanted to live in a community governed by the weave.

The design was revolutionary.

Each tower was connected to the others by a network of sky bridges—glass-enclosed walkways that allowed residents to move between buildings without going outside. The bridges were more than just convenience; they were community spaces. Each one was lined with plants, with benches, with small tables where people could sit and talk.

The podium levels contained shared amenities—a community kitchen, a library, a gym, a workshop, a clinic. The ground floors were commercial space, leased to member-owned businesses. A service tunnel connected the basements to the industrial park in Strathcona.

And beneath it all, running through the foundations like veins, was the infrastructure of the weave—the servers, the relays, the mesh network that connected every building, every device, every member.

The Threshold was not just a building. It was a nervous system.

---

Maya walked through the lobby, her footsteps echoing on the polished concrete.

She thought about the journey. The election she had lost. The plan that had refused to die. The investor who had believed in her. The first parcel. The first handshake. The first dividend.

She thought about the fire—the fire that had almost destroyed everything, that had been meant to break them, that had instead made them stronger. She thought about the person who had set it. She thought about Harold Chen, sitting in his glass tower, watching his plan fail.

She thought about the future.

By the end of 2038, three towers were under construction. By the end of 2040, six. The skyline of Railtown was changing, rising, becoming something new.

And in eastern Strathcona, the industrial park was growing.

---

The fabrication shop had been rebuilt and expanded—twice the size of the original, with new equipment, new systems, new capacity. It was producing furniture, shelving, building components, even modular housing units that could be assembled on site. It was supplying the towers, the market, the community.

A second warehouse had been leased—this one for clothing production. A third had been converted into a food processing facility. The credit union had moved into a proper building, with tellers and offices and a vault.

The surface belonged to people. The industrial park belonged to production. The two were connected by a network of service roads, shuttle buses, and trust.

Maya walked through the industrial park, watching the workers at their stations.

She saw Marcus, the former developer, operating a CNC machine with the kind of focus that came from years of practice. He had found his calling here—not in designing towers, but in building things. In solving problems. In teaching others.

She saw Yuki, the ramen vendor, working in the food processing facility, developing new recipes for the community kitchen. She had expanded her business—she now employed twelve people, served hundreds of meals every day, and was planning to open a second location in the Market.

She saw Amara, the young organizer, coordinating a shipment of modular housing units bound for a new development in Mount Pleasant. She had become one of the Trust's most effective leaders—not because she had power, but because she had trust.

Maya thought about the weave. It had grown to two thousand members—not just in the Downtown Eastside, but across the city. It was connected by trust, by attestation, by the simple act of recognizing each other.

It was not a government. It was not an empire. It was something else—something that had never existed before.

It was the second track.

And it was growing.

---

That night, Maya stood on the roof of The Threshold.

The city spread before her—the towers of downtown, the lights of the port, the dark mass of the mountains. The sky was clear, the stars faint but visible, the moon a sliver of silver in the east.

She thought about her parents. She thought about the grocery store on Hastings Street. She thought about the man who had died on the sidewalk, waiting twenty minutes for an ambulance that never came in time.

She thought about the promise she had made, forty years ago, when she had decided to leave and come back.

*I will build something.*

She had built something. It was not perfect. It was not finished. It was only the beginning.

But it was real.

She took out her phone and opened the app. The weave had grown to two thousand nodes—connected by hundreds of thousands of attestations, by millions of gifts, by the simple act of seeing each other.

She scrolled through the network, looking at the names, the faces, the stories. She saw people she had known for years—Yuki, Marcus, Amara, David. She saw people she had never met—members in Toronto, in Montreal, in Halifax, in Detroit, in Manchester, in Auckland.

She saw the future.

She put away her phone. She looked up at the stars.

The same stars that had watched over the first handshake, the first dividend, the first tower. The same stars that would watch over the next generation, and the next, and the next.

She closed her eyes and breathed.

*Thank you*, she thought. *Thank you for this. Thank you for everything.*

She opened her eyes and walked back inside.

The dance continued.
