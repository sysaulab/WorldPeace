
### The Numbers

**March 2060. The Downtown Eastside. Vancouver.**

The numbers came in at midnight.

Maya Chen sat in her apartment in The Threshold, watching the data stream across her tablet. She was eighty-two years old now—her hair white, her face lined with the years of struggle and triumph. But her eyes were still bright, still curious, still full of the wonder that had driven her since the day she had decided to run for mayor and lost.

The numbers were staggering.

The community now numbered six thousand members—not just in the Downtown Eastside, but in affiliated communities across Vancouver, across Canada, across the world. The Trust owned three thousand five hundred housing units, one hundred commercial spaces, eight industrial buildings, three farms, and a portfolio of investments that generated sixty million dollars in annual revenue.

The loans had been repaid. The ten million dollars from Elena Voss had been returned with interest. The credit union had become a chartered bank, with access to the Bank of Canada's settlement system. The industrial park in Strathcona had expanded into a network of fabrication hubs, connected by service roads and logistics corridors.

The economic engine was running at full steam.

And the surplus—the money left over after all expenses, all reinvestments, all reserves—was distributed to the members.

---

Maya set down her tablet and walked to the window.

The city spread before her—the towers of Railtown, the lights of the port, the dark mass of the mountains. The Market glowed in the distance. The industrial park hummed in Strathcona. The weave was alive.

She thought about the journey. The election she had lost. The plan that had refused to die. The investor who had believed in her. The first parcel. The first handshake. The first dividend. The fire. The tower. The Market. The Andon Cord. The spillover. The second track.

She thought about the people who had helped her—David, Yuki, Marcus, Amara, and all the others. Some were still alive. Some had died. All had contributed. All had been seen.

She thought about the future.

---

The quarterly assembly was held the next evening.

It was the largest assembly in the community's history—over two thousand members gathered in the Great Hall, with thousands more participating remotely through the Agora. The agenda was full: reports from the Trust board, proposals for new developments, discussions about the community's response to the refugee crisis in Europe.

But the main item was the dividend.

The surplus for the quarter was twelve million dollars. After reinvestment and reserves, eight million was available for distribution. Divided equally among six thousand members, that came to approximately thirteen hundred dollars per member—six hundred and fifty in base money, six hundred and fifty in reciprocal money.

It was the largest dividend in the community's history.

Maya stood at the front of the Great Hall, watching the vote. The proposal passed with 94% support—the highest margin ever recorded.

She thought about the first dividend, thirty years ago. Twenty-five dollars per week. Enough for a phone card, a bus ticket, a cup of coffee. The members had celebrated as if they had won the lottery.

Now they were distributing thirteen hundred dollars per quarter. And no one was celebrating. It had become normal. It had become expected. It had become the baseline.

That was the point.

---

After the assembly, Maya walked through the Market.

It was late—most of the stalls were closed, the lights dimmed, the chairs stacked on the tables. But the Lounge was still open, and she found David Okonkwo sitting by the window, a cup of tea in his hand.

He was eighty-five now—older than her, frailer, his hair gone, his hands trembling slightly. But his eyes were still sharp, still kind, still full of the calm that had carried her through the darkest moments.

"You look tired," he said.

"I am tired."

"Sit down. Have some tea."

She sat. A server appeared, poured her a cup of oolong, disappeared. Maya wrapped her hands around the warm ceramic and looked out the window at the park.

"The numbers are good," she said.

"The numbers are excellent."

"When I started this—when I lost the election—I never imagined it would be this big. I thought maybe a few buildings. A few hundred people. A small community that could take care of itself."

"And now?"

"Now it's a city. A network. A movement. It's more than I ever dreamed."

David nodded slowly.

"That's the thing about seeds," he said. "You never know how big they'll grow. You just plant them and hope."

Maya was silent for a moment. Then she said: "Do you think we did it? Do you think we built something that will last?"

David looked at her.

"I think we built something that will outlast us. I think we built something that will outlast our children. I think we built something that will outlast the city, the country, the world as we know it."

He paused.

"But I don't think it's finished. It's never finished. That's the point."

Maya nodded. She thought about the Andon Cord—the mechanism that allowed the community to pause, to reflect, to reconsider. She thought about the Consent Layer—the system that allowed laws to be enacted and voided continuously, as the community's consent shifted. She thought about the weave—the network of trust that connected every member to every other.

The system was designed to adapt. To learn. To grow. It was never finished. It was always becoming.

That was the point.

---

That night, Maya sat alone in her apartment.

She thought about the numbers. The six thousand members. The three thousand five hundred housing units. The sixty million dollars in annual revenue. The eight million dollars in dividends.

She thought about the people behind the numbers. The members who had been homeless, who had been hopeless, who had been forgotten. The members who had found a home, a purpose, a community. The members who had been seen.

She thought about the future. The children who would grow up in this community. The generations who would inherit what she had built. The world that was still waiting to be born.

She took out her phone and opened the app. The weave had grown to six thousand nodes—connected by hundreds of thousands of attestations, by millions of gifts, by the simple act of seeing each other.

She scrolled through the network, looking at the names, the faces, the stories. She saw people she had known for years. She saw people she had never met. She saw the future.

She put away her phone. She looked out the window at the city.

The towers of Railtown rose in the distance. The industrial park hummed in Strathcona. The Market glowed in the darkness.

And the dance continued.
