
### The Opposition

**June 2034. The Terminal Club. Vancouver.**

The Terminal Club occupied the top floor of a glass tower in Coal Harbour, forty-two storeys above the water. It was not a public place. It had no sign, no listing, no website. Its members were admitted by invitation only, and the invitations were never written down.

The room was designed to make men feel powerful. Floor-to-ceiling windows framed the mountains and the harbour. The furniture was leather and dark wood. The lighting was low and warm. A single bartender—discreet, silent, forgettable—stood behind a counter of polished granite.

Nine men and one woman sat around a table near the window. They were the people who owned Vancouver—not officially, not legally, but in the way that mattered. They owned the land. They owned the buildings. They owned the politicians who approved the permits and the planners who wrote the bylaws and the journalists who decided which stories got told.

They had been meeting like this for twenty years. They had never needed to meet about the Downtown Eastside before, because the Downtown Eastside had never been worth meeting about. It was a wound in the city's side, a place where the poor died quietly and the rich never went. It was useful, in its way—a reservoir of cheap labour, a dumping ground for the problems no one wanted to solve, a cautionary tale that kept the middle class grateful for what they had.

But now the wound was healing. And that was a problem.

Harold Chen called the meeting to order.

He was a developer—the largest residential developer in the city, the man who had built Yaletown and Coal Harbour and half the glass towers that now lined the water. He was sixty-two years old, silver-haired, immaculately dressed, with the kind of smooth, practiced smile that had closed a thousand deals. He had donated to every political party, every charity, every cultural institution that mattered. He was, by every public measure, a philanthropist and a civic leader.

He was also, by every private measure, a predator.

"Thank you all for coming," he said. "I know you're busy. So I'll be brief."

He gestured to a screen on the wall. A map of the Downtown Eastside appeared—not the neighbourhood as it was, but the neighbourhood as it had been drawn in the city's official development plan. A patchwork of coloured zones, each one representing a parcel of land, a potential site, a future profit.

"Two years ago, this was the most valuable undeveloped land in North America," Chen said. "Four hundred acres of prime real estate, five minutes from downtown, with water views on three sides. The city had approved upzoning. The province had committed infrastructure funding. We were going to build the biggest mixed-use development this city has ever seen. Condos, offices, hotels, retail. A new neighbourhood for forty thousand people."

He paused.

"And then Maya Chen and her friends started buying up parcels."

A murmur ran through the room. Everyone knew about the Last Mile Project. Everyone had been watching it with varying degrees of irritation and alarm. But no one had yet done anything about it.

"In the past eighteen months, the Last Mile Trust has acquired fourteen parcels in the Downtown Eastside," Chen continued. "Fourteen parcels that were supposed to be ours. They've built one building—a small one, only forty units—and they're about to break ground on a second. They've leased warehouse space in Strathcona. They've opened a credit union. They've started distributing what they call a 'dividend' to their members."

He looked around the table.

"And the worst part? It's working."

---

The woman at the end of the table spoke first.

Her name was Diane Wu. She was the president of the Vancouver Landlord Association, a quiet, sharp-eyed woman in her fifties who owned six thousand rental units across the city. She had built her empire one building at a time, buying low, renting high, evicting when necessary. She was not sentimental about her tenants. She was not sentimental about anything.

"Working?" she said. "It's forty units. That's nothing. We own more than that in a single block."

"It's not the units," Chen said. "It's the model. They're not just building housing. They're building a parallel economy. A parallel government. They're collecting revenue, distributing resources, resolving disputes. They're doing things that we do—except they're doing them without us."

"So what? They're a charity. They'll run out of money."

"They're not a charity. They're a trust. They're capitalized. They've got an investor—some woman from Singapore, we think, though we haven't been able to confirm it—and they're generating revenue from their own businesses. They don't need donations. They don't need grants. They don't need us."

He tapped the screen. A new image appeared—a photograph of the six-storey building on Hastings and Carrall. It looked modest, almost insignificant against the glass towers of downtown. But there was something about it that made Chen's jaw tighten.

"They call this building The Threshold," he said. "It's the first one. They're planning more. They've got a master plan—a blueprint for the entire neighbourhood. Parcel by parcel, building by building. They're going to take the whole thing."

"And if they do?" Wu asked.

"Then we lose four hundred acres of prime real estate. We lose the upzoning profit—which, at current market rates, would have been somewhere in the range of twelve billion dollars. We lose the political influence that comes from being the city's largest developers. And we lose something else—something harder to measure."

He looked around the room.

"We lose the narrative."

---

The man who spoke next was a city councillor named Robert Tanaka.

He was sixty-five, in his fourth term, and he had never lost an election. He had done it by being careful—by never taking a position that could cost him votes, by never making an enemy he didn't have to, by always saying exactly what the people in the room wanted to hear. He was a weathervane, not a leader. But weathervanes were useful when you needed to know which way the wind was blowing.

"What do you mean, the narrative?" he asked.

"I mean the story," Chen said. "The story that Vancouver is a great city. The story that the market works. The story that if you work hard and play by the rules, you'll be rewarded. The story that the poor are poor because of their own choices, and the rich are rich because of their own merit."

He paused.

"The Last Mile is a counter-story. It says that the market doesn't work—that it extracts rather than produces, that it concentrates rather than distributes, that it rewards ownership rather than contribution. It says that the poor are poor because the system is designed to keep them poor. It says that another world is possible."

He looked at Tanaka.

"If people start believing that story, we're finished. Not just in the Downtown Eastside. Everywhere. People will start asking why they're paying rent to landlords instead of owning their own housing. Why they're paying fees to platforms instead of owning their own data. Why they're paying interest to banks instead of owning their own money."

"So what do we do?" Tanaka asked.

"We discredit them. We find something—anything—that we can use against them. A scandal, a failure, a mistake. Something that makes people doubt."

"And if we can't find anything?"

Chen smiled. It was not a pleasant smile.

"Then we manufacture it."

---

The plan took shape over the next three hours.

It was not a single plan but a series of overlapping campaigns, each designed to attack the Last Mile from a different angle.

The first campaign was financial. Diane Wu would instruct her property managers to begin buying up parcels adjacent to the Last Mile acquisitions—not to develop them, but to hold them, to drive up prices, to make expansion more expensive. She would also pressure her contacts at the major banks to restrict credit to the Trust and its affiliated businesses.

The second campaign was legal. Robert Tanaka would instruct the city's planning department to conduct a "routine audit" of all permits issued to the Last Mile Trust—a process that would tie them up in paperwork for months. He would also propose a new bylaw requiring all community housing providers to meet the same building standards as market-rate developments—a standard that would make the Trust's modular designs prohibitively expensive to build.

The third campaign was political. Chen would personally approach the mayor—Sarah Kim, who had so far stayed neutral on the Last Mile—and remind her of the political consequences of supporting the project. He would also reach out to the provincial government and suggest that the Trust's unusual governance model raised "regulatory concerns" that might require a formal review.

The fourth campaign was media. A friendly columnist would begin writing stories about the "gentrification" of the Downtown Eastside—how the Last Mile was driving up property values and forcing out long-term residents. A sympathetic non-profit would release a report questioning the Trust's governance model, raising concerns about "accountability" and "transparency." A social media campaign would begin spreading rumours about the Trust's finances.

And the fifth campaign—the one Chen kept to himself, the one he did not discuss with the others—was something else entirely.

---

After the meeting, Chen walked alone to the elevator.

He rode down in silence, watching the city lights pass the glass. Forty-two floors. Two hundred metres. A long way to fall.

He thought about the last campaign. The one he had not mentioned.

He had a contact. A man he had used before, years ago, when a rival developer had gotten too aggressive, too ambitious, too close to something Chen wanted. The man was expensive. He was careful. He was, above all, deniable.

Chen had never asked for details. He had simply paid the money and watched the problem solve itself.

Now he reached into his pocket and pulled out a phone—a cheap, prepaid model, unregistered, untraceable. He typed a single message:

*"I need you to look at someone."*

He typed the name: *Maya Chen.*

He added a second line: *"Start with her finances. Her personal life. Anything you can find."*

Then he pressed send.

The reply came within minutes.

*"Understood. Timeline?"*

Chen typed: *"Six months. Longer if necessary."*

*"Payment?"*

*"Usual terms. Half now, half on delivery."*

*"Accepted."*

Chen pocketed the phone. The elevator reached the lobby. The doors opened, and he stepped out into the marble expanse of the building's ground floor.

He walked past the security desk, past the art installations that had cost more than most people earned in a lifetime, past the doorman who tipped his hat and called him sir.

He stepped out into the night. The city rose around him—the towers he had built, the neighbourhoods he had shaped, the empire he had created.

He thought about Maya Chen. He thought about the Last Mile. He thought about the counter-story that was spreading through the neighbourhood like a virus.

He thought about the fire that would burn it all down.

And he smiled.
