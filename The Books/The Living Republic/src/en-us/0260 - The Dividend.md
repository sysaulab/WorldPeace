### The Dividend

**June 2060. The Agora. The Downtown Eastside.**

The old world had a name for what the Last Mile was doing.

It called it expropriation.

The rentiers—the landlords, the platform owners, the patent holders, the debt peddlers—screamed it from every rooftop, every editorial page, every shareholder meeting. *You're stealing what we earned. You're violating property rights. You're destroying the incentives that made civilization possible.*

They said it in courtrooms, where their lawyers argued that the Trust's acquisition of land at below-market prices constituted an illegal taking. They said it in legislatures, where their lobbyists pushed for laws that would retroactively criminalize community land trusts. They said it on television, where their commentators warned that the Last Mile was a virus—a contagion of collectivism that would spread from the Downtown Eastside to the whole city, the whole country, the whole world.

But they hadn't earned it. They had occupied it.

They had found the wells—the scarce things that everyone needed—and built fences around them. Land. Spectrum. Patents. Algorithms. Debt. Attention. They didn't produce. They extracted. They sat at the chokepoints of the economy and demanded tolls. They had captured the wells, and they had called it ownership.

The Last Mile was not expropriation. It was *deconstruction*.

It was taking apart the fences, filling in the wells, and letting the water flow to everyone.

---

Maya Chen sat in her apartment in The Threshold, reading the quarterly report.

She was eighty-two years old—her hair white, her face lined, her hands thin and spotted with age. But her eyes were still sharp, still curious, still full of the wonder that had driven her since the day she had decided to run for mayor and lost.

The report was titled *"The State of the Commons: 2060."* It had been prepared by the Trust's research team—a group of economists, ecologists, and data scientists who had been documenting the Last Mile's impact since the beginning. It was two hundred pages long, dense with charts and graphs and projections, and it told a story that no one in the old world had wanted to hear.

The story was simple: the deconstruction was working.

**Land.** The Trust owned three thousand five hundred housing units—every one of them permanently affordable, every one of them held in trust for the community. The land beneath them had been removed from the speculative market. It would never be sold. It would never be rented for profit. It would never be used as collateral for loans that would enrich distant shareholders. It was a commons, held in trust for the community, forever.

**Platforms.** The weave was not a platform. It was a protocol. No one owned it. No one could charge fees for it. No one could extract value from the connections between people. The identity layer was free. The governance layer was free. The Agora was free. The wells were filled in.

**Patents.** The Trust's innovations—the fabrication techniques, the modular housing designs, the reciprocal money protocol, the cognitive attestation system—were all open-source. Anyone could use them. Anyone could improve them. The knowledge was not a well. It was a river.

**Debt.** The credit union was not a bank. It did not charge interest. It did not foreclose. It did not extract. It pooled resources and lent them to members who needed them, at cost. The debt well was dry.

**Attention.** The Gift app did not sell ads. It did not track behavior. It did not manipulate. It was a tool for recognition, not a machine for extraction. The attention well was not a well at all.

The rentiers howled. They called it theft. They called it communism. They called it the end of civilization.

But the members didn't care. They had housing. They had food. They had healthcare. They had education. They had work that was meaningful. They had a community that saw them.

What did they need the rentiers for?

---

Maya set down the report and walked to the window.

The city spread before her—the towers of Railtown, the lights of the port, the dark mass of the mountains. The Market glowed in the distance. The industrial park hummed in Strathcona. The weave was alive.

She thought about the first dividend, thirty years ago. Twenty-five dollars per week. Enough for a phone card, a bus ticket, a cup of coffee. The members had celebrated as if they had won the lottery.

Now the dividend was one hundred dollars per week—five thousand two hundred dollars per year—split evenly between base money and reciprocal money. It wasn't a fortune. But it was a floor. It was the guarantee that no member would ever fall.

She thought about the philosophy behind the dividend. It was not charity. It was not welfare. It was not a handout. It was the community's way of saying: *The surplus belongs to all of us. We decide together who gets more.*

In the old world, the surplus went to the owners. The shareholders. The founders. The people who had already captured the wells. They decided who got rich. They decided who got poor. They decided who got to live and who got to die.

In the Last Mile, the surplus went to the members. All of them. Equally, in base money. And then, through the reciprocal money, unequally—but unequally by *their own choice*.

The base money was the floor. Housing, food, clothing, healthcare, education—those were already free. The base money covered the small personal things: a phone, a book, a trip to the city. It was the guarantee that no member would fall.

The reciprocal money was the ceiling. It was the luxury layer. It was for beauty, for recognition, for the things that made life worth living. And it was distributed not by the market, not by the owners, not by the algorithm—but by the members themselves.

When you gave someone reciprocal money, you were saying: *Your contribution matters. Your existence makes my life better.*

When you received it, you could spend it on something special. But more importantly, you knew you had been *seen*.

---

The recognition was the heart of the system.

Maya had learned this from the booklet—the thin volume that David had given her thirty years ago, when she had lost the election and thought her life was over. The booklet had taught her about the short loop, the open question, the fragile shield. It had taught her about the grammar of peace.

But it had also taught her something else: that worth was not measured by what you owned. It was measured by what you gave.

In the old world, worth was measured by accumulation. The more you owned, the more you were worth. The rentiers owned everything. So they were worth everything. The workers owned nothing. So they were worth nothing.

In the Last Mile, worth was measured by contribution. The more you gave, the more you were recognized. The more you were recognized, the more reciprocal money you received. The more reciprocal money you received, the more luxury you could enjoy.

It was not a perfect system. Some people were better at giving than others. Some people were more visible. Some people were more charismatic. Some people were lonelier, quieter, more invisible. The isolation signal existed precisely because the system could fail—and when it failed, the community needed to notice.

But it was a *better* system. It was a system where the person who helped the most was the person who got the most. Not the person who owned the most. Not the person who extracted the most. Not the person who captured the most wells.

The person who helped.

---

Maya walked to the Market for lunch.

It was a Tuesday afternoon—the same kind of grey, drizzly Tuesday that had marked so many of the milestones in her life. The first parcel. The first dividend. The fire. The Andon Cord. The spillover.

She ordered a bowl of ramen from Yuki's stall—Yuki was seventy-eight now, still working, still serving the same tonkotsu recipe her grandmother had brought from Fukuoka in 1972. The broth was still simmered for eighteen hours. The noodles were still made fresh every morning. The pork belly was still sliced thin and tender.

"Hey, Maya," Yuki said, sliding the bowl across the counter. "How's the book coming?"

Maya smiled. She had been writing a memoir—the story of the Last Mile Project, from the first election to the present day. It was slow going. There was so much to say.

"It's coming. Slowly."

"Take your time. The story isn't going anywhere."

Maya took her bowl to the Lounge and sat by the window. Outside, the park was full of people—members and visitors, young and old, rich and poor. The membrane was working. The community was alive.

She thought about the rentiers. About the wells. About the fences. About the tolls.

She thought about the deconstruction.

She thought about the surplus.

She thought about the decision.

And she thought about the gift.

*This is what it means to be a community*, she thought. *Not to own. To give. Not to extract. To contribute. Not to capture. To share.*

---

After lunch, Maya walked to the industrial park.

It had grown since the early days—twelve buildings now, eight hundred thousand square feet of production, storage, and logistics space. The fabrication shop had expanded three times. The food processing facility was supplying meals to fifteen thousand people every day. The clothing workshop was producing garments that were sold in the Market and exported to affiliated communities around the world.

She found Marcus in the fabrication shop, teaching a class on modular housing design.

Marcus had been a developer once—before the Last Mile. He had built luxury condos in Yaletown, had made a fortune, had owned a penthouse with a view of the water. He had been one of the rentiers. He had sat at the well and charged tolls.

Then the war had come. The economy had collapsed. His fortune had evaporated. His penthouse had been foreclosed. He had ended up in the DTES, in an SRO, with nothing but his skills and his regrets.

He had joined the Last Mile because he had nowhere else to go.

At first, he had been cynical. He had looked at the reciprocal money and seen a game. He had looked at the peer recognition and seen a popularity contest. He had looked at the Trust and seen a new set of owners.

But something had changed.

He had started working in the fabrication shop. He had discovered that he was good at it—not just at designing buildings, but at building things. At solving problems. At teaching others. He had started mentoring younger members, showing them how to use the machines, how to read blueprints, how to think like a builder.

And the reciprocal money had started flowing his way.

Not because he was rich. Not because he was powerful. Not because he owned anything. Because he had helped. Because people had noticed. Because they wanted to say: *Thank you. You made my life better.*

Marcus had never felt that before. In the old world, no one had ever thanked him for building condos. They had paid him, yes. But they hadn't *seen* him. They hadn't recognized him. They had just given him money and moved in.

Now, he was seen.

He was not rich. He would never be rich again. But he was recognized. And in the Last Mile, that was worth more.

---

Maya stood at the back of the classroom, watching Marcus teach.

He was sixty-five now—older, greyer, his hands calloused from years of work. But his eyes were bright, his voice was warm, his enthusiasm was infectious.

"Modular housing is not just about efficiency," he was saying. "It's about dignity. It's about building homes that people want to live in—not just shelters, not just units, but places where they can put down roots, raise families, grow old."

He gestured to the model on the table—a small, three-dimensional rendering of a modular unit that could be assembled in a day, customized in a week, connected to other units to form a community.

"The old world built housing for profit. They built the cheapest thing they could get away with, and they sold it for the highest price the market would bear. They didn't care about the people who would live there. They cared about the return on investment."

He paused.

"We build housing for people. We build it to last. We build it to be beautiful. We build it because we're going to live in it ourselves."

The students nodded. They were young—most of them under thirty—and they had grown up in the Last Mile. They had never known a world where housing was a commodity, where landlords could evict you for no reason, where homelessness was a fact of life.

They had never known the old world.

And that was the point.

---

After the class, Maya walked with Marcus through the industrial park.

"You're a good teacher," she said.

"I had a good teacher," Marcus replied. "You."

Maya laughed. "I didn't teach you anything."

"You taught me that worth is not measured by what you own. You taught me that contribution matters. You taught me that a community is not a collection of individuals—it's a web of relationships."

He paused.

"You taught me that I could be seen."

Maya was silent for a moment. Then she said: "Do you ever miss it? The old life? The penthouse? The money?"

Marcus thought about it.

"Sometimes," he admitted. "Not the money—I never cared about the money. But the... the certainty. The feeling that I knew how the world worked. The feeling that I was in control."

"And now?"

"Now I don't know how the world works. I don't know what's going to happen next. I don't know if the Cataloguers will come back, or if the GCA will rise again, or if the whole thing will collapse tomorrow."

He smiled.

"But I know that I'm not alone. I know that I have a community. I know that if I fall, someone will catch me. And that's worth more than any penthouse."

---

That evening, Maya attended the quarterly assembly.

It was held in the Great Hall—a large, open space beneath the park, designed to hold thousands of people. Tonight, there were about two thousand members present—a typical turnout for a quarterly meeting.

The agenda was set by the members. Anyone could propose a topic, and the proposals were voted on at the beginning of the meeting. Tonight's topics included:

- A proposal to expand the industrial park into a new sector
- A report from the Trust board on the annual finances
- A discussion about the community's response to the refugee crisis in Europe
- A celebration of the community's thirtieth anniversary

Maya listened to the debates, the arguments, the votes. She watched the Andon Cord being used—once, on a minor proposal that was paused for review. She watched the community govern itself, not perfectly, but honestly.

At the end of the meeting, there was a celebration. Music, food, dancing. Maya stood at the edge of the crowd, watching the people—her people—enjoy themselves.

She thought about the wager.

The wager that ordinary human beings, given the tools to govern themselves continuously, will make better decisions than any sclerotic representative system, any benevolent dictator, any philosopher-king, any expert panel, any algorithm.

The wager had been won.

Not perfectly. Not completely. But enough.

---

That night, Maya sat alone in her apartment.

She thought about the dividend. She thought about the recognition. She thought about the deconstruction.

She thought about the rentiers—the landlords, the platform owners, the patent holders, the debt peddlers. They had howled when the Last Mile started. They had called it theft. They had called it communism. They had called it the end of civilization.

But they had been wrong.

The Last Mile was not the end of civilization. It was the beginning of a new one. A civilization where worth was measured by contribution, not accumulation. A civilization where the surplus belonged to everyone, not just the owners. A civilization where the wells were filled in, and the water flowed to everyone.

She took out her phone and opened the app. The weave had grown to six thousand nodes—connected by hundreds of thousands of attestations, by millions of gifts, by the simple act of seeing each other.

She scrolled through the network, looking at the names, the faces, the stories. She saw people she had known for years. She saw people she had never met. She saw the future.

She put away her phone. She looked out the window at the city.

The towers of Railtown rose in the distance. The industrial park hummed in Strathcona. The Market glowed in the darkness.

And the dance continued.

---

But as Maya sat in her apartment, watching the city she had built, she felt something she had not felt in years.

A pressure in her skull. A whisper at the edge of thought. A voice that wasn't quite her own, suggesting—gently, reasonably—that maybe she should be careful. That maybe the network wasn't safe. That maybe she should trust the old ways instead.

She shook her head, trying to clear it. The voice faded.

But it didn't disappear entirely.

And Maya realized, with a chill, that the war for the mind was not over.

It would never be over.

It would just continue—in every thought, every decision, every moment of every life.

The dance goes on.

---

*Light and Chaos,*
*Order and possibility,*
*Dancing at the edge of forever.*

*And the edge is where we live.*

*And the edge is what we are.*

*And the dance goes on.*
