
### The Oil Years

**October 1973. Sillycone. The New Mexico Desert.**

The slides had gotten beautiful.

Katherine Vance stood at the back of the presentation room, watching her team put the finishing touches on the quarterly deck. The room was state-of-the-art now—holographic projectors, immersive displays, a sound system that could make the audience feel like they were standing in a forest or swimming through a coral reef. The budget for the presentation alone was larger than the entire operating budget of the town twenty years ago.

The slides themselves were works of art. Literally. Katherine had hired designers from the advertising agencies on Madison Avenue. She had hired poets to write the narration, composers to score the music, cinematographers to film the footage. She had turned the quarterly presentation into a production—a performance, a spectacle, a piece of theatre that would make the Cataloguers feel something.

The only problem was that the numbers were lies.

Katherine knew this. She had known it for years. She had built the models herself, had written the code that generated the projections, had watched the graphs curve upward and to the right while the actual data—the real data, the data that came from satellites and sensors and ground surveys—showed the opposite. The forests were still falling. The oceans were still dying. The extinction rate was still climbing.

And the slides were still beautiful.

---

She walked to her office—a corner suite on the top floor of the administration building, with windows that looked out over the desert. The sun was setting, painting the mountains in shades of orange and purple. It was a view that had once inspired her. Now it just made her tired.

She sat down at her desk and opened the drawer.

The journal was there—her mother's journal, the one she had found in the safe after Miriam died. She had read it a hundred times. She had memorized every word, every pause, every tear stain on the yellowed pages. She had carried it with her through every promotion, every move, every year.

She opened it to the last page.

*"I was the first witness. I was the first dissenter. I was the first coward. Let my daughter be braver than I was."*

Katherine closed the journal. She put it back in the drawer. She locked the drawer.

Then she opened the safe beneath the floor.

The secret record was there—the real data, the true numbers, the projections that no one else had seen. She had been keeping it for fifteen years now, updating it every quarter, adding new evidence, building a case that would someday—somehow—be made public.

She didn't know when. She didn't know how. She just knew that she had to keep it.

*The truth is the only thing they can't take.*

She sat down at her desk and opened her laptop. The quarterly presentation was scheduled for tomorrow. She had a dozen things to do—review the slides, rehearse the narration, brief the Minister of Ecstatic Declarations on her talking points.

But she didn't do any of them.

She just sat there, staring at the screen, thinking about her mother.

---

Miriam Vance had been dead for thirteen years.

Katherine had been at her bedside when she died—had held her hand, had heard her last words, had watched the light fade from her eyes. She had grieved, had mourned, had moved on. She had built a career. She had raised a daughter. She had become the person her mother had wanted her to be.

But she had never told anyone the truth.

Not her husband, who had died in a car accident five years ago. Not her daughter, Ellie, who was only eight years old and didn't deserve to carry that weight. Not her colleagues, who thought she was a brilliant data architect and didn't know she was also a keeper of secrets.

She was alone with the truth. And she had been alone with it for so long that she had almost forgotten what it felt like to share.

She thought about the first presentation she had attended—the one in 1953, when she was twelve years old. She had sat in the back of the chamber, watching her mother stand before the Cataloguers, watching the beautiful lies flicker across the wall. She had not understood what she was seeing. She had not understood the weight of what her mother was doing.

Now she understood.

She understood that her mother had been trapped—trapped by the lie, trapped by the system, trapped by the belief that the truth would destroy everything. She understood that Miriam had made a choice—a terrible choice, a necessary choice, a choice that had cost her everything.

And she understood that she was making the same choice.

She was the senior data architect. She was the one who designed the models, who wrote the code, who made the numbers say what they needed to say. She was the one who turned catastrophe into progress, who made extinction look like resilience, who made collapse look like hope.

She was the keeper of the lie.

And she hated herself for it.

---

The presentation was held the next morning.

Katherine stood at the back of the chamber, watching the Cataloguers watch the slides. They were the same as they had always been—three tall, grey beings of luminous stillness, their dark eyes absorbing everything and giving nothing back. They did not react to the music. They did not react to the poetry. They did not react to the beautiful images of forests and oceans and coral reefs that had been extinct for decades.

They just watched.

And at the end, they hummed.

It was the same hum that Miriam had heard in 1953—the same low, throbbing vibration that meant *we are still watching*. Katherine had heard it a hundred times. She had learned to interpret it, to read its nuances, to understand what it meant.

It meant they didn't believe her.

They never believed her. They just kept watching.

---

After the presentation, Katherine returned to her office.

She sat at her desk, staring at the wall. She thought about her mother. She thought about the journal. She thought about the secret record beneath the floor.

She thought about Ellie—her daughter, who was eight years old, who would grow up in this town, who would inherit the burden of the lie. She thought about what she would tell her, when the time came. She thought about whether she would have the courage to tell her the truth.

She thought about the future.

And then she made a decision.

She opened the safe. She took out the secret record—the real data, the true numbers, the projections that no one else had seen. She made a copy. She put the copy in a small, fireproof case. She hid the case in the back of a closet, behind a box of old photographs.

Then she went back to her desk and started working on the next quarter's slides.

The beautiful lies continued.

---

That night, Katherine sat in her living room, watching the news.

The oil crisis was on every channel—the OPEC embargo, the gas lines, the economic chaos. The world was reeling. The systems that had seemed so stable, so permanent, so inevitable were suddenly revealed as fragile, contingent, temporary.

Katherine thought about the Program. She thought about the deadline. She thought about the one hundred years that were now sixty years. She thought about what would happen when the deadline arrived—when the Cataloguers finally stopped watching and started acting.

She thought about the quarantine. The containment. The management of the species.

She thought about Ellie.

Her daughter was sleeping in the next room, her breath soft and steady. She would grow up in this town. She would learn the beautiful story. She would become part of the machine.

And someday, Katherine knew, she would have to tell her the truth.

*The truth is the only thing they can't take.*

She touched her jacket—the same jacket her mother had worn in the desert, patched and faded but still intact, the small square of paper still sewn into the lining.

She made a promise to herself, the same promise her mother had made thirty years ago.

*I will remember. I will always remember. And someday, someone will find this. Someone will know.*

She stood and walked to the window. The desert stretched before her, vast and empty, the stars blazing overhead. The lights of Sillycone glowed in the distance—a small, fragile beacon in the darkness.

And somewhere, deep beneath the hardware store, the disc was humming.

*We are still watching.*

*We will continue to watch.*
