
### The Millennium Presentation

**December 1999. Sillycone. The New Mexico Desert.**

The chamber had been transformed.

Gone were the concrete walls, the copper mesh, the harsh fluorescent lights. In their place was a forest—a living, breathing forest, rendered in holographic light. Trees rose toward a ceiling that had been replaced with a sky of impossible blue. Birdsong echoed from hidden speakers. The air smelled of pine and moss and morning dew.

It was beautiful. It was immersive. It was a lie.

Katherine Vance stood at the back of the chamber, watching the technicians put the finishing touches on the presentation. She was forty-nine years old now—older, greyer, more tired. The years in Sillycone had taken their toll. She had watched the town grow from a collection of trailers to a functioning community. She had watched the workers arrive, the soldiers patrol, the scientists probe and measure and theorize. She had watched the lie take root, spreading through the town like a vine, wrapping itself around every conversation, every relationship, every life.

She had written the lie. She had given it words. She had made it beautiful.

And now she was being asked to make it even more beautiful.

---

The Minister of Ecstatic Declarations was a woman named Cassandra Vale.

She was forty-two years old, blonde, photogenic, with the kind of practiced sincerity that came from years of standing in front of cameras. She had been a lifestyle influencer before joining the Program—a minor celebrity with a million followers and a knack for making people feel good about consuming things. The Director had hired her three years ago, after the previous Minister had suffered a breakdown during a presentation and had to be quietly reassigned.

Cassandra's job was simple: she was the face of the Program. She was the one who stood in front of the Cataloguers and told them that everything was fine. She was the one who smiled and gestured at the beautiful slides and spoke the beautiful words that Katherine had written.

She was the one who made the lie feel like hope.

"I've added a spoken-word interlude," Cassandra said, appearing at Katherine's elbow. "Something to break up the technical sections. Make it more emotional, you know?"

Katherine looked at her. "What kind of spoken-word interlude?"

"It's about resilience. About hope. About the power of collective intention." Cassandra's eyes were bright with enthusiasm. "I've been workshopping it with a poet from New York. It's really powerful."

"Can I see it?"

Cassandra handed her a tablet. Katherine read the words—a series of inspirational phrases, stitched together with the kind of vague, uplifting language that sounded profound but meant nothing.

*We are the dreamers of a new dawn.*

*We are the builders of a better world.*

*We are the light that shines in the darkness.*

*We are the hope that never dies.*

Katherine read it twice. Then she handed the tablet back.

"It's perfect," she said.

Cassandra beamed. "I knew you'd like it."

---

The Millennium Presentation was scheduled for December 31, 1999.

It was the biggest production the Program had ever attempted—a full-scale immersive experience, designed to impress the Cataloguers with the depth of humanity's commitment to the mission. The chamber had been expanded, the projectors upgraded, the sound system replaced. The budget was larger than the entire operating budget of the town ten years ago.

The slides themselves were works of art. The biodiversity bar graphs now showed a dramatic upward trajectory—not because the biodiversity was actually increasing, but because Katherine had adjusted the baselines, changed the metrics, cherry-picked the data. The carbon emissions curve now showed a steep decline—not because emissions were actually declining, but because Katherine had shifted the timeline, excluded the worst years, extrapolated from the best.

The butterflies were holographic. They flapped their wings. They did not notice that the real butterflies were extinct.

---

The Cataloguers arrived at midnight.

They were the same as they had always been—three tall, grey beings of luminous stillness, their dark eyes absorbing everything and giving nothing back. They stood in a triangle around the disc, waiting.

Katherine presented. She had rehearsed the narration a hundred times. She had practiced the tone, the gestures, the pauses. She had told herself that she was not lying—she was translating, interpreting, finding a version of the truth that could be heard.

She almost believed it.

"This is our projection for 2050," she said, gesturing to the holographic display. "If current trends continue, and if our restoration programs are implemented as planned, we will have achieved a fifty percent increase in biodiversity across all major biomes."

The Cataloguers watched in silence.

"We have also developed a comprehensive model for carbon reduction," Katherine continued. "If implemented globally, these measures will stabilize atmospheric CO2 at 350 parts per million by 2075."

The Cataloguers watched in silence.

"We believe that we can succeed."

The Cataloguers watched in silence.

And then, Cassandra stepped forward.

She had changed into a flowing white dress. Her hair was loose, her face illuminated by the holographic light. She raised her hands, and the music swelled—a soaring orchestral score that had been composed for this exact moment.

*We are the dreamers of a new dawn,* she said.

The words echoed through the chamber.

*We are the builders of a better world.*

The holographic butterflies flapped their wings.

*We are the light that shines in the darkness.*

The music swelled.

*We are the hope that never dies.*

She paused, her arms outstretched, her face lifted toward the sky.

The Cataloguers watched in silence.

And then, at the end, they hummed.

---

After the presentation, Katherine returned to her office.

She sat at her desk, staring at the wall. She thought about her mother. She thought about the journal. She thought about the secret record beneath the floor.

She thought about Ellie—her daughter, who was thirty years old now, who was working as a junior architect in San Francisco, who would someday inherit the burden of the lie. She thought about what she would tell her, when the time came. She thought about whether she would have the courage to tell her the truth.

She thought about the future.

And then she made a decision.

She opened the safe. She took out the secret record—the real data, the true numbers, the projections that no one else had seen. She made a copy. She put the copy in a small, fireproof case. She hid the case in the back of a closet, behind a box of old photographs.

Then she went back to her desk and started working on the next quarter's slides.

The beautiful lies continued.

---

That night, Katherine sat in her living room, watching the news.

The world was celebrating. The new millennium was arriving—a new century, a new era, a new beginning. The news was full of hope and optimism, of grand plans and bold visions. The human race was entering a golden age.

Katherine thought about the Program. She thought about the deadline. She thought about the one hundred years that were now forty-eight years. She thought about what would happen when the deadline arrived—when the Cataloguers finally stopped watching and started acting.

She thought about the quarantine. The containment. The management of the species.

She thought about Ellie.

Her daughter was sleeping in the guest room, home for the holidays. She would inherit this town. She would inherit the lie. She would become part of the machine.

And someday, Katherine knew, she would have to tell her the truth.

*The truth is the only thing they can't take.*

She touched her jacket—the same jacket her mother had worn in the desert, patched and faded but still intact, the small square of paper still sewn into the lining.

She made a promise to herself, the same promise her mother had made fifty-two years ago.

*I will remember. I will always remember. And someday, someone will find this. Someone will know.*

She stood and walked to the window. The desert stretched before her, vast and empty, the stars blazing overhead. The lights of Sillycone glowed in the distance—a small, fragile beacon in the darkness.

And somewhere, deep beneath the hardware store, the disc was humming.

*We are still watching.*

*We will continue to watch.*

---

The next morning, Katherine received a message from the Director.

*"The presentation was excellent. The interlude was exactly what we needed. The butterflies were a nice touch."*

She read the message three times. Then she deleted it.

She walked to her office. She sat down at her desk. She opened her laptop.

And she started working on the next quarter's slides.
