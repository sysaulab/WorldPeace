
### The First Crisis

**March 2035. The Industrial Park. Eastern Strathcona.**

The fire started at 3:17 AM.

It began in the fabrication shop—a spark from a welding station, a pile of sawdust that should have been swept the night before, a moment of carelessness that would cost the community everything. The flames moved through the workshop like a living thing, consuming the 3D printers, the CNC machine, the welding station, the wood shop. By the time the fire department arrived, the roof had collapsed. By the time the fire was out, the shop was a smoking ruin.

No one was hurt. That was the first thing Maya checked when she arrived at 4:30 AM, her hair still wet from the shower she had been taking when the call came. No one was hurt. The night shift had clocked out at midnight. The building had been empty.

But the damage was catastrophic.

Five hundred thousand dollars in equipment. Two hundred thousand dollars in inventory. Six months of lost production. The Trust's insurance covered some of it—but not all. The deductible was one hundred thousand dollars. The lost revenue was incalculable.

Maya stood at the edge of the ruin, watching the firefighters pack up their equipment. The smell of smoke was everywhere—in her hair, in her clothes, in her lungs. The sky was just beginning to lighten, the mountains emerging from the darkness like waking giants.

David appeared beside her.

"The fire inspector says it started in the welding station. He's calling it an accident."

"An accident."

"For now."

Maya turned to look at him. His face was grey with ash and exhaustion.

"You don't believe that."

"Do you?"

She didn't answer. She thought about the meeting at the Terminal Club—the one she had heard about through a contact, a source she had cultivated during her years in politics. She thought about Harold Chen. She thought about the plan to discredit them, to find something, to manufacture something if they couldn't find it.

She thought about the fact that the fire had started exactly where it would do the most damage.

"I don't believe in accidents anymore," she said.

---

The community gathered at noon in the Russian Hall—a meeting space they had rented in a nearby community centre, the largest room they could find on short notice. Three hundred people crowded into the space, standing against the walls, sitting on the floor, waiting for someone to tell them what had happened.

Maya stood at the front of the room. She had washed her face, changed her clothes, tried to make herself presentable. But she was exhausted—bone-deep, soul-crushing exhausted. The election had been exhausting. The last four years had been exhausting. And now this.

"Thank you all for coming," she said. "I know you've heard about the fire. I know you're scared. I'm scared too."

She paused, looking at the faces in the crowd. Yuki, the ramen vendor, her eyes red from crying. Marcus, the former developer, his jaw tight with anger. Amara, the young organizer, her hands clenched into fists. And behind them, hundreds of others—the members who had built this community, who had believed in the vision, who had given everything they had.

"Here's what we know," Maya continued. "The fire started at approximately 3 AM in the fabrication shop. The building is a total loss. The equipment is destroyed. The inventory is gone. We've lost six months of production."

A murmur ran through the crowd. Someone shouted: "What do we do now?"

"We rebuild."

The words hung in the air.

"We rebuild," Maya repeated. "We rebuild the shop. We replace the equipment. We start again. But we do it right this time—with new safety systems, new training, new protocols. We turn this crisis into an opportunity."

"We can't afford it," someone called out. "We're barely breaking even. One more crisis like this and we're finished."

"We can't afford not to rebuild," Maya said. "The shop is the heart of this community. Without it, we're just another housing project. Without it, we're just another non-profit managing poverty. Without it, we're nothing."

She paused.

"But we can't do it alone. We need help—not just with money, but with time. With skills. With care. We need everyone who can contribute to contribute. We need to show the world that we're not just a community when things are going well. We're a community when things are falling apart."

She looked around the room.

"So I'm asking you—all of you—to help. Not because you have to. Because you want to. Because you believe in what we're building. Because you know that this is bigger than any one of us."

The room was silent for a long moment.

Then Marcus spoke.

"I'll help."

Then Yuki. Then Amara. Then another voice, and another, and another.

By the end of the meeting, two hundred members had volunteered to help rebuild the shop. They would work evenings and weekends. They would learn new skills. They would build new systems. They would turn a crisis into an opportunity.

---

The investigation took three weeks.

The fire inspector—a man named Robert Chen, no relation to Harold—was thorough and professional. He examined the wreckage, interviewed the night shift workers, studied the security footage. He concluded that the fire had been caused by a spark from a welding station igniting a pile of sawdust that had been left unswept.

It was an accident. A tragic, preventable accident.

But Maya didn't believe it.

She hired a private investigator—a former arson detective named Sarah Okonkwo. Sarah spent two weeks examining the evidence, talking to witnesses, studying the security footage. Her conclusion was different.

The fire had been set.

The security footage had been tampered with—the thirty minutes before the fire had been erased, and the timestamps had been altered to hide the gap. The welding station had been modified—a small device had been placed near the gas line, designed to spark at a specific time. And the sawdust pile had been arranged—deliberately placed where it would catch the spark.

Someone had planned this. Someone had wanted the shop to burn.

But who?

Sarah's investigation pointed to a man named Victor Reyes—a former employee who had been fired six months earlier for stealing tools. Reyes had a criminal record, a history of violence, and a grudge against the Trust. He had been seen near the industrial park the night before the fire.

But Reyes was a pawn. Someone had paid him. Someone had given him the device, the code to the security system, the schedule of the night shift.

Someone who wanted to hurt the Last Mile without being connected to it.

Maya took the evidence to the police. They investigated. They questioned Reyes. They promised to look into it.

But nothing happened.

Reyes disappeared. The evidence was inconclusive. The case went cold.

And Maya knew—with a certainty that went deeper than evidence—that Harold Chen was behind it.

She couldn't prove it. She couldn't even accuse him without risking a lawsuit, a scandal, a distraction. But she knew.

And she would not forget.

---

The shop reopened six months later.

It was better than before—safer, more efficient, more beautiful. The new welding stations had automatic shut-off systems. The sawdust collection had been upgraded to a vacuum system. The fire suppression had been expanded to cover every corner of the building.

The community had raised the money themselves—not just from the Trust, but from individual members. Yuki had donated her savings. Marcus had sold his car. Amara had organized a fundraising campaign that had brought in donations from across the city.

The reopening ceremony was held on a rainy Tuesday in September—the same kind of rain that had fallen on the day the first parcel was acquired, the same kind of rain that had fallen on the day the first dividend was distributed.

Maya stood at the entrance to the new shop, a pair of scissors in her hand.

"Six months ago, we lost everything," she said. "The fire destroyed our equipment, our inventory, our confidence. It was meant to break us. It was meant to make us give up."

She paused.

"But it didn't. We rebuilt. We came together. We proved that no fire—no crisis, no attack—can destroy what we've built."

She cut the ribbon. The crowd cheered. The shop was open.

And Maya thought about the person who had started the fire. She thought about Harold Chen, sitting in his glass tower, watching the news, learning that his plan had failed.

She thought about what she would do next.

She didn't know yet. But she knew one thing.

She would not stop. She would not be intimidated. She would not let them win.

The dance continued.

---

That night, Maya sat alone in the new shop.

The machines were silent. The lights were off. The only sound was the rain on the roof—a steady, patient drumming that seemed to wash away the fear and the anger and the exhaustion.

She thought about her parents. She thought about the grocery store on Hastings Street. She thought about the man who had died on the sidewalk, waiting twenty minutes for an ambulance that never came in time.

She thought about the fire. She thought about the person who had set it. She thought about the forces that wanted to destroy everything she had built.

And she thought about the future.

She took out her phone and opened the app. The weave had grown to five hundred members—not just in the Downtown Eastside, but across the city. They were connected by trust, by attestation, by the simple act of recognizing each other.

She scrolled through the network, looking at the names, the faces, the stories. She saw Yuki, who had rebuilt her ramen stall after the fire. She saw Marcus, who had become one of the shop's most skilled fabricators. She saw Amara, who had organized the fundraising campaign that had saved the community.

She saw all of them—the people who had believed in the vision, who had given everything they had, who had refused to give up.

And she felt something she hadn't felt in months.

Hope.

She put away her phone. She stood up. She walked to the door.

The rain had stopped. The clouds were breaking. A sliver of moon appeared between the buildings.

She stepped outside and looked at the city.

The towers of Railtown rose in the distance. The industrial park hummed in Strathcona. The weave was alive.

And the dance continued.
