
### The Handshake

**June 2031. The Golden Lotus Noodle Shop. Vancouver.**

The Golden Lotus had been on Hastings Street for forty years.

It was a small restaurant—twelve tables, a counter with six stools, a kitchen that was visible through a fogged glass partition. The walls were decorated with faded photographs of Hong Kong, a paper calendar from 1997, and a single string of red paper lanterns that had been there so long they had turned brown. The floor was linoleum, worn thin in the traffic lanes. The air smelled of ginger, garlic, and the particular sweetness of star anise.

It was, by any measure, an unremarkable place.

But it was trusted.

The family that ran it—the Lis—had been in the neighbourhood for three generations. They had served noodles to strikers and scabs, to activists and addicts, to cops and criminals. They had never taken sides. They had never called the police. They had never asked questions they didn't need answered.

They had simply been there, a constant in a neighbourhood where nothing was constant.

That was why Maya had chosen it.

---

She arrived at seven in the evening, when the dinner rush was winding down. The Lis' daughter, Mei, was working the counter—a woman in her fifties, with her mother's sharp eyes and her father's calm hands. She nodded at Maya as she entered.

"Back room," she said. "It's ready."

Maya walked through the restaurant, past the tables where a few late diners were finishing their meals. She pushed through a beaded curtain at the back and entered a small room—a storage space, really, with a table, four chairs, and a single bare bulb hanging from the ceiling.

David Okonkwo was already there.

He was sitting at the table, his tablet in front of him, a cup of tea growing cold at his elbow. He looked up as she entered.

"You're early," he said.

"So are you."

"I wanted to make sure everything was set up."

Maya sat down across from him. She reached into her bag and pulled out her phone—a cheap, prepaid model she had bought for this specific purpose. No GPS. No biometrics. No connection to her regular accounts.

"Did you tell anyone?" she asked.

"No. You?"

"No."

They sat in silence for a moment. Outside, they could hear the murmur of the restaurant—the clink of dishes, the low hum of conversation, the sizzle of the wok.

"Okay," Maya said. "Let's do this."

---

The protocol was simple.

David had designed it himself, working from the booklet, from his own experience as a union organizer, from the dozens of trust networks he had built and seen destroyed over the years. It was not a technological solution. It was a social one—a way of establishing trust between two people, without any central authority, without any bureaucracy, without any system that could be captured or controlled.

The first step was the key exchange.

Maya opened an app on her phone—a simple, open-source program that David had modified for their purposes. It generated a cryptographic key pair: a private key, which she would keep secret, and a public key, which she would share.

She pressed the button. The app hummed for a moment, then displayed a string of characters—her public key.

David did the same. His public key appeared on his screen.

They exchanged phones. Each scanned the other's QR code. The app displayed a prompt:

*"Attest to identity of David Okonkwo?"*

Maya hesitated. This was the moment. The moment of trust.

She pressed "Confirm."

Her phone signed the attestation with her private key and sent it to David's device. David's device did the same—signing an attestation of her identity and sending it back.

"Now we're connected," David said. "We've each attested to the other's identity. We're each other's first peer in the network."

Maya looked at her phone. The screen displayed a simple graph: two nodes, connected by a line. Her and David.

"That's it?" she asked.

"That's it. You've just created a peer cell. The smallest unit of the weave."

---

They spent the next hour talking about what came next.

The weave would grow slowly, carefully, one handshake at a time. Each new member would be vouched for by someone already in the network. Each handshake would be a small act of trust—a statement that said: *I know this person. I trust this person. I am willing to stake my reputation on their integrity.*

The network would not be centralized. There would be no server, no database, no headquarters. The weave would exist in the connections between people—in the attestations, the keys, the relationships that could not be seized or shut down.

"We need to be careful," David said. "The moment we start recruiting, we start attracting attention. The developers, the city, the police—they're all going to want to know what we're doing."

"Let them watch. There's nothing to see."

"There's always something to see. The question is whether they understand what they're looking at."

Maya nodded. She thought about the election, about the campaign, about the moment when she had realized that the system was not designed to help people like her—people from the DTES, people who wanted to build something new.

"We're not building a political movement," she said. "We're not building a protest. We're building infrastructure. The weave is the foundation. Everything else—the housing, the businesses, the services—will be built on top of it."

"And the Trust?"

"The Trust holds the assets. The weave holds the trust. They're separate, but they're connected. The Trust can be regulated, taxed, audited. The weave can't. It's just people, knowing each other, helping each other."

David smiled. "You've been thinking about this."

"I've been thinking about this for thirty years."

---

They recruited the first new members slowly.

The first was a woman named Yuki Tanaka, who ran a small ramen stall in the Hastings Street Market. She had been in the neighbourhood for twenty years. She knew everyone. She was trusted.

Maya approached her on a Tuesday afternoon, when the market was quiet.

"I want to tell you about something," Maya said. "Something we're building."

Yuki listened. She asked questions. She was skeptical, but curious.

"What do you need from me?" she asked.

"Just your handshake. Just your trust. And your willingness to vouch for others you trust."

Yuki was silent for a long moment. Then she nodded.

"Okay. Show me how it works."

The handshake took five minutes. Yuki became the third member of the weave.

The fourth was a man named Marcus, a former developer who had lost everything in the crash and was now working as a caretaker at the community centre. He was cynical, sharp, and utterly reliable. He joined for the same reason he did everything: because it made sense.

The fifth was a young woman named Amara, who had been homeless for three years and was now living in transitional housing. She had no resources, no power, no connections. But she had something else—a fierce intelligence, a refusal to give up, and a deep knowledge of the neighbourhood's hidden networks.

By September, the weave had grown to fifty members.

By December, it had reached one hundred and twenty.

---

The growth was not without its challenges.

Some people wanted to join for the wrong reasons—to gain access to resources, to build their own power, to spy on the network. David turned them away, gently but firmly.

"We're not a charity," he told one man who had asked for money. "We're not a political party. We're not a gang. We're a community. If you want to join, you have to be willing to give as much as you take."

Others wanted to join too quickly—to bring in dozens of people at once, to scale before the foundation was solid.

"We can't rush this," Maya told a young organizer who had proposed a mass recruitment drive. "Trust takes time. Every handshake has to be real. If we bring in people who aren't ready, we'll destroy what we've built."

The weave grew slowly. But it grew solidly.

---

On New Year's Eve, Maya stood on the roof of her apartment building, looking out at the city.

The lights of downtown glittered in the distance. The mountains rose dark against the sky. The harbour reflected the moon.

She thought about the year that had passed. The election. The defeat. The plan that wouldn't die. The first parcel. The handshake.

She thought about the people who had joined the weave—Yuki, Marcus, Amara, and all the others. They were not heroes. They were not visionaries. They were ordinary people who had decided to trust each other, to help each other, to build something together.

That was the miracle. Not the technology. Not the money. The trust.

She took out her phone and opened the app. The graph had grown—one hundred and twenty nodes, connected by hundreds of lines. It looked like a constellation. It looked like a web. It looked like the beginning of something new.

She sent a message to David.

*"Happy New Year. We did it."*

The reply came within seconds.

*"We did the first part. Now the hard part begins."*

Maya smiled. She put her phone away and looked out at the city.

The hard part was always beginning. That was the nature of building something new.

But for the first time in years, she felt something she had almost forgotten.

Hope.
