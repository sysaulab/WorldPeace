
### The Cone War

**August 2026. Sillycone. The New Mexico Desert.**

The traffic cones had become a problem.

There were too many of them. That was the first thing any visitor noticed—if there had been any visitors, which there weren't, because Sillycone was a closed city that appeared on no maps. But if there had been visitors, they would have noticed the cones. They were everywhere. Orange and white, battered and faded, arranged in patterns that seemed to have no logic and no purpose.

There were cones around potholes that had been filled years ago. Cones around manhole covers that were perfectly secure. Cones around cracks in the sidewalk that no one had ever bothered to repair. Cones marking detours that led nowhere, protecting hazards that didn't exist, guiding traffic that never came.

The cones were a joke. They had started as a joke, back in 1948, when the town was first built. The road into Sillycone was under construction—or pretending to be under construction—and someone had ordered a few dozen cones to make the fiction seem real. The cones had multiplied over the decades, like mushrooms after rain, until they had become the town's most distinctive feature.

No one knew how many there were. Estimates ranged from five hundred to five thousand. The official count, maintained by the Department of Infrastructure and Traffic Management (which consisted of one part-time employee and a clipboard), was 1,247. But the official count was almost certainly wrong.

The cones were a joke. But they were also a budget line.

---

The budget crisis began in June.

It was not a sudden crisis. It was the culmination of decades of mismanagement, declining revenues, and the simple fact that the Program had been running on the same funding model since 1947. The original appropriation—a secret line item buried in the defense budget—had been generous at the time. But it had not been adjusted for inflation. It had not been increased to meet the growing costs of the Program. It had not been supplemented by any new sources of revenue.

The Program was broke.

The Director—Harold Krupp, the great-grandson of General Thaddeus Krupp, the third generation of his family to run the lie—called a meeting of the senior staff.

"We need to make cuts," he said. "Deep cuts. Across every department. The Seed's maintenance budget is untouchable, obviously. The presentations are untouchable. But everything else is on the table."

He looked around the room. His eyes landed on the part-time employee from the Department of Infrastructure and Traffic Management.

"The cone budget," he said. "How much are we spending on cones?"

---

The Hotel Manager was outraged.

Her name was Margaret Osei—no relation to David Okonkwo, the union organizer in Vancouver, though the coincidence of their West African names had always amused her. She was seventy-two years old, had been running the Sillycone Hotel for forty years, and had strong opinions about almost everything.

She stood in the Director's office, her arms crossed, her face flushed with anger.

"Absolutely not," she said.

The Director blinked. "Excuse me?"

"The cones. You want to cut the cone budget. I'm telling you no."

The Director leaned back in his chair. He had been dealing with Margaret Osei for thirty years—had dealt with her when he was a junior administrator, had dealt with her when he was promoted, had dealt with her when he became Director. She had never been afraid of him. She had never been impressed by him. She had never treated him as anything other than a nuisance.

"The cone budget is discretionary," he said. "The cones serve no operational purpose. They are a relic of the original cover story. We don't need them anymore."

"That's where you're wrong."

"Enlighten me."

Margaret walked to the window and pointed at the street below. A few battered vehicles were parked along the curb. A handful of pedestrians—the town's remaining residents—were walking toward the general store. And everywhere, in every direction, were cones. Orange and white. Faded and battered. Arranged in patterns that seemed to have no logic and no purpose.

"The cones are the only reason we're a closed city," she said.

"That doesn't make sense."

"It makes perfect sense. Think about it. How do you keep people out of a town that doesn't appear on any map?"

"By not appearing on any map."

"That doesn't work. People find places that don't appear on maps. They stumble on them. They hear rumors. They get lost and end up somewhere they shouldn't be. You need something else. Something that makes people want to stay away."

The Director was silent.

"The cones," Margaret continued, "make us look like a construction site. A permanent construction site. A place that's always being fixed, always being improved, always just about to be finished. No one wants to visit a construction site. No one wants to live in a construction site. No one wants to ask questions about a construction site."

She turned back to the Director.

"The cones are our camouflage. They're the reason we've survived for eighty years. They're the reason no one has ever asked why a town with no name, no zip code, and no official existence has a hotel, a general store, and a hardware store. They're the reason we're still here."

She leaned forward.

"If you cut the cone budget, you might as well put up a sign that says 'Secret Government Facility—Please Inquire Within.'"

The Director stared at her.

"You're serious."

"I'm always serious. You should know that by now."

He was silent for a long moment. Then he sighed.

"Fine. The cones stay."

Margaret smiled—a small, satisfied smile.

"Thank you, Director. I knew you'd see reason."

She turned and walked out of the office.

---

The cone budget was saved.

It was not the largest line item in the Program's budget. It was not the most important. It was not the most visible. But it was the one that mattered the most—at least to Margaret Osei, who had spent forty years defending her town against the forces that wanted to expose it, dismantle it, destroy it.

The Director had learned a lesson that day. It was a lesson about bureaucracy, about inertia, about the strange ways that systems survived long past the point when they should have collapsed. It was a lesson about the cones—those ridiculous, battered, ubiquitous cones—that had become the most important security measure in the Program's history.

The cones stayed.

The town remained closed.

The lie continued.

---

Jasper Finch watched the meeting from his office.

He had not been invited—he was the senior engineer, not a senior administrator. But he had heard about it through the rumour mill that connected every department in Sillycone. He had heard about the budget crisis, the proposed cuts, the confrontation between the Director and the Hotel Manager.

He had heard about the cones.

He sat at his desk, staring at the wall. He thought about the absurdity of it all. The Program had been running for seventy-nine years. It had spent billions of dollars. It had buried the most important truth in human history. And now it was fighting about traffic cones.

It was almost funny. Almost.

But there was something else. Something that had been nagging at him for months—since the desk flip, since the Director's confession, since he had finally opened his father's data drive and read the truth.

The Program was dying.

Not because of the budget crisis. Not because of the Cataloguers. Not because of anything external. It was dying because it had lost its purpose. It had started as a desperate attempt to save humanity from a cosmic deadline. It had become a bureaucratic machine that existed to perpetuate itself. It had become a town that existed to keep people out. It had become a set of cones that existed because they had always existed.

The Program was a zombie—a dead thing that kept moving because no one had the courage to bury it.

Jasper thought about his father. Dr. Alan Finch had tried to tell the truth. He had tried to break the silence. He had failed.

But his failure had planted a seed.

The data drive was still there. The truth was still there. And someday—Jasper didn't know when, didn't know how—someone would find it.

Someone would know.

Someone would tell the truth.

He turned back to his work. The Seed hummed beneath the town, recording everything—the meeting, the confrontation, the cones, the lies. The Seed was the only thing in Sillycone that had never stopped telling the truth.

And someday, the truth would matter.
