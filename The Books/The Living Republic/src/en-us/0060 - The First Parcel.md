
### The First Parcel

**March 2031. 231 Hastings Street. Vancouver.**

The first parcel was a parking lot.

It was a small lot—just 4,000 square feet—on the corner of Hastings and Carrall, two blocks from the corner where Maya's parents had run their grocery store. It had been vacant for years, used by commuters during the day and by drug dealers at night. The asphalt was cracked and weed-choked. A single rusted sign, faded beyond legibility, leaned against a chain-link fence that had been cut and repaired so many times it looked like a scar.

It was, by any measure, an ugly piece of land.

But Maya stood at its edge on a cold March morning and saw something else entirely. She saw the seed. She saw what could grow here—housing, community space, a workshop, a kitchen. She saw the people who would live here, work here, build their lives here. She saw the first step of a long journey.

"It's smaller than I remembered," said David Okonkwo, standing beside her with a tablet in his hand and a coffee in the other.

"It's four thousand square feet. It's exactly the size we need to start."

"It's exactly the size of a parking lot for twelve cars."

"Twelve cars that won't be parked here anymore." Maya pulled her jacket tighter against the wind. "The holding company in Toronto—what did they say?"

"They said they'd take $2.1 million. Firm. No negotiation."

"That's $300,000 more than we offered."

"I know. They know we want it. They know we have the money. They know we're not going away."

Maya was silent for a moment, watching the traffic on Hastings. A bus rumbled past, its windows fogged with condensation. A man in a wheelchair navigated the potholed sidewalk. A woman pushed a shopping cart filled with cans and bottles.

"Elena said $10 million would be enough for the first six parcels and the industrial lease. If we spend $2.1 million on this one—"

"We'll have enough. Barely. But we'll have enough."

"Then let's do it."

---

The negotiation took three months.

Not because the holding company was difficult—they were, in fact, remarkably efficient for a Toronto real estate firm that had bought the lot during the boom and had been waiting for the right moment to sell. They had bought it for $800,000 in 2018. They were now selling it for $2.1 million. They were making a profit. They didn't care who bought it or what they did with it.

The delay came from somewhere else entirely.

The delay came from the city.

Maya had known that acquiring the land was only the first step. She had known that zoning, permitting, and community consultation would take time. She had not known how much time, or how many barriers would be placed in her path.

The first barrier was a routine zoning review.

The lot was zoned for commercial use—C-2, which allowed retail, office, and light industrial. It did not allow residential. To build housing, Maya would need a rezoning—a process that typically took eighteen months, required multiple public hearings, and could be blocked by a single determined opponent.

"We could apply for a variance," David suggested. "A small project with community support—"

"We're not building a small project. We're building a model. If we compromise on this first parcel, we set a precedent for every parcel that follows."

"So we apply for a rezoning."

"We apply for a rezoning."

The application was filed in April. The first public hearing was scheduled for June.

---

June came faster than Maya expected.

The hearing was held in a municipal courtroom in downtown Vancouver—a sterile, wood-paneled room with fluorescent lights and uncomfortable chairs. Maya sat at a table at the front, flanked by David and a young lawyer named Priya Sharma who had volunteered to represent the project.

The room was half-full. A few supporters. A few opponents. A handful of city staff with clipboards and laptops.

The chair of the planning committee, a councillor named Harold Chen, opened the hearing with a practiced smile.

"Ms. Chen, you have fifteen minutes to present your case."

Maya stood.

She had prepared for this moment. She had rehearsed the arguments, anticipated the objections, studied the zoning code until she could recite it in her sleep. She had a slideshow, a handout, a binder full of data.

But when she opened her mouth, she didn't talk about zoning. She didn't talk about floor-area ratios or setback requirements or parking minimums.

She talked about her parents.

"My mother and father ran a grocery store on Hastings Street," she said. "They sold vegetables and canned goods and the kind of things that people bought when they didn't have much money. They knew their customers by name. They knew their stories. They knew when someone was struggling, and they helped—not with charity, but with credit, with patience, with the kind of quiet generosity that never gets counted in the ledger."

She paused.

"One day, a man collapsed on the sidewalk outside their store. He was shaking. He was grey. My father knelt beside him and held his hand. My mother called an ambulance. It took twenty minutes to arrive. By then, the man was dead."

She looked at the committee members, one by one.

"That was thirty years ago. The neighbourhood has gotten worse. More people are dying. More people are alone. More people are falling through the cracks of a system that was never designed to catch them."

She gestured to the slideshow behind her—not the zoning diagrams, not the floor plans, but a single photograph. The parking lot. Empty. Weed-choked. Waiting.

"This is what we want to build. Not a luxury development. Not a profit-driven condo tower. A community. A place where people can live, work, and govern themselves. A place where no one has to die alone on the sidewalk because the ambulance took twenty minutes to arrive."

She paused.

"The Last Mile is not just a housing project. It's a plan for self-governance. It's a plan for resilience. It's a plan for a neighbourhood that can take care of its own."

She looked at Harold Chen.

"I know there are concerns about zoning. I know there are questions about density, about parking, about property values. I've read the reports. I've studied the data. And I'm here to tell you that the data supports us. The community supports us. And if you give us a chance, we will prove that this model works."

She sat down.

The room was silent.

And then, slowly, the applause began.

---

The rezoning was approved in September.

It was not a unanimous decision. Two councillors voted against it—Harold Chen among them—citing concerns about "community character" and "parking impacts." But the majority voted in favour, swayed by the community support, the compelling presentation, and the simple fact that no one had a better idea.

The deal closed on a rainy Tuesday in October.

Maya stood on the empty lot, feeling the weight of what they had done. They owned land. Actual land. In the heart of the DTES.

David stood beside her, holding an umbrella.

"We did it," he said.

"We did the first part. Now the hard part begins."

"You always say that."

"Because it's always true."

She looked at the lot—the cracked asphalt, the rusted sign, the chain-link fence. In her mind, she saw the building that would rise here. Six storeys. Forty units. Community space on the ground floor. A service tunnel connecting to the industrial park in Strathcona. A place where people could live with dignity, with purpose, with hope.

"Who's going to live here?" David asked.

"I don't know yet. But we're going to find them. And we're going to build something that lasts."

She paused.

"And we're going to make sure that no one dies alone on the sidewalk. Not in this neighbourhood. Not ever again."

David nodded. He didn't say anything. He didn't need to.

They stood together in the rain, looking at the empty lot, seeing the future.

---

That night, Maya received a message from Elena Voss.

*"I heard the rezoning passed. Congratulations. The next $10 million is ready when you are."*

Maya smiled. She typed a reply.

*"Not yet. Let's prove the model works first. Then we'll talk about scaling."*

She paused, then added:

*"Thank you. For believing in this. For believing in me."*

The reply came within seconds.

*"I don't believe in people. I believe in systems. And your system is sound. Keep building."*

Maya put her phone away and looked out the window. The rain had stopped. The clouds were breaking. A sliver of moon appeared between the buildings.

She thought about her parents. She thought about the man who had died on the sidewalk. She thought about the promise she had made, thirty years ago, when she had decided to leave and come back.

*I will build something.*

She had started. The seed was planted. The first parcel was hers.

And the work was just beginning.
