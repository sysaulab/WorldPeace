
### The Spillover

**September 2045. Vancouver City Hall.**

The new mayor was a woman named Sarah Kim.

She had been elected on a platform of "pragmatic progressivism"—a phrase that meant nothing and everything. She was fifty-two years old, a former city planner, a lifelong public servant who had spent her career in the trenches of municipal government. She had no ideology to speak of, no grand vision, no burning desire to transform the world. She just wanted to make the city work.

But she was smart. And she was curious. And she had been watching the Last Mile Project with growing interest for years.

---

Her first month in office, she visited the Downtown Eastside.

It was not a formal visit. There were no cameras, no entourage, no press releases. She just showed up one morning at the Market, wearing jeans and a rain jacket, and started walking.

She walked through the Market—the fresh produce stalls, the prepared food counters, the Lounge. She talked to vendors, to customers, to members. She asked questions. She listened to answers. She took notes on her phone.

She walked through the towers—The Threshold, The Commons, The Bridge. She visited apartments, community spaces, the rooftop gardens. She talked to residents—young and old, long-time members and recent arrivals. She asked them why they had come, what they had found, what they still needed.

She walked through the industrial park in Strathcona—the fabrication shop, the food processing facility, the clothing workshop. She watched workers at their stations. She talked to managers, to engineers, to apprentices. She asked about the supply chains, the revenue models, the governance structures.

And at the end of the day, she sat with Maya Chen in the Lounge.

---

"I have to admit," Sarah said, "I was skeptical."

"And now?"

"Now I think it might be the most important thing happening in this city."

She paused, stirring her coffee.

"I've spent my whole career in government. I've seen a thousand programs come and go. I've seen millions of dollars spent on poverty reduction, homelessness, addiction services. And nothing changes. The problems just get worse."

She looked at Maya.

"But this—this is different. You're not managing poverty. You're ending it. You're not treating addiction. You're preventing it. You're not building housing. You're building community."

Maya nodded. "That's the point."

"I know. And that's why I want to help."

She leaned forward.

"I want to scale it. Not just in the Downtown Eastside—across the whole city. I want to create a framework that lets other neighbourhoods do what you've done here."

Maya was silent for a moment. Then she said: "That's the goal. But it has to be organic. It has to come from the community. You can't impose it from above."

"I know. But I can remove the barriers. I can change the zoning, the funding, the regulations. I can make it easier for communities to govern themselves."

"That would help."

Sarah smiled. "Then let's do it."

---

The framework took six months to develop.

It was called the Community Governance Act—a piece of municipal legislation that would allow neighbourhoods to apply for "self-governance status." Under the act, communities that met certain criteria—a minimum membership, a demonstrated track record of successful projects, a commitment to the principles of the Last Mile—would be granted the authority to make decisions about local land use, resource allocation, and service delivery.

It was not independence. It was not sovereignty. It was something more modest—a recognition that communities could govern themselves better than the city could govern them.

The act was not universally popular.

The developers opposed it—they saw it as a threat to their profits, their power, their ability to shape the city to their will. The bureaucrats opposed it—they saw it as a threat to their authority, their budgets, their careers. The politicians opposed it—they saw it as a threat to their control, their influence, their ability to reward their friends and punish their enemies.

But Sarah Kim was not a politician who could be easily intimidated. She had spent her career in the trenches. She knew how to navigate the corridors of power. She knew how to build coalitions, how to compromise, how to get things done.

She introduced the act in June 2046. It passed by a single vote.

---

The first community to apply for self-governance status was Mount Pleasant.

It was not the most obvious choice. Mount Pleasant was not a poor neighbourhood—it was a middle-class neighbourhood, with a mix of homeowners, renters, and small businesses. It had no history of activism, no tradition of community organizing, no connection to the Last Mile.

But something had changed.

A group of residents—young families, mostly, who had moved to the neighbourhood in the past decade—had been watching the Last Mile Project with interest. They had read the proposal. They had visited the Market. They had talked to members. And they had started to wonder: *Why can't we do this here?*

They formed a group. They held meetings. They built a weave. And in September 2046, they applied for self-governance status.

Their application was approved in December.

---

Maya attended the first assembly of the Mount Pleasant community in January 2047.

It was held in a community hall—a modest building, nothing like the Great Hall in the Downtown Eastside. About two hundred people were present—young families, small business owners, retirees, students. They sat in folding chairs, looking at each other with a mixture of excitement and uncertainty.

The agenda was set by the members. The first item was a proposal to convert a vacant lot into a community garden. The second was a proposal to create a childcare cooperative. The third was a proposal to establish a credit union.

Maya listened to the debates. She watched the votes. She saw the same thing she had seen in the Downtown Eastside—people learning to govern themselves, making mistakes, correcting course, building trust.

It was not perfect. It was messy. It was slow. But it was alive.

---

After the assembly, Maya walked through the streets of Mount Pleasant.

She thought about the journey. The election she had lost. The plan that had refused to die. The investor who had believed in her. The first parcel. The first handshake. The first dividend. The fire. The tower. The Market. The Andon Cord.

She thought about the future.

The spillover was happening. The Last Mile was no longer a single community. It was a model, a template, a seed that could be planted anywhere. Mount Pleasant was the first. It would not be the last.

She took out her phone and opened the app. The weave had grown to eight thousand members—not just in the Downtown Eastside, not just in Mount Pleasant, but across the city, across the country, across the world.

She scrolled through the network, looking at the names, the faces, the stories. She saw people she had known for years. She saw people she had never met. She saw the future.

She put away her phone. She looked up at the sky.

The sun was setting. The clouds were breaking. A sliver of moon appeared between the buildings.

She thought about the dance.

Light and Chaos, order and possibility, dancing at the edge of forever.

She smiled.

The dance continued.

---

That night, Maya received a message from Sarah Kim.

*"I heard the Mount Pleasant assembly went well. Congratulations."*

Maya typed a reply.

*"It's just the beginning. There will be others."*

The reply came within seconds.

*"I know. That's what I'm counting on."*

Maya put away her phone. She looked out the window at the city—the towers of Railtown, the lights of the port, the dark mass of the mountains.

She thought about the seed. The seed she had planted fifteen years ago, when she had decided to keep going. The seed that had grown into this.

She thought about the future.

And she smiled.
