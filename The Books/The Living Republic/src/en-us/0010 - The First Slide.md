
### The First Slide

**July 1947. The Jornada del Muerto. New Mexico.**

The desert had a way of swallowing sound.

Miriam Vance noticed it the moment she stepped off the transport truck—the way her boots crunched on the alkali dust and then the noise just... vanished. No echo. No reverberation. Just the vast, indifferent silence of a land that had been empty for a thousand years.

She was thirty-two years old, a linguist by training, a translator by trade. She had been recruited six months ago for a project she wasn't allowed to name, in a location she wasn't allowed to disclose. The government had come to her university, pulled her file, and made an offer she couldn't refuse—not because she was patriotic, but because they had asked for her specifically. They needed someone who could hear language in patterns, who could find meaning in sounds that weren't words.

They needed someone who could listen.

The compound was a collection of low, featureless buildings arranged in a loose circle around a central plaza. It looked like a military base that had been designed by someone who hated architecture. No windows faced outward. No doors opened to the desert. Everything pointed inward, toward the secret at the center.

Miriam was escorted to a small room by two soldiers who didn't speak. The room had a cot, a desk, a chair, and a single bare bulb hanging from the ceiling. On the desk was a notebook—leather-bound, unlined—and a pen.

She sat down and waited.

Three hours later, the door opened.

The man who entered was tall, silver-haired, with the kind of weathered face that suggested he had spent decades outdoors and decades more pretending he hadn't. He wore a simple uniform with no insignia, no rank, no identifying marks. But there was no mistaking the authority in his bearing.

"Miss Vance," he said. "I'm General Thaddeus Krupp. Thank you for coming."

"I wasn't given a choice."

"No," Krupp agreed. "You weren't." He sat down across from her, folding his hands on the desk. "What I'm about to tell you is classified at a level that doesn't officially exist. You will not discuss it with anyone. You will not write about it. You will not remember it, except when you are in this compound, in this room, with me or one of my designated associates. Do you understand?"

"I understand that you're trying to frighten me."

Krupp's expression didn't change. "I'm not trying to frighten you. I'm trying to prepare you. What you're about to see will change everything you think you know about the universe. It will change everything you think you know about humanity. And it will change everything you think you know about yourself."

He stood. "Come with me."

---

The chamber was buried sixty feet beneath the desert floor, accessible only through a series of locked doors and descending corridors. The walls were reinforced concrete, the lighting was harsh and fluorescent, and the air tasted of ozone and something else—something metallic, almost electric.

In the center of the chamber, resting on a platform of polished stone, was a disc.

It was perhaps twelve feet in diameter, made of a metal that seemed to shift color as Miriam looked at it—now silver, now gold, now a deep, iridescent blue. It was warm to the touch, though no heat source was visible. And it was humming—a low, throbbing vibration that seemed to come from everywhere and nowhere at once.

"What is it?" Miriam whispered.

"We don't know," Krupp said. "It appeared three weeks ago. No warning. No precursor. Just... there. In the middle of the desert. We found it before anyone else could."

"Found it? It didn't... arrive?"

"If it did, we didn't see it. No craft. No trail. No radiation signature. It was simply present, as if it had always been there and we had only just noticed."

Miriam stepped closer. The hum grew louder—not in volume, but in presence, as if the sound were pressing against her skin.

"It's communicating," she said.

Krupp's eyes sharpened. "What makes you say that?"

"The pattern. It's not random. It's... structured. Like language, but not like any language I've heard." She paused, tilting her head. "It's waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"I don't know. But it's waiting."

She reached out, her fingers hovering just above the surface of the disc. The hum intensified. The air around her hand seemed to shimmer.

And then—

The light.

It was not a flash. It was not a beam. It was a presence—a woman made of light, standing before her, her form shifting and flowing like water, like fire, like something that had never been solid and never would be.

Miriam stumbled backward, her heart pounding. Krupp didn't move. He had seen this before.

The woman of light spoke.

Her voice was not sound. It was meaning—direct, unfiltered, bypassing the ears entirely and speaking directly to the mind. And yet, somehow, Miriam heard it as words. As language. As something she could understand.

*"You have been observed. You have been assessed. You have been found wanting."*

Miriam's mouth opened, but no words came.

*"Your species is destroying its habitat. You are causing a mass extinction event. You are destabilizing the climate. You are poisoning the air, the water, the soil. You are consuming resources faster than they can regenerate. You are, in the language you understand, committing suicide."*

The woman of light paused. Her form flickered—sadness, perhaps, or something like it.

*"We are the Cataloguers. We observe. We assess. We intervene when necessary. You have been given a warning. You have one hundred years to restore the biodiversity of your planet. If you fail, we will impose a solution. The solution will not be pleasant."*

Miriam found her voice. "What solution?"

*"The quarantine. The containment. The management of your species in a state that does not threaten the larger ecological balance. You will be cared for. You will be safe. You will be managed. You will not be free."*

The light began to fade.

*"You have one hundred years. Use them wisely."*

And then she was gone.

The chamber was silent. The hum had stopped. The disc was cold.

Miriam stood frozen, her mind reeling, her body trembling. She had just heard the most important message in human history. She had just been given the most important warning in human history.

And she knew—with a certainty that went deeper than thought—that no one would believe it.

---

She was escorted back to her room. She sat on the cot. She stared at the wall.

And then she picked up the notebook.

She wrote down everything. Every word. Every nuance. Every flicker of the woman of light's form. Every pause. Every silence. She wrote for hours, her hand cramping, her eyes burning, her mind refusing to stop.

When she was finished, she read what she had written.

It was accurate. It was complete. It was the truth.

And she knew—with the same certainty that had gripped her in the chamber—that the truth would be buried.

Krupp would bury it. The government would bury it. The world would bury it, because the world was not ready to hear that it had been weighed and found wanting.

But Miriam had one small advantage.

She was a linguist. She understood that words were not just meaning—they were evidence. And evidence, if preserved, could survive.

She tore a single page from the notebook—the page that contained the most essential part of the message, the part about the quarantine, the part about the management, the part about the loss of freedom.

She folded it carefully. She sewed it into the lining of her jacket. And she made a promise to herself, a promise that would echo through generations of her family.

*I will remember. I will always remember. And someday, someone will find this. Someone will know.*

She closed the notebook. She set it on the desk. And she waited for Krupp to come and take it away.

---

Krupp came the next morning. He looked tired, as if he hadn't slept. He picked up the notebook, flipped through it, nodded.

"You did well," he said. "This is exactly what we need."

"What will you do with it?"

"Study it. Analyze it. Develop a response." He paused. "The President will be briefed this afternoon. A special committee will be formed. We'll need your help—your expertise, your perspective."

"My perspective?"

"You were there. You heard it directly. That makes you valuable."

"Valuable," Miriam repeated. The word tasted like ash.

"We're going to manage this, Miss Vance. We're going to tell the public a version of the truth they can handle. We're going to create a program—a joint space initiative, something that sounds hopeful, something that sounds like a gift. We're going to buy ourselves time."

"Time for what?"

"Time to figure out how to save ourselves."

He turned to leave. Then he paused at the door.

"There's a town we're building. A closed city. A place where the program will be based. It doesn't have a name yet. We've been calling it Sillycone."

Miriam frowned. "Sillycone?"

"It's a joke. A code name. No one will take it seriously." He smiled—a thin, humorless smile. "That's the point."

He left.

Miriam sat alone in the room. She touched her jacket, feeling the small square of paper sewn into the lining.

She thought about the woman of light. She thought about the warning. She thought about the hundred years.

She thought about the lie that was already being built, the beautiful lie, the lie that would hold the world together while it fell apart.

She had helped build it. She had written the first page.

And she would spend the rest of her life trying to bury the truth—even as she carried it with her, a small, impossible seed, waiting for the moment when someone would find it and know.

---

*The truth is the only thing they can't take.*

*But they will try.*

*They will try for a hundred years.*

*And when the hundred years are up, the truth will be the only thing that remains.*
