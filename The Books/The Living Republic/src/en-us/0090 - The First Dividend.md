
### The First Dividend

**March 2033. The Golden Lotus Noodle Shop. Vancouver.**

The rain had been falling for three days.

It was the kind of rain that Vancouver specialized in—not a storm, not a downpour, just a steady, patient drizzle that soaked through coats and shoes and souls. The kind of rain that made you forget the sun had ever existed. The kind of rain that made you want to stay inside, pull the covers over your head, and wait for spring.

Maya Chen stood at the window of the Golden Lotus, watching the water stream down the glass. Behind her, the back room was crowded—thirty people squeezed around the table, sitting on the floor, leaning against the walls. The air was thick with the smell of wet wool and ginger tea.

They had gathered for the first dividend.

It had been two years since the handshake in this room. Two years since Maya and David had exchanged keys and become each other's first peer. Two years of slow, careful growth—one handshake at a time, one attestation at a time, building a network of trust that no one could seize or shut down.

The weave now had three hundred members.

The first parcel—the parking lot on Hastings and Carrall—had been developed. A six-storey building rose where the weeds had grown. Forty units of housing, all designed for single occupants or couples. The ground floor was commercial space, leased to a community-owned grocery store. A service tunnel connected the basement to the industrial park in Strathcona.

The industrial park itself was thriving. The fabrication shop had been rebuilt after the fire—better, stronger, safer. The food processing facility was supplying meals to two hundred people every day. The credit union had opened in a small office near the market, offering savings accounts and small loans and the first glimmerings of a new kind of economy.

And now, for the first time, there was a surplus.

Not much. Just enough. But enough.

---

David stood at the front of the room, a tablet in his hand.

"Alright," he said. "Let's go through the numbers."

He projected a simple chart onto the wall—a pie chart, divided into colored sections. It looked almost absurdly simple after all the months of work.

"Total revenue for the quarter: $840,000. That's from the grocery store, the fabrication shop, the food processing facility, and the credit union. Total expenses: $780,000. That includes loan repayments, operating costs, wages, and reserves."

He paused.

"Surplus: $60,000."

A murmur ran through the room. Sixty thousand dollars. It wasn't a fortune. But it was real. It was tangible. It was the first proof that the model could work—that a community-owned economy could generate more than it consumed.

"We've decided—the board and I, based on the feedback we've received—to distribute this surplus as a dividend. Equal to all members. Base money and reciprocal money, split evenly."

He tapped the tablet. The chart changed, dividing into smaller sections.

"Each member will receive $25 per week in base money. That's deposited directly to your credit union account. You can spend it anywhere, on anything. It's yours."

Another murmur. Twenty-five dollars a week wasn't much—not in a city where rent could eat a thousand dollars a month. But for the people in this room—people who had been homeless, people who had been working minimum wage jobs, people who had been surviving on disability payments and food bank hampers—it was something.

"Each member will also receive $25 per week in reciprocal money. That's credited to your account, but it's locked. You can't spend it directly. You can only activate it by transferring it to another member—as a gift, a tip, a recognition."

He paused, letting the words sink in.

"The reciprocal money is the heart of this system. It's not charity. It's not payment for services. It's recognition. When you give someone reciprocal money, you're saying: *I see you. Your contribution matters. Your existence makes my life better.*"

---

Maya watched the room as David spoke.

She saw Yuki, the ramen vendor, sitting near the front, her hands wrapped around a cup of tea. Yuki had been one of the first members—the third handshake, after Maya and David. She had built her small business with a Trust loan, had employed six people, had served hundreds of meals every day. She was not rich. But she was seen.

She saw Marcus, the former developer, leaning against the wall with his arms crossed. Marcus had been cynical at first—had looked at the reciprocal money and seen a game, a popularity contest, a new set of owners. But something had changed. He had started working in the fabrication shop, had discovered that he was good at it, had started mentoring younger members. And the reciprocal money had started flowing his way—not because he was rich, not because he was powerful, but because he had helped.

She saw Amara Osei, the young woman who had been homeless for three years, sitting on the floor with her back against the wall. Amara Osei had joined the weave six months ago. She had no resources, no power, no connections. But she had something else—a fierce intelligence, a refusal to give up, a deep knowledge of the neighbourhood's hidden networks. She had become one of the weave's most effective organizers, connecting with people that no one else could reach.

She saw all of them—the three hundred members of the weave, gathered in this small room, waiting to hear how the first dividend would be distributed.

David finished speaking. He looked around the room.

"Does anyone have questions?"

---

The questions came slowly at first.

"How do we know the reciprocal money is fair?" asked a man near the back—a former social worker named Thomas. "What if people just give it to their friends?"

"That's the point," David said. "You give it to the people you trust. The people who have helped you. The people who have made your life better. The system is designed to reward contribution, not popularity."

"But what if someone is lonely? What if they don't have friends? What if no one gives them reciprocal money?"

The room went quiet. It was a good question—the kind of question that cut to the heart of the system.

Maya stepped forward.

"That's the most important question," she said. "And the answer is: we watch. We pay attention. If someone is not receiving reciprocal money—if their flow of recognition has dried up—we notice. Not with surveillance. With care. A community health worker reaches out. A neighbour stops by. A companion visits."

She paused.

"The reciprocal money is not just a mechanism. It's a signal. It tells us who is connected and who is isolated. It tells us who is seen and who is invisible. In the old world, isolation was invisible. People died alone in their apartments, their bodies discovered weeks later. In the weave, isolation is a signal—a canary in the coal mine of community health."

Thomas nodded slowly. He seemed satisfied.

Another hand went up—a young woman named Priya, who had joined the weave three months ago.

"What about the base money? Is it really unconditional? Do we have to do anything to earn it?"

"No," Maya said. "The base money is unconditional. It's the floor beneath which no member can fall. Housing is already free. Food is already free. Healthcare is already free. The base money covers the small personal things—a phone, a book, a trip to the city. It's the guarantee that no member will ever be destitute."

"But what if someone just... doesn't work? What if they just take the money and do nothing?"

Maya smiled.

"Then they take the money and do nothing. And that's okay. Because the system is not designed to punish people for being human. It's designed to give them the freedom to contribute in their own way, at their own pace. Some people will work. Some people will create. Some people will care for others. Some people will just... be. And that's enough."

She paused.

"The old world measured worth by what you owned. The more you owned, the more you were worth. We measure worth by what you give. The more you contribute, the more you are recognized. But contribution doesn't have to be economic. It can be a kind word. A listening ear. A hand on a shoulder. The system is designed to see all of it."

---

The distribution began at noon.

Members lined up at a small table near the door, where David and two volunteers were managing the transactions. Each member presented their device—their phone, their tablet, whatever they had—and the volunteers verified their identity through the PeerID system.

The base money was deposited immediately. The reciprocal money was credited but locked.

Maya watched as the first members received their dividends.

Yuki was first. She presented her device, received her base money, and then looked at the reciprocal balance—$25, credited but not spendable. She smiled.

"Now I have to give it away," she said.

"That's the idea," David replied.

Yuki looked around the room. Her eyes landed on an elderly woman named Mrs. Lam—no relation to Maya, but a distant cousin of the Lis who ran the Golden Lotus. Mrs. Lam had been living in the neighbourhood for fifty years. She had raised three children, buried a husband, survived cancer twice. She was frail now, her hands gnarled with arthritis, her eyes clouded with cataracts.

Yuki walked over to her.

"Mrs. Lam," she said. "You helped me when I first opened my stall. You gave me your recipe for broth. You told me not to give up."

Mrs. Lam looked up, surprised.

"That was twenty years ago," she said.

"I know. But I never forgot." Yuki opened her device. "I want to give you this. It's not much. But it's my way of saying thank you."

She transferred $10 in reciprocal money to Mrs. Lam's account.

Mrs. Lam stared at her device. The money was now spendable—activated by the gift.

"I don't know what to say," she whispered.

"You don't have to say anything," Yuki said. "Just know that you are seen."

---

The reciprocal transactions flowed through the room like a current.

Marcus gave $5 to a young man named Jamal who had helped him fix a broken machine in the fabrication shop.

Amara Osei gave $3 to a child who had helped her carry groceries up the stairs.

Thomas gave $10 to a woman named Maria who had cooked a meal that reminded him of his grandmother's kitchen.

Each transfer was small. Each transfer was a recognition. Each transfer said: *I see you. You matter. Your existence makes my life better.*

Maya watched the flow of gifts with something close to wonder.

She had spent thirty years fighting systems—fighting the developers, the politicians, the bureaucrats, the market. She had lost elections, been dismissed, been told that her ideas were naive, impossible, dangerous.

But this—this small room, these three hundred people, this flow of recognition—was something else entirely. It was not a fight. It was a building. It was the seed of a new kind of economy, a new kind of community, a new kind of world.

She felt a hand on her shoulder. She turned.

David stood beside her, his tablet in his hand.

"How does it feel?" he asked.

"Strange," Maya said. "I've been working toward this for so long. And now that it's here, I don't know what to feel."

"You feel hope," David said. "That's what you feel."

Maya nodded slowly.

"Yes," she said. "I think that's it."

---

That night, Maya walked home through the rain.

She thought about the dividend. She thought about the reciprocal money. She thought about the flow of recognition that had moved through the room like a living thing.

She thought about her mother and father, who had run a grocery store on Hastings Street, who had helped their neighbours without expecting anything in return. They had practiced the grammar without knowing it. They had lived the short loop, asked the open question, protected the fragile.

She thought about the man who had died on the sidewalk outside their store. She thought about the ambulance that had taken twenty minutes to arrive. She thought about the promise she had made to herself, thirty years ago, when she had decided to leave and come back.

*I will build something.*

She had built something. It was small. It was fragile. It was only the beginning.

But it was real.

She stopped at the corner of Hastings and Carrall. The parking lot was gone. In its place, a six-storey building rose—The Threshold, they called it, because it was the first threshold, the first step, the first proof that another world was possible.

Lights glowed in the windows. People were living there—people who had been homeless, people who had been hopeless, people who had been forgotten. They were living there now. They were seen.

Maya stood in the rain and looked at the building.

She thought about the truth. The truth that Miriam Vance had written down in 1947, sewn into her jacket, carried across generations. The truth that was still buried, still hidden, still waiting.

She thought about the one hundred years. She thought about the deadline. She thought about the quarantine, the containment, the management of the species.

She thought about the seed she had planted—not just the buildings, not just the economy, but the grammar. The short loop. The open question. The fragile shield.

If the world was going to end—if the Cataloguers were going to impose their solution—at least there would be this. At least there would be a few hundred people who had learned to govern themselves. A few hundred people who had learned to see each other. A few hundred people who had learned to build something that could survive.

She pulled her jacket tighter and walked home.

The rain continued to fall. The lights of The Threshold glowed in the darkness. And somewhere, deep beneath the desert, the disc was humming.

*We are still watching.*

*We will continue to watch.*
