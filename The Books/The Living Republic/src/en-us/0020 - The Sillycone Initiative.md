
### The Sillycone Initiative

**August 1947. The Pentagon. Washington, D.C.**

The room had no windows.

Miriam had been in Washington for three days, waiting. She had been given a room at a hotel near the Capitol, told to stay available, told not to leave without permission. She had spent the time reading—novels, mostly, anything to keep her mind off the desert, off the woman of light, off the words that still echoed in her skull.

*You have been found wanting.*

She had failed to sleep. Every time she closed her eyes, she saw the disc. The shimmer. The presence.

And every time she woke, she touched her jacket, feeling the small square of paper sewn into the lining.

*The truth is the only thing they can't take.*

The door opened. A soldier—young, expressionless—gestured for her to follow. She walked through a maze of corridors, each one identical, each one lit by the same harsh fluorescent light. Finally, they stopped at a door marked only with a number: 217.

The soldier opened it. Miriam stepped inside.

The room was small, dominated by a rectangular table. Around it sat four people.

General Krupp, of course. He nodded at her as she entered, his expression unreadable.

Dr. Howard Lin, a physicist she had met briefly in New Mexico. He was a small man, precise, with the kind of nervous energy that came from too much coffee and too little sleep. He had studied the disc's energy signature, or tried to. He had told Miriam, once, that the disc was "not a machine, not in any sense we understand."

Miriam Vance. Herself.

And a man in a civilian suit.

He was the one who drew her attention. He was neither tall nor short, neither handsome nor ugly. His face was the kind of face that was impossible to remember after you looked away. He wore a gray suit, a white shirt, a dark tie. He looked like an accountant, or a mid-level bureaucrat, or a man who had spent his entire life cultivating the art of being overlooked.

He did not give his name.

"Please, sit," Krupp said.

Miriam sat. The man in the civilian suit watched her with pale, almost colorless eyes.

"You've been briefed on the situation," Krupp said. "You know what we're dealing with. You know what's at stake."

"I know what I heard," Miriam said. "I don't know what's at stake."

"You know enough." Krupp leaned forward. "We've spent the past three weeks analyzing the message. We've consulted with linguists, physicists, biologists, theologians. We've run simulations, projections, scenarios. The conclusion is unanimous: we cannot tell the public the truth."

Miriam felt a cold knot form in her stomach. "Why not?"

"Because the truth would cause panic. Mass panic. Riots. Economic collapse. The kind of chaos that would make the Great Depression look like a mild recession." Krupp's voice was flat, matter-of-fact. "And panic would make it harder to do what needs to be done."

"Which is what?"

"Save the planet."

Dr. Lin spoke for the first time. "The message was clear. We have one hundred years to restore biodiversity. That means reforestation, ocean cleanup, carbon reduction, habitat restoration. It means transforming our entire civilization—our economy, our agriculture, our energy systems. It means doing things that will be deeply unpopular with powerful interests."

"We can't do that in a panic," Krupp said. "We need stability. We need public cooperation. We need a narrative that gives people hope instead of fear."

"A lie," Miriam said.

"A story," the man in the civilian suit said. His voice was soft, pleasant, utterly forgettable. "A story that helps people understand what needs to be done without terrifying them into paralysis."

Miriam looked at him. "Who are you?"

"I'm someone who helps stories get told in the right way."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer you're going to get."

Krupp cleared his throat. "The point is this: we're going to create a program. A joint space initiative—something that sounds hopeful, something that sounds like a gift. We're going to tell the public that the visitors were peaceful, that they offered us technology, that they want to help humanity's development."

"That's not what they said."

"No. But it's what people will believe. And it's what will give us the political cover to do what needs to be done."

Miriam was silent for a long moment. She thought about the woman of light. She thought about the warning. She thought about the quarantine, the containment, the management of the species.

And she thought about the lie—the beautiful, necessary, terrible lie—that was already being built around her.

"What do you want from me?" she asked.

"We want you to help us tell the story," Krupp said. "You're a linguist. You understand how language shapes perception. We need someone who can craft the message—who can make the truth sound like a gift instead of a threat."

"You want me to lie."

"I want you to translate. To find the words that will help people hear what they need to hear without breaking."

"That's the same thing."

The man in the civilian suit smiled. It was not a warm smile. "Miss Vance, you're a linguist. You know that translation is never neutral. Every act of translation is an act of interpretation. Every interpretation is an act of creation. You're not being asked to lie. You're being asked to create a version of the truth that people can live with."

Miriam stared at him. She wanted to argue. She wanted to refuse. She wanted to stand up, walk out, and tell the world what she had heard.

But she knew—with a certainty that was already settling into her bones—that no one would believe her. And even if they did, they would panic. And panic would make everything worse.

She thought about the hundred years. She thought about the children who would be born, the generations who would live and die under the shadow of the deadline. She thought about the woman of light, waiting, watching, judging.

*You have been found wanting.*

"What do you want me to do?" she asked.

Krupp slid a folder across the table. "We're building a town. A closed city. A place where the program will be based. We need a name. Something that sounds hopeful, forward-looking, inspiring. And we need a story—a narrative that explains what we're doing and why."

Miriam opened the folder. Inside were photographs—aerial shots of a construction site in the desert, not far from where she had seen the disc. Plans. Diagrams. Budget projections.

"There's no name yet," Krupp said. "We've been calling it Sillycone, as a joke. But we need something better."

Miriam looked at the photographs. The town was being built in a remote valley, surrounded by mountains. It had a single road leading in, a single road leading out. It had a hotel, a general store, a hardware store, a school. It had houses for the workers, barracks for the soldiers, laboratories for the scientists.

It looked like a military base. It looked like a prison.

It looked like a place where secrets would be kept.

"Sillycone," Miriam said slowly. "You should keep the name."

Krupp frowned. "It's a joke."

"That's why it works. No one will take it seriously. No one will ask questions. It will be a place that appears on no maps, a town that no one has heard of, a secret that hides in plain sight."

The man in the civilian suit nodded slowly. "She's right. The best disguises are the ones that don't look like disguises."

Krupp considered this. Then he shrugged. "Fine. Sillycone it is. Now—the narrative. What do we tell people?"

Miriam thought for a moment. She thought about the woman of light. She thought about the warning. She thought about the hundred years.

"We tell them the visitors were peaceful," she said. "We tell them they offered us a gift—a technology, a source of clean energy, something that will help us heal the planet. We tell them that the program is a partnership, a collaboration between humanity and the visitors. We tell them that we are working together to build a better world."

"And the deadline?"

"We don't mention it. Not directly. We talk about a 'long-term commitment,' a 'generational project,' a 'shared vision for the future.' We make it sound like an opportunity, not a sentence."

"And the truth?"

"The truth stays here. In this room. In Sillycone. In the archives."

The man in the civilian suit leaned forward. "And you, Miss Vance? Will you keep the truth?"

Miriam touched her jacket, feeling the small square of paper sewn into the lining.

"I'll keep it," she said. "I'll keep it safe."

---

The town was built in eighteen months.

It was, by any measure, a remarkable achievement. A complete community, with housing, infrastructure, laboratories, and a state-of-the-art communications system, constructed in the middle of nowhere. The workers were told they were building a research facility. The soldiers were told they were guarding a secret. The scientists were told they were studying the future.

No one was told the truth.

Miriam moved into a small house on the edge of town—a modest structure, two bedrooms, a kitchen, a living room with a fireplace. It had been built for her, designed to her specifications. She had asked for bookshelves. She had asked for a desk. She had asked for a place where she could think.

She spent the first year writing.

She wrote the official narrative—the story that would be told to the public, to the press, to the world. She wrote speeches for the President, press releases for the program, brochures for the visitors' center that would never receive visitors. She wrote the beautiful lie.

And she wrote the truth.

She kept a journal—a private journal, hidden in a locked drawer, written in a cipher she had invented herself. She wrote down everything: the message, the warning, the quarantine, the hundred years. She wrote down her doubts, her fears, her guilt. She wrote down the names of the people who had been in the room, the decisions that had been made, the lies that had been told.

She wrote for the future. For the children who would inherit this world. For the generations who would live under the shadow of the deadline.

She wrote because she believed—despite everything—that the truth would matter.

---

In 1948, the first official presentation was delivered to the President.

Miriam was not present. She had written the speech, but she had not been invited to deliver it. She listened on the radio, in her small house in Sillycone, as the President told the nation about the "peaceful visitors" and the "gift of clean energy" and the "partnership for a better world."

She heard the applause. She heard the cheering. She heard the hope in the President's voice.

And she felt nothing.

She looked at the journal on her desk. She looked at the small square of paper sewn into her jacket.

She thought about the woman of light. She thought about the warning.

*You have one hundred years.*

The clock was ticking.

---

That night, she walked to the edge of town. The desert stretched before her, vast and empty, the stars blazing overhead. She stood alone, in the darkness, and she listened.

She listened for the hum. She listened for the voice. She listened for the presence of the woman of light.

But there was nothing.

Only the wind. Only the silence. Only the vast, indifferent emptiness of a land that had been empty for a thousand years.

She thought about the lie. She thought about the truth. She thought about the distance between them—the distance that would grow, year by year, as the beautiful story buried the terrible truth.

She thought about her daughter. Katherine, she would name her. Katherine, who would be born in this town, who would grow up under the shadow of the deadline, who would inherit the burden of the lie.

She thought about what she would tell her. She thought about what she could tell her.

*The truth is the only thing they can't take.*

But what if the truth was buried so deep that no one could find it? What if the lie became so beautiful, so comforting, so necessary, that the truth seemed like a nightmare?

She touched her jacket, feeling the small square of paper.

*I will remember. I will always remember.*

She turned and walked back to town. The lights of Sillycone glowed in the distance—a small, fragile beacon in the vast, indifferent dark.

And the clock kept ticking.
