
### The Cataloguers Change

**December 2038. Sillycone. The New Mexico Desert.**

The presentation was scheduled for noon.

It had been scheduled for noon for eighty-five years—ever since the first formal review in 1953, when Miriam Vance had stood before the Cataloguers and told the first beautiful lie. The time had never changed. The format had never changed. The chamber had never changed, not really, not in any way that mattered.

But something was different this year.

Katherine Vance had noticed it first—a subtle shift in the pattern of the Cataloguers' attendance. They had always been punctual, arriving at exactly noon, standing in their triangle around the disc, watching in perfect stillness until the presentation was complete. They had never missed a review. They had never been late. They had never shown any variation in their behavior at all.

Until now.

It had started three years ago, in 2035. The Cataloguers had arrived five minutes late. It was a small thing—so small that no one else had noticed. But Katherine had noticed. She had been watching them for fifty years. She had learned to read their stillness, to interpret their silence, to understand the things they didn't say.

Five minutes late was not nothing.

The next year, they were ten minutes late. The year after that, twenty. This year—2038—they had not arrived at all.

---

The chamber was empty.

Katherine stood at the podium, her notes in her hand, the holographic projectors humming behind her. The slides were ready. The narration was rehearsed. The butterflies were programmed to flap their wings at exactly the right moment.

But there was no one to present to.

She waited. Noon passed. Twelve-fifteen. Twelve-thirty. The chamber remained empty—the disc silent, the platform bare, the triangle of light where the Cataloguers always stood now dark and cold.

"They're not coming," said the Director—Harold Krupp, who had been standing at the back of the chamber, watching the door.

"They're late," Katherine said.

"They're not late. They're not coming."

"We don't know that."

"We know." He walked to the podium and stood beside her. "They've given up. They're not coming anymore."

Katherine stared at the empty chamber. She thought about the first presentation—the one in 1953, when her mother had stood before the Cataloguers and told the first beautiful lie. She thought about the eighty-five years of presentations that had followed. She thought about the silence, the stillness, the unblinking eyes.

And she thought about the hum.

*We are still watching.*

*We will continue to watch.*

"They're still watching," she said. "They're just not watching us."

---

The Engineer—Jasper Finch—was in his office when the news arrived.

He had not attended the presentation. He never attended the presentations. He was the senior tech lead, not a presenter, not an administrator, not anyone who mattered in the grand choreography of the lie. He maintained the Seed. He kept the systems running. He did his job.

But he had heard the whispers. He had heard the rumours that had been circulating through the town for weeks—that the Cataloguers were losing interest, that they were not coming, that the Program was finally being abandoned.

He had not believed it. Not at first. The Cataloguers were the foundation of everything—the reason for the town, the reason for the lie, the reason for the presentations. Without them, what was the point?

But now, as he sat at his desk, staring at the data from the Seed, he realized that the rumours were true.

The Cataloguers were not coming.

They had found a better audience.

---

The Seed had been recording everything—every word, every decision, every lie—for ninety-one years. It had recorded the presentations, the meetings, the confrontations. It had recorded the cone wars, the budget crises, the desk flips. It had recorded the truth, buried beneath layers of silence and secrets.

But it had also recorded something else.

It had recorded a signal—a faint, distant signal, coming from outside the town. It had been there for years, growing stronger, more insistent. Jasper had noticed it a decade ago but had dismissed it as noise—a glitch, an anomaly, a problem with the sensors.

Now he realized what it was.

It was the Cataloguers. They were communicating with someone else—someone outside Sillycone, someone outside the Program. They had found a new audience. They had found people who were not lying to them.

Jasper thought about the truth—the data drive, the secret record, the evidence his father had collected. He thought about the people who might be listening—the network, the weave, the second track. He thought about the world outside this town, the world that had been kept in the dark for ninety-one years.

The Cataloguers had found a better audience.

And Jasper knew, with a certainty that went deeper than evidence, that the end was coming.

---

That night, Katherine Vance sat alone in her office.

She had not told anyone about her suspicions—not the Director, not the senior staff, not the presenters. She had kept the secret, as she had kept so many secrets, as her mother had kept so many secrets before her.

But she knew.

The Cataloguers were not coming back. They had found a better audience. They had found people who were not lying to them.

She thought about her mother. She thought about the journal—the small, leather-bound book that Miriam Vance had kept, hidden in the safe, waiting for the moment when the truth would matter.

She thought about Ellie—her daughter, who was forty years old now, who was living in San Francisco, who had no idea what her mother had done. She thought about what she would tell her, when the time came.

*The truth is the only thing they can't take.*

She stood and walked to the window. The desert stretched before her, vast and empty, the stars blazing overhead. The lights of Sillycone glowed in the distance—a small, fragile beacon in the darkness.

And somewhere, deep beneath the hardware store, the disc was silent.

The hum had stopped.

The Cataloguers were gone.
