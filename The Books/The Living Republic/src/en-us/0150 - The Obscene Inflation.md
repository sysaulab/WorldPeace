
### The Obscene Inflation

**June 1985. Sillycone. The New Mexico Desert.**

The Director's office was on the top floor of the administration building, a corner suite with windows that looked out over the desert. The sun was setting, painting the mountains in shades of orange and purple. It was a view that had inspired poets for centuries. Now it belonged to him.

Director Harold Krupp—the great-grandson of General Thaddeus Krupp, the man who had buried the truth in 1947—sat behind a desk of polished mahogany, reviewing the latest slide deck. He was fifty-eight years old, silver-haired, with the kind of smooth, practiced smile that had closed a thousand deals. He had been running the Program for twenty years. He had never questioned the mission. He had never doubted the necessity of the lie.

But he was beginning to worry about the numbers.

"The biodiversity bar graphs are too flat," he said, tapping the screen. "Can we add more arrows?"

Katherine Vance stood across from him, her tablet in her hand. She was thirty-five now—older, thinner, more tired. The years in Sillycone had aged her in ways she hadn't expected. She had watched the town grow from a collection of trailers to a functioning community. She had watched the workers arrive, the soldiers patrol, the scientists probe and measure and theorize. She had watched the lie take root, spreading through the town like a vine, wrapping itself around every conversation, every relationship, every life.

She had written the lie. She had given it words. She had made it beautiful.

And now she was being asked to make it even more beautiful.

"We've added all the arrows we can," she said. "The data—"

"The data is irrelevant." Krupp leaned back in his chair. "The direction is what matters. We need to show them we're serious. We need to show them we're making progress."

"We are making progress. The reforestation programs in Brazil, the ocean cleanup in the Pacific, the—"

"The what? The carbon capture? The renewable energy? The sustainable agriculture?" Krupp laughed—a thin, humorless sound. "Those are pilot programs. Small-scale experiments. They don't add up to anything."

"They add up to something. They add up to hope."

"Hope is not a metric." Krupp leaned forward, his eyes hard. "The Cataloguers don't care about hope. They care about results. They care about biodiversity. They care about the extinction rate. And the extinction rate is still climbing."

Katherine was silent. She knew the numbers. She knew the real numbers—the ones she kept in the secret record beneath the floor, the ones that showed the truth. The forests were still falling. The oceans were still dying. The extinction rate was ten thousand times higher than the natural background rate.

And the slides were still beautiful.

---

The quarterly presentation was held the next morning.

Katherine stood at the back of the chamber, watching her team put the finishing touches on the deck. The room was state-of-the-art now—holographic projectors, immersive displays, a sound system that could make the audience feel like they were standing in a forest or swimming through a coral reef.

The slides themselves were works of art. The biodiversity bar graphs now showed a steep upward trajectory—not because the biodiversity was actually increasing, but because Katherine had adjusted the baselines, changed the metrics, cherry-picked the data. The carbon emissions curve now showed a dramatic decline—not because emissions were actually declining, but because Katherine had shifted the timeline, excluded the worst years, extrapolated from the best.

The arrows were everywhere. Green arrows. Blue arrows. Arrows pointing up and to the right, toward a future that would never arrive.

The Cataloguers arrived at ten o'clock.

They were the same as they had always been—three tall, grey beings of luminous stillness, their dark eyes absorbing everything and giving nothing back. They stood in a triangle around the disc, waiting.

Katherine presented. She had rehearsed the narration a hundred times. She had practiced the tone, the gestures, the pauses. She had told herself that she was not lying—she was translating, interpreting, finding a version of the truth that could be heard.

She almost believed it.

"This is our projection for 2050," she said, gesturing to the holographic display. "If current trends continue, and if our restoration programs are implemented as planned, we will have achieved a forty percent increase in biodiversity across all major biomes."

The Cataloguers watched in silence.

"We have also developed a comprehensive model for carbon reduction," Katherine continued. "If implemented globally, these measures will stabilize atmospheric CO2 at 350 parts per million by 2075."

The Cataloguers watched in silence.

"We believe that we can succeed."

The Cataloguers watched in silence.

And then, at the end, they hummed.

It was the same hum that Miriam Vance had heard in 1953—the same low, throbbing vibration that meant *we are still watching*. Katherine had heard it a hundred times. She had learned to interpret it, to read its nuances, to understand what it meant.

It meant they didn't believe her.

They never believed her. They just kept watching.

---

After the presentation, Katherine returned to her office.

She sat at her desk, staring at the wall. She thought about her mother. She thought about the journal. She thought about the secret record beneath the floor.

She thought about Ellie—her daughter, who was twenty years old now, who was studying at university in California, who would someday inherit the burden of the lie. She thought about what she would tell her, when the time came. She thought about whether she would have the courage to tell her the truth.

She thought about the future.

And then she made a decision.

She opened the safe. She took out the secret record—the real data, the true numbers, the projections that no one else had seen. She made a copy. She put the copy in a small, fireproof case. She hid the case in the back of a closet, behind a box of old photographs.

Then she went back to her desk and started working on the next quarter's slides.

The beautiful lies continued.

---

That night, Katherine sat in her living room, watching the news.

The news was full of the usual disasters—the famines, the floods, the wars. The world was reeling. The systems that had seemed so stable, so permanent, so inevitable were suddenly revealed as fragile, contingent, temporary.

Katherine thought about the Program. She thought about the deadline. She thought about the one hundred years that were now fifty-eight years. She thought about what would happen when the deadline arrived—when the Cataloguers finally stopped watching and started acting.

She thought about the quarantine. The containment. The management of the species.

She thought about Ellie.

Her daughter was sleeping in the guest room, home for the summer. She would inherit this town. She would inherit the lie. She would become part of the machine.

And someday, Katherine knew, she would have to tell her the truth.

*The truth is the only thing they can't take.*

She touched her jacket—the same jacket her mother had worn in the desert, patched and faded but still intact, the small square of paper still sewn into the lining.

She made a promise to herself, the same promise her mother had made thirty-eight years ago.

*I will remember. I will always remember. And someday, someone will find this. Someone will know.*

She stood and walked to the window. The desert stretched before her, vast and empty, the stars blazing overhead. The lights of Sillycone glowed in the distance—a small, fragile beacon in the darkness.

And somewhere, deep beneath the hardware store, the disc was humming.

*We are still watching.*

*We will continue to watch.*

---

The next morning, Katherine received a message from the Director.

*"The presentation was excellent. The arrows were exactly what we needed. Keep up the good work."*

She read the message three times. Then she deleted it.

She walked to her office. She sat down at her desk. She opened her laptop.

And she started working on the next quarter's slides.
